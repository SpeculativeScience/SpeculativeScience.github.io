# Sensibility

> This webpage is a direct translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Anthropology and Psychology, Vol. II* (1840) 

The immediate sensory impression of the object on the subject is sensation, it is the subject's immediate self-determination. The subject finds a change that has taken place within itself, the cause of which, however, it does not yet relate to an object outside of it. In sensation, subject and object are immediately one and sensory representation cannot yet distinguish the two from one another. Since the subject is spirit, it must, as a universal, distinguish itself from sensory individuality. For, alongside this individual content of sensory sensation, it has other, and yet another, and so on. Through this the subject remains what is simple and separates itself from this plurality of content. 

Since sensation is directed toward the object separated from it, that is, toward its ultimate cause, it becomes representation. This is nothing other than consciousness of sensation, which thereby becomes the representation of an object. Spirit relates itself as something general, something unchanging in itself to the infinite multiplicity of its states and representations. It separates the multitude of sensations from itself in space and time, so that this diversity does not obscure its unity.

The opposition of subject and object that appeared in representation is abolished again. As spirit transforms the individual, its finite representations are united into a single entity, and they no longer retain the character of sensuous individuality they had in their spatial interaction. The diversity of perceptions that are combined form the object of representation.

## Sensation

Since spirit has freed itself from the bonds of nature, the natural object has become something foreign and external to it. The process of spirit consists in abolishing the apparent dissimilarity of the two related sides of subject and object, and in tearing down the separation that has placed itself between them. In themselves, they have not ceased to exist in substantial unity, the task is only to raise this unconscious harmony to a conscious one, to make the unity objective through cognition. The real glimmer of this consciousness that wrestles itself out of unconsciousness is sensation. It is, fundamentally, the ego's conscious experience of its immediate reality. 

Sensation is the integration of nature by spirit within itself. Since the ego has achieved mastery over its natural being, out of its infinite multiplicity it remains at the same time simple and independent. Every determination that the ego has it has the power to recognizes as its own and is therefore not limited or constrained by it, but knows how to assert its freedom against its nature. The dissimilarity of subject and object, with which sensation seemed to start with, is thus abolished in sensation, and in it the sensation and the sensation are one. Before the sensation actually occurs, however, the two are initially separated from one another. Spirit is only receptive to the sensation, i.e. the possibility exists within it to receive this or that impression. Likewise, that which produces the impression, or that which is perceptible, is included in the sensation. In terms of potentiality, both sides are each a different existence. The one is the sensing subject, the other the sensitive object. In actual sensation, however, they merge into one; for in their innermost essence they are identical. Sensation is thus the higher third in which the sensing subject and the sensed object find oneness. It is nothing other than this: that the sensible object is seen as the proper determination of the sensing subject.

What sensation senses are not the sense organs, but the impressions produced by them. The ego, in reducing the infinite multiplicity of natural impressions to the incomprehensibility of its ideality, does not yet act directly upon nature, so its idealization is therefore not yet complete. Instead, the natural element that the ego has within itself, its body, mediates this activity. The sense organ, as the activity of the embodied subject, is thus the medius terminus that organizes the multiplicit of sensations into a unity that is comprehended by the subject. This is the totality of external sensation, as the realization of the concept of sensation, from which the more precise nature and manner of the union of subject and object will now become clear. The infinity of the natural modifications of things must, in order to be able to be raised to the level of reason, be reduced to a certain multiplicity. To this end, the natural properties of things must be brought under categories, and to these then corresponds the quintuplicity of the external senses. Yet even the external sense must relate the multiplicity of its forces to a unity. 

The external sensations of the senses are organized within the self, as a general feeling, that is present throughout the entire body and constitute an internal sensation through which we feel our general well-being and discontent, our vitality. This general internal feeling is not on the same level as the five senses, but can be regarded as the unified force resulting from the interplay of sensations and the self. Since the sense of vitality offers no external sensation, but only the self-representation of our corporeality, it is closest to the internal sensation. Through the vital sense all the senses are related to one another as a totality, and this relationship is necessary so that the multiplicity of external sensation can be reduced to the simplicity of the sense-perceiver. This relationship is already partly contained in the fact that the senses support one another. Furthermore, we link the sensations of the most diverse senses into a whole and perceive them as a single object. We involuntarily associate the sweetness of sugar with its whiteness, and the sour taste of lemon with its yellow color, so that we can even infer from one sensation another. Indeed, the senses support one another in such a way that one, as it were, takes the place of the other when the latter has been destroyed. Thus, the blind feel more finely and the deaf see more sharply, so the faculty of sensation that withdraws from one sense grows toward the others. However, one sense can never completely replace another, because each has its own particular domain of objects. One might therefore want to have examples of blind people who can indicate the color of bodies by touching their surfaces, but this is only due to chemical nature and manner in which the color of a body modifies its surface that becomes the subject of their sensation.

## Intuition

The two sides linked in sensation, the external and internal, have already separated themselves from each other. The sensing subject recognizes themselves in the presence of what is sensationed, which is the immediate self-consciousness of themselves, and with this we have what we call "I". The ideality of natural things in which sensation was immediate is now mediated by the spirit and exists for it as intuition. We can say that this apprehension of self-consciousness, the separation of the pure I from the manifold content of what is sensationed, is a standpoint unique to the human being. Animals do not develop this capacity for intuition throughout their entire lives, they fully inhabit the world sensations and cannot distinguish themselves from what they sense. For them, the connection between the sensing and the sensed is true in the complete sense, they are bound to the rhythms of nature and blindly follows its hints and commands. Humans, by contrast, have the power to recognize what is not physically present because they can recognize that their sensations as their own and distinguish themselves from the content of their experience. Infants have not yet developed this power to distinguish their sensations from themselves. For them, the “I” is initially only a special sensation that appears alongside the others, to the extent that they recognize themselves by their first name. It is only when the child refers to themselves as "I", signifying the infinite progress it has made in its development, does the external world appear as distinguished from it. The characteristic of self-consciousness is its unchangeability in the midst of all sensation.

Because consciousness exists only through its differentiation from the content of sensation, it follows that it cannot be the consciousness of the external world. The one generates the other, and so both mutually imply each other. I am only self-consciousness if and only if I distinguish myself from the object of my sensation, but in order to distinguish myself, the object of sensation must actually be present and I must be conscious of it as a being. In contrast to self-consciousness, consciousness of the external world is in constant change. I am always aware of a different state and pass from one external sensation to another, but since these external sensations also become internal sensations, the self that exists in reality is not a simply, self-identical entity, but contains the infinite multiplicity of its mental states. If I recognize that I have mental states that are constantly changing, then this would be empirical self-consciousness. But if I recognize myself as a pure I, the consciousness of my identity irrespectively of my history, then this would be pure self-consciousness. Pure self-consciousness, however, can only be recognized once it has been abstracted from empirical self-consciousness, which is the higher truth, since in it consciousness and self-consciousness coexist as an inseparable unity. I only have consciousness in that I am the one who has something to be conscious of. Thus, consciousness requires self-awareness, and conversely, we have already seen above that this is only possible through the consciousness of the object. 

The activity of self-consciously recognizing a specific content that exists in consciousness is intuition. It involves directing the attention of the mind toward this specific content of sensation and its isolation from the infinite multiplicity of my sensations. I must focus on "this" individual content of external or internal sensation if I want to fulfill my ego with it. This direction toward it, apart from its goal, is attention. Intuition is complete in attention, and this is the condition of everything that follows, it is the first impulse of thinking, whose power begins here. Attentiveness is what first makes a sensation my own. There could be a thousand different stimuli in my surroundings and I would have no idea if any of them even existed if I don't notice them. In this sense, attentiveness is beginning of education. Countless objects pass by the uneducated person without them paying attention. It is a distinctive characteristic of the educated person to distinguishes between individual sensory impressions more because they give far greater attention to them. The educated person examines objects from all sides down to the last detail, whereas the uneducated person remains insistent that their first impression is complete truth and that there is nothing more to learn. 

The retention of a sensation, which is precisely the essence of attention, requires effort and mental stamina because the multiplicity of intuitions appear and disappear one after another. The intuitions do not wait until the mind grants them an audience, but they constantly strives to gain an audience of their own accord. They distance the mind in this way from the fixation on a single perception and seek to counterbalance this lack of attention. If any sensation succeeds in gaining superiority over the attention, the state of attentiveness vanishes and self-consciousness becomes unfocused. Attention is self-consciousness that takes control and directs consciousness, but in distraction the relationship is inverted and consciousness is the one in control of self-consciousness.

However, the overcoming of attention by distraction does not immediately clear the field of awareness. For since the reduction of the infinite multiplicity of consciousness to the unity of transcendental apperception is the necessary goal of the activity of the spirit, attention seeks to rise again from this and compose itself again. In composing itself, self-consciousness withdraws from the multiplicity of consciousness into its simplicity. Since the awareness of multiplicity is then just as necessary, the mind must make a comparison with it and allow a specific multiplicity to hold its attention. This demonstrates its attentiveness by recognizing the unity in multiplicity, not limiting itself to one intuition. This heightened attention summarizes several simple intuitions into a complex intuition. This act of seeing, as the apprehension of such a multiplicity, without thereby in the slightest disruption of the unity of consciousness, is now representation.

## Representation

The intuition to which I am attentive ceases to be the one that I am attentive to. Because of the infinite multiplicity of intuitions that intrude upon me, I can only grasp one after the other. By allowing them to appear before my consciousness as distinct and separate instances, I cannot, despite their separation, resist connecting them. In fact, they are already always connected with each other in the one and the same moment that are present in my mind. The complete representation of the object completes the objectification of intuitions. To the extent that the intuitions of my concentrated attention are visible to my mind as diverse, they are also visible to one another. The plurality of intuitions that I have are thus subsumed under the form of superposition, which is the pure intuition of space or spatial intuition. Because of their externality, the intuitions find themselves in space, coexisting peacefully next to the other and without disturbing one another. Yet space is also, because of its uninterrupted continuity, the condition and means of their unification, which is accomplished precisely through the inner unity of my consciousness. But also, the intuitions do not lose the character of externality. For in order to be intuited internally in time, I must separate one from the other and gradually pass through them one after the other before I can combine them into a single unity. Just as space is the form of external intuition, so time is the form of internal intuition. Through both forms, however, I bring the multiplicity of my intuitions under a common unity and give them existence. For only when I know that my intuitions are a juxtaposition of individual heres and a succession of individual moments do I distinguish them from myself as the universal I. Only through the intuition of this multiplicity, which I order in space and time and subsequently separate from myself, do I see things as they are in reality. The complete intuition can therefore rightly be called representation. 

Precisely through this separation of things by the mind, which has been accomplished in representation and through which I grasp the object in its truth, a stronger unity of both sides has simultaneously come to light than the simple immediate unity of the perceiving and the perceived could offer. As the object of sensation has been brought under the forms of space and time in representation, its content has also changed. Instead of a simple here and a simple now, I now consider a complex of here and now. It is not only the mind that summarizes the manifold content of representation into one unity, but because the mind perceives the object as it truly is, this summarizing of the many sensations into one unity also applies to the object itself. Thus, one here is not merely beside another here, in every actual here, several spatial heres be distinguished, an above, below, left, and right. The indivisible here of representation is the interweaving of all the subtle qualities and characteristics. Not only is the ego the universal that subsumes this multiplicity under its unity, but the object is equally this universal, or the concept that encompasses a multiplicity of determinations. The object of representation is therefore, in its pure content, the thing with its many characteristics, of which it is the bearer. Previously, the complete representation had merely a subjective existence in the perceiver, now acquires objective existence and both sides correspond perfectly to one another. Not only in the ego, but also in the object, the impressions of various senses are held in a common unity just as the ego pertains to the pure self. Being is an abstraction if I detach it from the respective fulfillment that is brought to it through representation, and just as abstract is the general concept of the object if I separate it from its characteristics. The thing conceived in this would be no more than the empty concept of thingness, the thing that exists as a mere container for properties. The human mind therefore does not impose this unity on sensations, thereby making them into an object, rather the unity is already inherent to thing intuited.

The summarization of the many here into one unity is, however, again only a one-sided view. For just as space encompasses a certain multiplicity of qualities as one thing, so it also separates certain other properties from this first connected thing and connects them to another thing, after all, space is both unifying and separating. By contemplating things in space, we are always caught up in the alternation of its different qualities. Because space is the absolute continuity of the separate, I again have to unite the things I just separated. It is only through ever-increasing synthesis that I arrive at the most complex view in the representation of everything as a world. Only through this single act is the world of representation complete. The thing, with its properties, was indeed already a generality, and in order to perceive it, I do not remain with the sensory particulars. But the universality of the thing exists by being composed of other things, so the thing is therefore only a finite or physical universal alongside other sensible universals. 

With the representation of the world, we have now arrived at a more comprehensive universality, which is no longer limited by other sensory particulars. But since space is infinitely continuous, this synthesis never completes itself since traversing an infinite distance in space would take an infinite duration of time. The objective unity that underlies the world is therefore never given in the external appearance of things, but only in their inner self. Nevertheless, the world is the only true entity, compared to which its individual parts are only vanishing reflections. The objective unity of the world is thus a beyond of sensible things, a supersensible world, into which the interposed veil of these sensible things prevents us from penetrating. In fact, however, the ability to summarize all particulars into a universality is the prerogative of the idealizing spirit, and by exercising this prerogative against the superposition of nature, it transfers its unity to it and makes the whole world into just such an objective unity as it considers the thing as the summary of its characteristics. The interior of the world, which appeared to the spirit as the distant beyond of a supersensible world, is rather its own interior — the intelligible world within itself, which by piercing the veil of sensible appearance, rediscovers only itself, or its own representations as the inner being of things. The transcendental unity of apperception, which subsumes all sensations into the unity of the self, likewise unites all things into the concept of the world. This objective unity, however, is in fact only one that underlies sensible things, and representation fails to completely capture the concept of the world in all its reality. The connection of all things to the concept of the world, however, is only an inner image of the spirit, to which no sensible object corresponds.
