# Prehistory

> This webpage is a direct translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Philosophy of History, Vol. I* (1879) 

The first element of the classification of the prehistoric world is the form of prehistoric time, that is, the natural and logical elements, conceived as the abstract categories under which it must be subsumed. These constitute the conditions that constitute the *when* of the prehistoric world, i.e. its past. The second aspect is the absolute present of the prehistoric world, its enduring content or actuality, conceived of as the *what* of the prehistoric world. However, since this content is determinate and so cannot last an eternal duration, prehistoric time must, in the third instance, undergo its dissolution. To its conceptual genesis and its factual existence, its passing away—its transience and eventual end—must necessarily be appended, for this marks the point at which prehistory becomes actual history.

## The Conditions of Prehistory

The conditions of the prehistoric world are the conceptual forms under which it exists, and having characterized these as the natural and logical categories under which it must be subsumed, the initial inquiry arises: what are these forms? As *prehistoric* time (*Urzeit*), it must, after all, exist *within* time, so our first task is to focus our attention upon the *when* of prehistoric time. Yet, inherent to its very etymology, the concept of prehistoric time harbors a contradiction: that while everything that belongs to time is something that has *come into being*, that which is *prehistoric* cannot itself have come into being. We cannot conceal from ourselves the first contradiction that prehistoric time—qua eternity—should not, strictly speaking, exist *within* time, or that it must at least exist *at all times*, given that everything temporal is transient by its very nature. Time, as the one natural element of the prehistoric world, necessarily entails its other natural element: Space. For everything that exists in time must also exist in space. The second question, therefore, concerns the *where* of the prehistoric world. If space is, in the first instance, the immovable standing in contrast to the mutability of time, the latter form of sensibility nevertheless imparts its own mobility to the former. The second contradiction lies in the fact that even the immobile space of the prehistoric world is swept up into this mutability. But this very changeability, manifesting as succession and coexistence, stands in contradiction to the third condition of prehistoric time: the logical category of unity, which directly follows from the concept of originality. If the eternity of the human race implies its immutability and self-identity, then it must also constitute a unified entity, for the multiplicity and diversity of distinctions inevitably entails a cycle of arising and passing away. This third contradiction exists between the logical category of unity and the multiplicity of the natural elements of space and time. Thus, the third question asks *how* the prehistoric world exists within this *when* and *where*, how it maintains its immutability amidst its own transformation, concerning its essence as it manifests in time and space. Only through resolving these three contradictions, which will reveal to us the very possibility of the prehistoric world, can we pave the way toward grasping its actual content.
