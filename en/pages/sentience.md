# Sentience

Note: This webpage is a direct translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Anthropology and Psychology Vol. I: The Doctrine of the Soul* (1840) 

## The World Soul

### Ensouled Nature

If nature and spirit are different expressions and images of the idea, the question arises as to what this difference consists of. To this end, we must first define the concept of nature, then the concept of spirit, and finally the relationship between the two.

In nature, the idea exists in the form of externality and is alienated from itself. Originally, the idea is the absolute unity of all differences and the complete supersession of their independence. Before God, nothing exists, everything is simply negated and superceded by Him. Since nature is the exact opposite of this, creation must be regarded simultaneously as a fall from and negation of the divine. Nature in its external existence is not divine, for its being is perpetually alienated from God even if its internal essence as idea is not. From the fact that nature's external appearance and internal essence do not correspond to one another, it follows that it is not only external to God and the human spirit, but also external to itself. Nature is external to its externality in that each of its forms are external to the other, which has no other than that the forms of nature exist in space. Space is the most abstract form of externality itself, where one place is next to another place, yet each place is indifferent to every other place and remains completely alien to its other. All the forms of nature have this relationship to one another, even to its own kind, e.g. a rose does not care whether another rose blooms next to it.

Nature gives itself over to infinite progress, always pushes beyond itself, and has its milestone of creation nowhere. This endlessness appears in the constant propagation of light as it travels throughout the universe without limit and is also simultaneously limited by finitude because there is never a standstill in its propagation. Nature even shows the same infinite progression in matter. Matter can be divided infinitely, for in every tiny part of it there are always mutually exclusive points, and we can never arrive at the absolutely indivisible, no matter how many times we repeat the division. Likewise, matter by definition offers resistance, and no part of it penetrates another. Of course, the many forms of nature do not indefinitely fall apart into fragments, they are held together by reason. But the forms of nature are themselves not conscious of the bond of reason that unites them. The planets perpetually move in their orbits and convey the appearance of independence. This independence, however, is not their free activity, they are bound to one another by the bonds of necessity. For necessity is precisely the relationship of several things that, notwithstanding their apparent independence, are nevertheless connected in their innermost being. The order of natural objects is the divine reason immanent within them, and since it constitutes only their inner being, forges a necessary bond between those related, by which they are not yet truly one.

However, even with this appearance of independence, nature does not stop there, but progresses to the existing unity of its separateness. Thus, in the organism, as highest form of nature, each part is no longer independent, but is member of the whole and can no longer exist separately from it. To cut off any living part from the living whole not only kills the part, but can potentially kill the whole. The plant retains the independence of its parts in that a branch severed from the trunk and stuck in the ground will grow into a new plant. This advantage is a merit of the plant's purpose as a plant, but not as an organism. There are even animals that can regenerate some of their parts, e.g. crustaceans regenerate their claws, a polyp cut in two remains the same animal as two complete polyps, etc. But the higher we ascend in the genera of organisms, the more interconnected and necessary their organs are to the unity of their organism. In the organism, only the appearance of externality remains. Each organ is the cause and effect of every other organ, to which each organ preserves itself through the activity other organs. But it is precisely by preserving itself that each organ contributes to the preservation of all the other organs since each is simultaneously an end and a means to each other. The animal's existence is derived from this external necessity, but not entirely. Animals still require an environment and other organisms to sustain themselves. They are moved by instinctual, physical demands such as thirst or hunger and must perpetually satisfy them as they arise to sustain themselves as organisms. Even here, the organism is still dependent on an external nature and is related to something external to it. The animal is necessarily determined by the nature surrounding it and cannot distinguish itself from the sensations by which it is determined. The animal cannot abstract themselves from their sensations, but is entirely immersed in each individual one. However, the animal soul still possesses a unified agency, but only one that is the active form that animates the matter of its body.

If we call the externality of differences towards one another reality, then the character of spirit, as the dependence of these differences, is ideality. Man immediately projects his sensations onto the external world, thereby overcoming whatever heterogeneous aspects they still possessed in relation to him, graspsing them as moments and aspects of his own consciousness. Spirit thus has the power to transform all real differences into ideal differences of its own universality. It is not nature whose reality displays the highest richness and the highest unfolding of the idea, but spirit, by reducing everything that had an independent existence in nature to a moment in its ideality, is conscious at every point of an infinite richness and of power over all that exists, which unfolds within it to its fullest extent. We carry all of nature around with us in our consciousness. All my thoughts about the world are idealizations of it and are borne only by my ego, and this infinitely manifold content does not detract from the simplicity of my ego in the slightest. Thus, it is only in the mind that we find the true simplicity for which we sought in vain in nature, a simplicity that is not baren and abstract, but is comprehensive and complete. For spirit, every prior moment that has passed is preserved in it, whereas nature, because it eternally strives beyond itself, it likewise has no memory of whatever came before it, whether in space or in time.

Since spirit is the negation and idealization of indifferent externality, it cannot be doubted whose negation it is. For it is only nature that presents itself in the mode of externality. Spirit is always active and can never be passive, for activity is its very substance. Every activity, however, presupposes an object upon which it acts, and this object of spirit is precisely nature. Spirit is initially bound to nature, it has a nature within itself and it always returns its externality to unity in that, as the living principle of the organism, it is the power to determine its members according to this unity. But spirit is not only the active form of its body, which continually absorbs and regenerates its materiality, it absorbs all of nature in the same way, grounds it in the ideal, and elevates it to thought. This is the continuous redemption of nature by spirit. Nature is always transformed by spirit into something different, something refined, it becomes ever more suited to the purposes of spirit.

The idealization of nature is not merely a one-sided act of spirit, it is also nature's own doing. For spirit, the ideality of all determinations is fully and eternally realized. Enclosed within nature's shell and forming its innermost core, the idea is nature's driving force that moves it to overcome its fragmented externality. The outer body of nature, because it does not correspond to its soul, cannot endure the tension of its inner spirit which seeks to break these bonds. The shell of nature must break for the essence within it to be revealed. Nature is therefore a succession of forms, with each higher form expressing this idealization in a more perfect way than the lower. The extraordinary nature of things increasingly withdraws into its center, or viewed from the opposite perspective, the entire striving of nature is directed toward bringing this center out of the dark and into the light.

In driving itself towards ideality, nature is also active, bringing spirit and nature in interaction with each other. Through its self-idealization, nature produces spirit by its own logic. However, the self-idealization of nature only goes as far as life, i.e. to the ideality of the material, which is still tied to materiality. This means nothing other than that, that life can only appear in a sensuous, individual form in nature. The living individual is determined by being distinguished from other living individuals and are externally related to them. This externality is the permanent condition that nature cannot absolutely overcome.

The living individual is no longer, as in the case of nourishment, something heterogeneous to the organic being, it is an individual of the same species. Nevertheless, the individuality of life remains the ultimate limit of nature. The idealization of individuality is universality, but nature does not, however, arrive at the consciousness of this universality, or even at the mere existence of the universal qua universal. In nature, the living individual indeed rises to the consciousness of its species. In the species process, the individuals negate their externality from one another and themselves perceive the species. The species reproduces itself, but only in the individual and as the infinite series of individuals. The product of the species process is not adequate to the species and does not correspond to its concept. That the living individual does not correspond to its concept manifests itself in illness and death. In death, the finitude of the living individual is proven. Death is the victory of the species over the individual, making it highest ideality to which nature brings itself. In doing so, however, nature sacrifices itself. Within nature, this ideality is only the abstract negation of death. In nature, all the moments of its development appear as external existences, so this abstract negation of the individual also appears as an independent moment. However, this negation has a positive side in that the conclusion of life in death closes the circle of necessity and reveals the complete nature of the species. The negation of death is the negation of the externality of nature, but since these differences are simultaneously negated and preserved in the universal, we have the species as it actually exists. The species is not beyond the individual, rather, it is in the individual and the individual is its activity. Spirit, as the negation and preservation of all differences of nature, is cognition. 

According to Schubert, natural cognition, as reproductive process of the species, is the source of physical death, whereas spiritual cognition is the resurrection of the individual in the death of its naturalness. Only man knows himself as a universal and is thereby consciousness of his essence, whereas the animal is only conscious of itself as an individual. In nature, everything exists as an individual, whereas in spirit, everything as a universal. In thought, the universal essence, the essence that is common to all, is included. Spirit, as pure thinking, appears to transcend and escape nature, but this would be a one-sided and distorted position of spirit toward nature. Instead, it is nature that is the product of the activity of spirit. The spirit supercedes all externality, but it is also the very activity of superceding it. It is not a quiescent, dead universal that is beyond all being, but a developing, living universal that is being itself. God creates the externality of nature in order to create the ideality of spirit as a return from it. Finite spirit is the way to absolute idealization and has absolute spirit as its goal. Nature is only a product, a creation that is destined to be superseded. However, finite spirit remains only an "ought" to infinity, not a true sublation of all that is natural. The transformation of nature by spirit is supposed to cease when nature perfectly corresponds to the purposes of spirit. Since absolute spirit realizes itself eternally, it always anticipates and requires its conditions, nature and finite spirit. Thus, there will never be a time when neither nature nor finite spirit exists. God's love is eternally realized in creation, so the future must also exist as a necessary illusion that is eternally swallowed up in order to be eternally regenerated.

### Embodied Spirit

The ideality of spirit lies the fact that its internal distinctions are nonexistent. For it, static being, separateness, and extension are all suspended. This does not make it an empty abstraction, but the infinite presence and fullness of its determinations. Nevertheless, spirit is not bound by its internal distinctions, they are absolutely dynamic in the unity of spirit and exists only to the extent that it sees and allows them to be valid. The distinctions of spirit, as posited, are a being which is likewise suspended; and this is freedom, all being in spirit is only a being-sect. The I, says Fichte, is only what it makes of itself, what it posits within itself, and before its self-positing it is nothing. The essence of spirit is the freedom to produce its own distinctions by itself, to bring them to consciousness, and to distinguish themselves from them. Negative freedom consists in being able to withdraw from everything, even from its own life. But man also possesses the positive freedom to pursue any one of his multiple distinctions at any moment in time. Nature is always determined by externality because externality is what constitutes it. But spirit can, at any moment, abolish what determines it and establish another determination in its place. The freedom of spirit, however, is not another static property that clings and adheres to the soul as a dead substance, but that the substance of spirit is freedom itself. Its substance is its activity and the content of this activity is self-determination. The person is the series of their deeds, the spirit is the totality of its thoughts. 

Since freedom is not a static determination of spirit, it consists in constantly determining itself and developing new distinctions from within. Because of its infinity and universality, spirit never stops realizing itself, whereas the endlessness of nature is just the variation and repetition of what has already existed. The simplicity of spirit is thus an inexhaustible source of dexterity. Initially, however, the simplicity of spirit eludes ordinary consciousness as if it were some riddle or wonder. The entire activity of spirit consists only in explaining this wonder, in solving this riddle, in revealing its inner nature. The activity of revealing itself is the second determination that constitutes the essence of spirit. The revealing and the revealed are identical in spirit, it exists only as a manifestation of itself. What it reveals is not any one determination or content, but the infinite fullness of determinations that characterize its own reality or efficacy. Nature, on the other hand, because it always represents only one of its differences, its remains unknown to itself. Nature is knowable only for us because the subjective idea of our cognition recognizes and reconstructs the objective idea that is hidden within it.

Spirit defines itself by making distinctions and revealing them in existence. This first revelation of spirit is nature. That God created nature has no other meaning that eternal intelligence creates the object that it cognizes by alienating itself from it in order to recognizes itself in it. Nature is, as Schelling expressed it, a petrified intelligence. Yet even in this estrangement from itself, eternal intelligence is still active, the revelation in it never rests since the harmony of nature is the eternal manifestation of the spirit in it.

The second revelation of spirit is that in which this separation is related to the ideality of spirit. Since nature is crystalized intelligence, spirit as consciousness cannot dwell within it. As nature congeals and crystalizes, spirit withdraws from it. The self-alienation of spirit to the externality of nature has imposed this determination upon itself, and it appears in this twofold opposition between indifferent reality and self-existing ideality. This is the standpoint of the finite spirit. This spirit, situated in relation to nature, is bound to it and simultaneously struggles with it to free itself from it. The finite spirit has been thrust into nature, like Ishmael into the desert, to find the source of eternal life through its own search and knock at the veins of the rocks to make its water spring forth beneath the stone. In the first revelation, intelligence was not yet given its due, for it did not yet come to consciousness. Now, the consciousness of spirit awakens from the slumber of nature. However, because with this first awakening of intelligence the dominion of the spirit is not yet complete, the world has certainly become an object of consciousness and yet the world remains an independent from the spirit. This is, at this stage, the limit of the spirit, which makes it precisely finite or human spirit.

This limitation of spirit is overcome in the third and final revelation. God manifests himself in this otherness of himself, is absolutely with himself in it. This absolute intelligence encompasses finite intelligences and their independence from one another rests solely on the form of externality that they, as natural beings, possess. But just as the infinite spirit the externality of nature vanishes in relation to it, so too does the externality of finite spirits vanish. The finite spirits do not remain independent from the infinite spirit, but are absorbed into unity with it. In this, the individual recognizes their highest goal and eternal blessedness. Everything that is generated by God is, or becomes in this way, part of God himself, and this is what Christianity understands by the eternal generation of the Son. God's love for the world is his incarnation through which he gives himself to it and is thus the All in all things. The source of the divine life is being itself, and this eruption of the infinite spirit from all the veins of nature and man is the eternal life of God.

## The Human Soul

The moments of spirit are not merely scattered across nature, nor merely opposed to one another, but have returned to identity and totality in the individual. The individual is the truly universal, the totality of determinations that are more or less developed in each individual. Thus we find the ideality of differences only in the human soul, and since this ideality is characteristic of the spirit, it is only the individual that is spirit. The different relationship of these determinations to each other constitutes what is the character of the individual, which vary contingently as much as . While the individual empirically belongs to a certain historical people and lineage, each individual has a conceptually unique character that distinguishes them from all other individuals.

After everything that has been stated so far, however, the unique character of the individual shouldn't be overemphasized. Education cultivates the rationality that is common to the species of unique individuals as a whole. Man becomes what he thinks, and thinking is the universal quality by which he becomes identical with all other people and becomes an active participant in education of the species. The main thing is that each person should develop the ideal of humanity, which is inherent in everyone, as perfectly as possible, but he can only do this if he sheds the particularity of character attached to him by nature and makes himself into the image of humanity. Public education ignores such particularities by demanding a general course of action for developing the general in the particular. In home life, the particular nature of the child are nurtured by the parent, but in school life, the general nature prevails. Teachers must judge their students according to same standard, and instruction should bring to consciousness precisely what is common to all, even if it must also connect with the particularities that each individual has. We can now classify these particularities ourselves according to how they are expressed. The development of the general in the individual must correspond to a completely general capacity of the individual to absorb or transform objectivity. Firstly, we can call this the individual's predisposition for the thing, the degree of which contingently varies among different individuals. Secondly, from this general attitude towards the external world, the individual progresses to the particularity of this attitude that constitutes their disposition or temperament towards it. Finally, we must consider the absolute individuation of this relationship, the idiosyncrasies, in which spirit rises to the very pinnacle of individualization.

### Predisposition

A predisposition that is common to individuals in general is an ability, whereas a predisposition that is uncommon to individuals in general is a talent. But if a talent exists in an individual with such great intensity to the point that it is entirely sui generis or unique, then it is genius.

#### Ability

Everyone has abilities, they are the potential that education makes actual and without which it could not realize. Therefore, if one asks the question whether it is predisposition or education that makes the person what they are, one must, on the one hand, answer this question according to the form of predisposition, and on the other hand, guard against the one-sidedness of each extreme. Some maintain that everything is possible through education, others maintain that everything is given by nature, but the truth lies in the mean. That education can do everything is refuted by the fact that the same education produces different results in different people. Education has no basis for instruction if it does not account for the predisposition and take it as its starting point. Yet, even the predisposition, no matter how great, is powerless without the mediation and guidance of education. The possibility of an ability remains completely useless if it is not developed through education. Man has an infinite number of abilities, and each individual is capable of learning a broad spectrum of different things. However, the fact that the individual is a finite being limited in space and time makes it impossible for any one person to develop infinity, and it is likewise unrealistic to demand this from education in the first place. Every person has the capacity to take up any kind of skill, yet they can only take up one and must let the capacity for another skill lie dormant in them. Ability in itself is therefore powerless without the interaction of education and predisposition working on each other for development of the individual. The result produced by this interaction can be ability, talent or genius, the difference of which depends on the diversity of one or another of these factors. The greater the potential of one factor, the less it will need the help of another to complete the development of the individual. General ability has the least potential of the three, requiring that education take a much more involved and active role in nurturing the growth of the predisposition. Genius already possesses, by its predisposition alone, the greatest amount of potential that it requires education the least. Even in circumstances where the individual has received very little education and lacks the means to educate themselves, their genius forges its own path by transforming all obstacles in its way as the means to educate themselves whenever possible. Talent stands in the middle out of the three and needs education even less than ability, yet it is developed significantly by favorable education and brought to a higher level of maturity than in the opposite case.

#### Talent

As far as the material difference between ability and talent is concerned, a talent is a more specific ability. Talent applies to everything that also applies to ability, but talent is a higher degree of this ability. It applies to the arts, mechanical skills, and all empirical knowledge, just as it applies to the highest activities of the human mind. But while talent has the same object as ability in general, for each individual person, a talent is less comprehensive than an ability. While every person possesses a multitude of abilities, talent in an individual always exists as a specific ability in relation to a specific object. For this reason, talent is not inventive because its true limit is the knowledge of its time, it only executes and improves upon what already exists.

#### Genius

In all the different aspects that have been highlighted regarding talent, genius forms a certain contrast to it. Firstly, genius quantitatively differs not only from general ability but also from talent, in that it is the highest degree of predisposition. However, when genius is compared with talent in particular, it does not appear to have the same scope as talent. For while talent relates to every kind of ability, genius is only narrowly attributed to the great inventions of the human mind, such as arts, science, and political life. Secondly, as far as comprehensiveness goes, genius is actually more comprehensive than talent. For even if one does not maintain that genius extends to all spheres of human activity, it is nevertheless certain that genius is responsible having created the activity itself, whereas talent only narrowly and perpetually improves on one activity in depth. The highest degree of this scope in genius is distinguished by the polymath, who seeks mastery in whatever subject they devote their attention and efforts to. Thirdly, genius differs from talent in that it is productive and original. What genius creates is something that has never existed before, it establishes a completely new standpoint that takes science and humanity another step further. Talent is merely the servant of genius, executing in detail what genius has created the general outline for.

### Temperament (Disposition)

Nature determines not only the general possibility of the individual to grasp and transform the external world, elevating themselves to the level of spirit, but also the particular way in which a person acts in relationship to the external world. The disposition of the individual towards the world is their temperament, which moreso expresses practical relationship of spirit to things, whereas predisposition moreso express its theoretical relationship. The individual is not, however, merely an expression of their temperament without any further characteristics. Otherwise, the individual would not be individual at all, but only a specimen of its genus or species, as animals increasingly sink to such forms at lower levels of organization. But man takes individuality seriously, they have a very particular mixture of natural determinations that constitutes their peculiar character, the distinguishing feature of the individual that is carved into their naturalness by the spirit. Character itself, however, has various stages, as it increasingly detaches itself from naturalness in order to become intellectual character.

### Idiosyncrasies

The individual who returns to the universal in this way must also, however, descend to the most particular particularity. The individual is the most unique being for whom particularity rises to the most eminent point reality in the idiosyncrasies that characterize them. These are the entirely peculiar dispositions of the individual, which are usually passed on and preserved through many generations, whether by natural or spiritual means. Among such particularities are the early development of children who are called prodigies, e.g. children who can perform most difficult mental arithmetic in the shortest possible time from a very early age. Some child prodigies plateau as quickly as they have blossomed, especially if it was only an artificial upbringing that made them shoot up quickly like greenhouse plants, but did not have the support or stability to sustain this growth later in life. By abandoning the momentum they once had, their genius is no longer actual because they have resigned themselves from utilizing it. Other child prodigies, however, are and remain actual geniuses if their predisposition is so powerful that it precedes any educational assistance. Finally, idiosyncrasies that manifest themselves as inexplicable phobias or philias also belong here. There are people who have such a natural attraction / aversion to certain things that the mere sight of them causes arousal / distress. Since the individual is the totality of the qualities of the mind, it is no longer bound to a single natural quality. The individual is thus in itself the totality, but it must also see and realize what it is in itself. The individual must therefore rise above the fixed determination of being, yet at the same time, they are still determined by nature at this level. Thus, it can only express its totality here in the way that it passes from one quality to another, and therefore represents a natural succession of changing qualities, all of which appear as developmental moments of the one underlying subject.

## Aging

Because the individual cannot be an complete expression of the species at every single moment, it must gradually achieve this through a succession of states, after which it relinquishes the existence that gave rise to its very incompleteness. Therefore, the individual has a physical beginning and end, a birth and a death, within which its natural and intellectual differences must unfold. There is also a sense in which the stages of life embody the temperaments, in that the disposition at each stage of life shapes the systems of the organism as a whole. In childhood, as in sanguine individuals, the sensory system predominates. Children are born with heads much larger in proportion to their bodies, and this part of the body grows much less in relation to the rest. The forms are at their roundest, the bones at their most tender, with warmth and moisture present to the highest degree. The growth of the physical body is most visibly evident in children. In youth, on the other hand, the irritable system develops abstractly on its own, with blood and muscles increasing their activity. Diseases of this system occur particularly at this age, like diseases of the sensory system in children. In adulthood, finally, the reproductive system predominates, and diseases of this system become more common. In old age, the energy of the particular functions dulls, and they are more or less sunk into indifferent insensitivity. The elder represents the other extreme to the child. Their bones have developed to its greatest hardness, the soft fleshy aspects have disappeared, everything becomes sharp and angular. Coldness and dryness are the most prominent features of the old man, and since, as Aristotle observes, life lies in vitality and warmth, the elder is closest to death because they lack both. 

If we want to characterize youth, adulthood, and old age in general according to their intellectual differences, we must relate these stages of life to the purpose of humankind and see how each fulfills its destiny in its own way. Human beings, as individual beings, are meant to represent the species itself, to be emanations of the universal kind of humanity. To this end, the human being must, through education, shed their particularity to comprehend the universal kind of the species, which is initially still foreign to them. Since this universality is not yet the individual's own, it exists externally as the existing state of the world in which they live and to which they is still inadequate. Thus, we distinguish between three basic stages of the human spirit. From the concealed opposition of their individuality and the world, man progresses to the struggle against it in order to overcome this conflict. Youth represents the immediate natural unity of the individual with the species, developing their inner being in order to conform to the species. To direct this activity towards the real world and to assert themselves within it is the work of adulthood. The adult stands independently in life, having to engage in a hard struggle against the world, whereas youth struggles with their own self. The elder, having completed the process of adulthood, has reconciled themselves with the world and returned to the original unity of childhood, but with the newfound wisdom of it.

### Infancy

The infant, to the extent that they cannot adequately communicate their needs, is completely dependent on parental care. Since the opposition between the universal and the particular has not yet appeared in the infant, it is an undeveloped individual who has not yet attained individuality, but is still immersed in universal natural life. The infant's progress now consists in moving from its state of dependence to independence, to becoming one with nature as a complete self-conscious being. The power of nature over the infant, which is greatest at the beginning, manifests itself through the infant's extreme neediness. This need is often revealed through crying, with which the infant immediately greets the light of the world into which they are born. But it is nevertheless a good thing that newborns cry, because if they did not, the parents would not know there is something they need. This cry is the awakening of the spirit over its subjection to nature. The struggle of infancy is a constant struggle with nature. Out of all the stages of life, infancy has the greatest potential for misfortune. An infant is completely helpless, vulnerable and exposed to every contingency of nature that threatens their existence. 

The developmental process of the infant has three significant milestones: learning to walk, getting teeth, and learning to speak. It has been said that these first steps of the human being are tremendous leaps, and that these first couple of years are formative for rest of their life. Firstly, through walking, the infant severes the first tie that binds it to nature. They are initially guided when they start to walk upright, but through walking, the infant can move wherever it wishes and frees itself its dependence on nature. Secondly, by developing teeth, the infant attains practical freedom in relation to their environment in that they now have the strength to eat solid foods and to not merely to receive it as soft nourishment or milk through its mother. These great epochs are not only of the highest influence on the body, but the mind as well. Finally, with this overcoming of the natural as an individual, the infant ceases to be a purely natural being. This metamorphosis of nature, in which the spirit is ignited, manifests itself in language. Language is the conception of things as universals. In language, the infant elevates the things surrounding it into the form of universality, to which they eventually conceive themselves as universal in referring to themselves as an "I". With this "I", the individuality of the child is complete. In seeing all of nature as a means to serve its own pleasure, equipped with this newfound egoism, the infant has matured into a child.

### Childhood

The consummate selfishness with which the child approaches the world should not be held against them, for it is the necessary condition of their hard-won independence from nature. With this independence also comes the beginning of a break with the existing world. The child, however, does not yet enter into struggle with the real world, which is generally foreign to the childhood years. The child relates to the world before them only as an imagined one, and they intuit this division between the world as it exists and the world as they imagine it. The child thus feels the need to grow up, they have the idea of ​​a higher state to which they are not yet suited and wishes to attain. What they want to be become is their highest aspiration, and it is in this aspiration that they must be raised. The first aspect of education is therefore the negative aspect, in that the child practically subordinates their natural will by following the rational will of another. The second, positive aspect of education consists in the fact that the concepts of the existing world also reaches them theoretically as instruction, the complement to discipline. The third aspect combines both, in a sense, as the artistic production of the objective world in the child's imagination, i.e. imaginative play. In these three moments, the life and occupation of childhood is created.

The universal kind, the species, which does not yet appear to consciousness of the child, after which their independence from nature has been perfected, can only be grasped as something external and foreign to the individual. This universal also does not yet have the consistency of the actual objective world, but still has an individual form, which is the higher, rational will of the parents or educators. Negative education, mainly in the home, but also still in school, consists in the child submitting to the will of the grown-ups, with them feeling that they not yet appropriate to their understanding. In order to get there, they must learn to obey. Discipline is the beginning of wisdom, those who know how to obey will later also understand how to command. And indeed, the child must first follow blindly. To argue with a child is a fruitless endeavor, for they always think they know better than and can even find being contrarian. The will of the grown-ups must reach them as such to bend their will. The purpose of discipline is to free the child from the drives of their natural will so that the rational will ceases to be something merely external to them.

Instruction constitutes the positive side of education. If the real world initially confronts the child as a higher, incomprehensible power, then this strangeness ceases through learning about it. Children are to become more familiar with the world, to incorporate it into their will and their thinking. However, they have not yet developed the abilities to actively and decisively engage with the world as adults have. The world can therefore only to be presented to them as an object of instruction. Children theoretically incorporate the real world into their thinking, simply learning about what it is, and this is precisely the purpose of instruction. This aspect of education is primarily reserved for public education in schools, whereas discipline falls more to the family at home, but both aspects can appear simultaneously in both. A realm of observations and ideas approaches the consciousness of the child, who on the one hand is sheltered from the real world in their home life, and on the other hand attends school as the first place in which they make contact with real world. 

The child's activity must always be a subject of routine practice. But since the child is unprepared to function as responsible member of society, this activity can only ever be ideal at this stage of life, existing in the realm of fantasy. In addition to this, an imitation of the real world must surround the child, in which they practice in order to one day become actual members of society. This imitation of reality cannot be taken seriously, but the person must also cannot remain at the level of fantasy or representation, and so this intermediate state, the need for which arises, is play. In play, the deeds of earlier generations are repeated and reenacted by the imagination of the growing child. The child's first activity, like all activity, is the negation of something that already exists. Thus, play begins with the urge to destroy. By destroying their toys (since they do not yet know to build things), the child sees themselves and their deed reflected in them, and is thus satisfied. Here, we can compare the play of the infant with the child. For the child, the object of play is not only toys, such as dolls, toy soldiers, etc., but the child's own persona. They frolic, fight, wage wars with one another, and thereby measure and exercise their strength. That imagination is primarily involved in play is evident from the fact that any object can be incorporated into play, and everything that the toy lacks in reality is completely replaced by the child's imagination.

### Youth

With puberty, which brings about the consciousness of the natural species, the intellectual consciousness of the species as a universal also arises. In the infant and the child, this universality is external, but only in youth does it become internal. Youth begins the seriousness of life and contact with the real world. Playful preliminary exercises for entering the real world no longer take place, rather, youth is the actual period of training for practical life. In the thoroughness of this activity, however, the opposition of the individual to the existing world unfolds in its full sharpness. The imagined world ceases to be foreign, it is reduced to the innermost ground of knowledge, and science is taught to the youth without reservation, thereby making knowledge of the real world the youth's own. Thus, the youth acquires the self-consciousness of their own reason, now capable of teaching themselves and without need for constant instruction. A thirst for knowledge supersedes the child's curiosity, the youth acquires a wealth of spiritual knowledge. In this way, a subjective universality develops in the mind of the youth, which is their own world, a world of their ideals. This inwardness predominates in the youth, they inhabit this inner world, longing to realize their ideals. Yet, by making themselves at home in this inner world, the opposition to the existing world arises. Universality has so completely taken hold of the youth that they seems to have abandoned the objective world. The world is thus, for the youth, an irrationality that has no justification in their eyes and that deserves to be abolished. The youth refuses to conform to the existing conditions of the world precisely because they do not correspond to their ideals. Because they do not yet experienced the real world, they consider their novel fantasies to be superior to anything else that has ever existed before. The youth is under the delusion that the world can be rationally shaped and planned in order to give reality to their ideals. Against these idealistic designs, the youth's own condition contrasts sharply against it. Because they are still in the process of forming their concept of the world, they themselves appear unfinished compared to the complete generality of the objective world. The delicate blossom of the ideal struggles to endure and withstand rough contact with reality. The ideal of a world as it ought to be, which stands vividly in the youth's imagination, but never comes to be, is the last obstacle the youth must face before reaching adulthood.

### Adulthood

With the suspension of ideals, the universality of the objective world is once again recognized. The adult does not bypass the existing conditions of the real world, they are recognized by them as a finished and complete reality. The subjective universality they originally attained in their youth is  seen as deficient, but only because it lacks objectivity. It is still an individual ideal that must first prove itself against reality and rise to true universality. The ideals, as subjective ends, must be realized, but they cannot be realized without activity. Thus, man's destiny is to be active in the real world, to make something of themselves within it, i.e. a member of society. The youth, who revels in the rich element of their ideals, has yet determine themselves, either because they lack the means to do so or they believe being a member of society will force them to give up their ideals. But man is only ever achieves anything when his activity becomes something actual. By entering into an actual relationship with the real world, man submits to it, serves it, and becomes its instrument.

While in youth, universality was on the side of the subject, in adulthood it is initially on the side of the object. The youth thus appears nobler and more selfless than the man. In the harsh struggle with the existing world, the adult must wrest their existence from it, providing for themselves and their family through work. Their relationship seems to be only one of need and dependence. Man thus becomes egoistic, striving for honor, prestige, and advantage. The youth, on the other hand, seems to forget themselves, caring not for themselves, but for the whole, for the possibility of a better future, seeking to give reality to his ideals. In truth, however, the matter is dialectically reversed. The youth always talks about improving the world, but in reality they do nothing except create themselves through their ideas, which is the implicit selfishness of the youth. The adult, on the other hand, relinquishes their desires in their work, and by seemingly caring only for themselves, their efforts are responsible for maintaining and developing real world. For the youth, rationality is only the possibility of rationality that does not exist yet. Living in their ideals, they see the world only as something foreign and separate from reason. Youth is so revolutionary in that it does not even recognize the world as it exists because the world is not as it ought to be. But to treat the contradiction between being and ought as a pervasive contradiction is just a youthful whim, a byproduct of the wanderings of adolescence. The adult recognizes the rationality in reality, and it is precisely through this recognition that they are able to gain a foothold in it. And by transforming the world through their activity, they come to the awareness that what is irrational and ought not to be is already real. This rationality that lacks reality is now the spark of science, from which they, as a youth, drew his ideals, and in which they, as an adult, brings to life, thus actually achieving and realizing the ideals that, for the youth, were merely subjective. Everything that the adult accomplishes and achieves succeeds is only by virtue of the ideas they acquired in their youth. Man is truly a cosmopolitan only when, through his activity, he leads the state and humankind toward a higher stage of development, accomplisheing the revolution that youth only dreams of. But this upheaval is simultaneously the preservation of the existing order, and what connects these two is the idea of ​​further development.

### Old Age

The adult grows old as the years pass, after which they have accomplished the purpose of their stage of life. If the adult has worked long in their profession, it becomes a habit, but once something becomes habitual, they begin to lose interest in it. I only have interest in acting as long as there is an obstacle to overcome, when this spur to action dulls, interest must also dull. Just as all vital functions weaken with age, so too does mental strength and activity tire. Since activity always focuses on the individual case, but the now elderly adult no longer has any interest in activity, they abstract from the individual case and returns to the abstract generality of theory. While the youth remains in the subjective generality of their ideals, and the man constantly realizes and isolates his ideas into concrete forms, the elder returns to the realm of the ideal. The ideal world of the youth does not yet correspond to reality, while that of the elder is detached from reality itself. They consider everything from a general perspective. Habit prevents him from seeing anything new in each individual case, rather, the experience they have acquired enables them to view everything according to tried and true principles. For the elder, there is nothing new under the sun, they have already experienced everything life has to offer. The experiences of their youth are particularly vivid for them and they often appeal to these, considering the state of affairs at that time to be the guideline and criterion for everything that still happens. 

The outstanding characteristic of the elder is therefore the striving to instruct the youth, to let them benefit from the rich treasure of their experiences so that they may avoid the pitfalls of life and act as they did before. Their means of instruction, however, is not action, but speech. The expression of this ideal world of theirs is itself the ideal of the word. The elder is garrulous, gladly shares their experiences, and incessantly recounts the deeds of the heroes of his youth. The stories they tell is the unity of the real and the ideal, which is restored in the elder. In the memory of the past, the elder is satisfied and reconciled, whereas the youth lives in the hope of the future, and the child in the pleasure of the present. The adult is, in a sense, the totality of these moments in that the deeds of the present, which they help to accomplish, sprout from the seeds of the past and allow the seeds of the future to germinate, making activity the connecting element of these three dimensions of time. The reconciliation to which the elder returns is certainly a unity with nature, as we saw in the child, so there is a sense in which the elder, according to the proverb, becomes a child again. At the same time, however, the unity to which the elder attains is the reverse of the child's harmony with nature, since the former arises entirely from consciousness and spirit. 

Through this progression of the ages of life, the individual is indeed a totality, but not yet fully developed, since they are always caught up in a moment of natural life. At most, the elder, as an ideal summation of these different stages of life in memory, is a subjective totality. But this totality is still not a real one for the individual as they are now. This totality only exists when the subject, as spirit, knows how to separate themselves from sleep and waking, from natural life, and confronts the same as a moment. The elder is also on the verge, as spirit, of relinquishing natural life, but only through the abstract negativity of death. That this separation from the natural is simultaneously the consciousness of the individual that it does not disappear as an individual, but rather that the individual re-establishes itself from unconsciousness as a knowing spiritual totality, distinguishes itself from its general natural life, and both modes of existence have always become alternating moments of its physical life as the dreaming soul.
