# Paterology: The Personality of God

## Introduction

The occasion that prompted me to deliver these lectures before you—concerning the personality of God and the immortality of the soul—is partly accidental, external, and personal, yet partly also substantive, internal, and essential.

As regards the external aspect first, it is surely well known to you—for the news has resounded throughout all of Germany—that, with reference to a mere few pages in the second volume of my *History of the Latest Systems of Philosophy* (wherein I merely briefly summarized, in their most general outlines, the views of modern philosophy regarding the subject at hand), a vociferous outcry of condemnation has been raised by a certain faction—one that imagines itself to be the sole possessor of true religion, and that does not hesitate to cast the stone of damnation against anyone who thinks differently, and most especially against philosophy itself. It is not to answer these calumnies that I appear before you here; rather, it is to dispel any potential misunderstandings to which my brief exposition might have been susceptible in the minds of those less intimately acquainted with the subject. Therefore, I intend here to discuss this question in a comprehensive, scholarly manner, drawing upon the ultimate principles of speculative thought.

Those opponents, however, rose no higher than hollow declamations; they refused to engage with arguments at all, thereby submitting the decision to the tribunal of the unscholarly public, and even sought to enlist the State in support of their zealous fervor. In the first edition of his polemic, Leo implored heaven and hell to persuade the State to strip the professors of the "newest philosophy" of their homes, livelihoods, and offices; in the second, he contented himself with merely appealing to the consciences of these lecturers, calling upon them to voluntarily renounce their right to speak, given that their doctrines were supposedly so pernicious to both State and Church. This concession—this retreat by the pious zealot—was, in truth, brought about by the voice of Germany as a whole; for not only did those under attack defend themselves with calm, measured, and convincing arguments, but others—even those belonging to an entirely opposing faction—duly and emphatically rebuffed such an "Albigensian crusade" within the republic of letters. The State, too, remained inert, refusing to allow itself to be used as an instrument by this new Church—a Church which, renouncing the Protestant freedom of thought, seeks to resurrect the dark ages of intellectual servitude and shackle Reason in the fetters of blind faith.

The inner impulse, however, that led me to subject this topic to public discussion is the state of philosophy in general—and, in particular, the history of recent philosophy over the past decade. As regards the former, the more a certain current within philosophy continues to deem the knowledge of divine matters impossible—viewing any illumination of such matters by reason partly as an absurdity and partly as a sacrilege committed against religion—the less may philosophy allow its absolute right to summon these subjects before its tribunal to be called into question through disuse. If, then—up until ten years ago—recent philosophy nevertheless remained silent on these subjects, the reason for this lay not in any desire to bypass them entirely or leave them unexamined; rather, it was because they were, for the most part, self-evident corollaries of the principles of this philosophy—conclusions that anyone could, therefore, derive from those principles without further ado. Moreover, among the numerous adherents of this philosophy, there appeared to be no divergence of opinion regarding these matters.

Once the principles had been established, however, the task of applying them further was undertaken. The results of speculation were to be popularized, and their consequences drawn out. This is the point at which it became apparent to what extent philosophy harmonized with the popular consciousness—or whether, conversely, it was called upon to modify that consciousness and elevate it to a higher plane. For even if philosophy—to quote Cicero—must be content with but a few judges, we nevertheless cannot grant the Roman orator the logical corollary of his statement: namely, that philosophy should deliberately shun the masses. On the contrary: once it has firmly established itself among the few capable of judging it, it will strive to disseminate its results—in whatever form—to the wider public; to extend its reach broadly; and, ultimately, to transform itself into the very fabric of the popular consciousness. Admittedly, this process may entail the necessity of relinquishing certain cherished notions—notions to which we cling all the more tenaciously because we imbibed them, as it were, with our mother's milk. Should philosophy, in the course of such an encounter with the common consciousness—or indeed through the potential unsettling of preconceived opinions—come to be regarded by the masses as "suspicious and odious" (to borrow Cicero's continuing phrase), then our task must be to demonstrate the groundlessness of such suspicion and to transform that animosity into love. From this perspective, therefore, the time has come to derive from the principles of philosophy those corollaries that serve to validate our religious convictions or to shed light upon our faith.

I have chosen to focus here, in particular, on the personality of God and the immortality of the soul—precisely because these are the very points regarding which the greatest divergence of views has now erupted, even among the adherents of the most recent philosophy; indeed, the history of philosophy over the last ten years is, in reality, nothing other than the history of the disputes raised within the very bosom of philosophy concerning these subjects. To reconcile these opposing currents—or at least to foster mutual understanding between them; to settle these disputes from within philosophy itself in such a manner—or even merely to point out the path toward their eventual reconciliation, and thereby to arrive at a conviction regarding the matter at hand that is, if possible, truly satisfying: this, then, is, more specifically, the essential motive that has impelled me to break my silence and to engage, without reservation, with the most compelling questions of contemporary philosophy. Your interest and your goodwill, gentlemen, will lend me the courage to exert my utmost strength in order to achieve a result that—even if only to a modest degree—does justice both to you and to the subject itself.

Yet, before we proceed to the discussion of the matter itself, we have a preliminary question to settle and an objection to address—one that is bound to confront us immediately. For one might ask: Why do we link these two points—the Personality of God and the Immortality of the Soul? Each, in its own right, would seem to be a sufficiently worthy subject of contemplation. Are we to treat of the one first, only to pass on to the other thereafter? In that case, they would constitute two independent inquiries, having nothing in common with one another and linked only externally. By announcing them here in conjunction, I have already implied that I regard their relationship as something more intimate; and this is precisely what I must now justify.

I posit between these two questions not merely a substantial kinship; rather I consider them to be absolutely identical—one and the same question. We must, first and foremost, demonstrate this essential unity of the two concepts by examining each from its own specific standpoint, thereby revealing that each is, in fact, contained within the other. Here, God and the Soul form a contrast—one which we may briefly characterize as the Eternal or Infinite Spirit on the one hand, and the Human or Finite Spirit on the other; and now, to each of these substances—which are ostensibly independent of one another—there is ascribed a predicate whereby the very Nature of the opposing substance appears to be imparted to it.

For if we speak of a personality of God, this appears contradictory, since personality—which expresses individuality and, consequently, finitude—seems, at first glance, to belong solely to man. Because human beings are persons, they mutually exclude one another; they limit, constrain, and encroach upon one another, and their interests frequently collide. It happens all too easily, therefore, that they allow their personal advantages—and, indeed, personal considerations in general—to take precedence over the matter at hand. Before God, however, there is no respect of persons; He acts solely out of objective motives grounded in the Nature of things and in Reason. Thus, by casting God as a person, we seem to place Him in opposition to other persons, to subject Him to the limitations of human personality, and thereby to reduce Him to the realm of finitude. Indeed, Stahl—who seeks to follow in Leo’s footsteps—goes so far as to regard it as the highest perfection of Christian consciousness that it conceives of God as having created the world arbitrarily—precisely because He is a person—and that God need not will the Good simply because it has been stamped as such by the immutable laws of Reason; rather, according to this view, it is God’s arbitrary will that, through explicit decree, first constitutes this or that as the Good. But are we entitled to ascribe personal predilections to God? Is the rational Nature of things not, in fact, the eternal counsel of God—a counsel preordained and transcending all personality? And is such arbitrariness not, precisely, the defining characteristic of finite, imperfect natures—a trait that flows directly from their very personality?

Conversely, by attributing immortality to the finite or human spirit—that is, to the soul—it becomes self-evident that we thereby grant it eternal duration and allow it to partake in the Nature of God. Thus, in these terms, each substance exchanges its proper determination for that of the other, and constitutes itself as the predicate of that very Being which stands in opposition to it.

Through this exchange, each of the questions raised becomes the counterpart of the other. To inquire into the personality of God is to investigate how the Divine comes to undergo finitude—and thereby, for the first time, to reveal and realize itself. To affirm the immortality of the soul signifies nothing other than admitting man into the imperishable spiritual realm of God. Hence, the question arises whether this mutual convergence—this reciprocal generation of one another—between these two problems constitutes, in truth, a detrimental contradiction, or whether it is not, rather, the very central objective of our entire inquiry. Indeed, we must answer this latter proposition in the affirmative.

We therefore raise both questions in conjunction—and for this very reason—because each constitutes merely the opposing half of one and the same whole; yet, at the same time, each is inextricably linked with the other and cannot be resolved without it. How could we determine the manner in which God might assume human Nature, were we not to demonstrate within that Nature those very elements by virtue of which it is capable of being elevated to meet the divine? If God descends to man, then man thereby ascends toward God. This, then, is the appropriate place—due regard being paid to the necessary modifications—to cite the words of Schiller in support of our thesis:

>When the gods were yet more human,

>Humans were more divine.

For this reason, too, the one assertion has invariably and necessarily entailed the other; and no sooner had the Imperishable been personified than men naturally sought to render the Personal imperishable as well.

If we now fuse those halves—as, by virtue of their inseparability, we must—into the composite whole we have alluded to, the question arises: what is this whole that here presents itself to us spontaneously, and as if involuntarily, emerging from the very formulation of these two questions? It is nothing less than the fundamental truth of Christianity—the very axis around which the history of humanity, and of its spiritual self-consciousness, has revolved for nearly two thousand years. This truth is, in fact, the unity of the divine and human natures—a unity which, when viewed from God’s perspective, constitutes His Incarnation; and when viewed from man’s perspective, constitutes his spiritual rebirth, or a higher, eternal life that he leads within the bosom of God. Thus, our two philosophically framed questions coincide precisely with those two Christian dogmas; for neither the personality of God nor the immortality of the soul appears in the Apostles' Creed as an article of faith expressed in these specific terms. The doctrine of the Incarnation alone encapsulates the personality of God; and the doctrine of eternal life coincides directly with the immortality of the soul.

We see, therefore, that the questions we have posed—at least in the specific phrasing under which I have introduced them thus far—are utterly devoid of any religious interest. They belong to the antiquated terminology of an earlier era of metaphysics; and since, in philosophy, expression and thought stand in a necessary reciprocal relationship, it follows immediately that a change in terminology must inevitably entail a corresponding modification of the underlying thought. Moreover, from a dogmatic standpoint, these expressions give rise to numerous objections. And after we have demonstrated the inadequacy thereof, we must then find the expression that we intend to put in place of the terminology to be discarded.

As regards, first of all, the concept of the personality of God: it immediately contradicts every sound system of dogmatics—one that does not seek to skirt around the deepest mystery of Christianity, the holiest treasure of human knowledge, in a frivolous, dismissive, or even mocking manner—namely, the Trinity. While the concept of personality does indeed appear explicitly in this dogma, it does so not in such a way that God is reduced to a single Person—which is, after all, what is generally understood by that metaphysical proposition—but rather in that a plurality of persons is ascribed to God. The stark exclusivity inherent in the concept of personality is thereby rendered fluid, and its limitations are transcended, inasmuch as the unity of the divine essence is affirmed as the ideality—and thus the fusion—of multiple persons, specifically three. According to this view, God is therefore no longer an isolated "Thou" standing in opposition to another equally isolated "Thou"; rather, He is a "We," within which atomistic personality has been shattered. The doctrine of the Trinity thus stands in direct contradiction to the proposition of the personality of God, as that proposition is commonly understood.

While the personality of God may be challenged from a theological standpoint, the concept of the "immortality of the soul" faces an attack stemming from a much more general perspective. For "immortality" is, in fact, a merely negative expression; it does not affirm this or that specific quality of the soul, but merely negates a quality that otherwise belongs to man—and is, indeed, indisputably ascribed to him. That man dies is the teaching of universal experience; and within the finitude of the individual organism lies, too, the necessity of its dissolution. It has sprung from something other than itself, requires external conditions for its existence, is thus bound to—and burdened by—that which is foreign to it, and thereby carries its "other"—or its end—within itself. Now, that which is predicated of man as a whole is, by the assertion of the soul's immortality, restricted to merely one aspect of man—his body; furthermore, this assertion quite uncritically posits body and soul in opposition to one another—without ever establishing the actual relationship between the two—and proceeds from the premise that, in every respect, the one is the exact opposite of the other. Thus, just as the body is composite while the soul is simple (and so forth), it is argued that—since the body is mortal—the soul must necessarily be non-mortal. Yet, what the soul actually is *in itself* remains entirely undetermined. All that is held to be known is this: that the soul does not perish along with the body, but rather—following the dissolution of this, its physical vehicle—is capable of dispensing with that vehicle entirely, or at the very least, is capable of making do with another one.

The immortality of the soul is, therefore—as Hegel observes—not a present quality inherent in the spirit; it is something future, something that is destined to befall it one day—or rather, something that is to be withheld from it. And regarding this future state, the only positive knowledge we possess is that it constitutes an infinite duration—the content of which, however, is to remain hidden from us while we are still in this life. Thus, the very concept of immortality is something entirely empty. Nor can it be affirmed as something actual, since at no single moment is this infinite duration ever fully present or completed. Immortality is, therefore, always merely a possibility—one that must constantly fear the opposing possibility: namely, annihilation. Indeed, it might even be argued that this latter possibility possesses the greater probability. For how can one deny the end, when one already knows the beginning? If the soul has once begun to exist, who can guarantee us that it will not, at some point, also cease to exist?

>For all that comes into being

>Is worthy only of passing away.

For this very reason, those who believed they were proceeding with the utmost precision and consistency—imagining themselves to be following Plato—felt compelled to postulate, alongside the immortality of the soul, an infinite *pre-existence* of souls as well.

Such difficulties and so many others are to be found in this doctrine of the immortality of the soul. They all stem from the fact that the other doctrine has been completely separated from it. For once the finite spirit is posited in isolation, it must indeed prove difficult to reintroduce—albeit indirectly through negations—the eternity that has been excluded from it. To render the atomistic,individual person eternal in its rigid isolation is fundamentally no different from deifying the finite. If one reproaches philosophy in the same breath—accusing it of doing precisely this while simultaneously denying the immortality of the soul—then that reproach falls rather upon those who believe they must defend this doctrine *against* philosophy, for it is *they* who ascribe absolute status to the finite *as* finite. Philosophy, however, denies only the *form*—not the *content*—of the proposition regarding the immortality of the soul. Here as well, religion once again provides the perfectly correct expression, in that it conceives of the preservation of the departed as the *Communion of Saints*—though all saints, according to Angelus Silesius, constitute but *One* Saint. This is the true conception of the question that we must adhere. Thus, the eternal Nature of the many individuals is rooted in the eternity of this *One* God—an eternity that encompasses all individual persons. The proposition that we are eternal only to the extent that we partake in the divine Nature is identical proposition to the question at hand, for what else is eternal besides God? Therefore, as long as the finite spirit remains strictly separated from the Absolute—and as long as no connecting link is found between the two—neither the one question nor the other can be adequately resolved. For if the finite spirit remains apart from the infinite, the latter is not personal, and—since the latter then also stands outside the finite—the latter, likewise, is not eternal. That God eternally finitizes Himself—eternally becomes Man—lies the absolute personality of God. But Man—as he is able to lift himself up to the task of manifesting this eternal personality of God within his own earthly existence, and thus of being a reflection and image of God—is thereby himself raised into eternity. Therefore, the immortality of the soul is the ceaseless *becoming-personality* of God, and—along with the eternal personality of God—an immortal element is thereby discovered within the individual.

This is the meaning I ascribe to both questions here. and if we wish to summarize them in a single expression, we may term them the problem of the eternal personality of the Spirit. For Spirit is the substance common to both man and the divine—that medium in which their mutual opposition and independence have vanished. The universal Spirit that permeates this opposition—in biblical parlance, is designated as the Holy Spirit—is the invisible bond and the *locus* of individual spirits, through which they all become *one* Spirit. The eternal personality that we ascribe to this Spirit is, therefore, a predicate that unites within itself two determinations commonly held apart—namely, personality (which is typically ascribed to the finite spirit) and eternity (which is typically ascribed to the infinite spirit). To both—in their reconciliation—reality accrues only through personality, and absolute Nature only through eternity. All that follows, therefore, is nothing other than the scientific substantiation of the meaning we have just now assertorically posited for these two questions—or, in other words, the solution to the problem of the eternal personality of the Spirit—a problem that unfolds into those two distinct questions depending on whether we approach it from the standpoint of infinity or from that of finitude.

For today, it remains only for us to present the division of the whole. The resolution of these questions—if it is not to remain a mere subjective assertion, but rather to receive a scientific foundation—will lead us into the very heart of metaphysics. The concept of personality and that of infinite duration are abstract logical concepts of a kind that we must first establish through a process of pure conceptual development, before we can apply them to the finite and the absolute spirit. This point will be most intelligible to those gentlemen who are simultaneously attending my lectures on Logic and Metaphysics, just as conversely it may also be viewed as a supplement to—and a specific elaboration of—a particular subject matter within those lectures. Furthermore, we shall have to examine more closely the Nature of the finite and the infinite spirit, as well as their mutual relationship. Thus, we shall transition from logical considerations to psychological investigations, and finally, we must touch upon theology. For as much as the relationship between the finite and the infinite spirit—viewed from its two distinct starting points—presents itself partly as the continuous incarnation of God and partly as the doctrine of man's eternal life, we must recognize and address our two fundamental questions within the context of these dogmas.  Ultimately, however, we aim to reach the culmination of our treatise in the exposition of the eternal personality of the spirit. In this manner, our truly scientific solution to the problem at hand will take the form of a logical-psychological-theological investigation—one that will, moreover, constitute the very core and center of our entire exposition.

## Universality and Individuality

To grasp the innermost significance of our doctrines, we must, for each of them, engage with a specific logical category—one that serves as its prerequisite and without which it absolutely cannot be understood. For the personality of God, this category is the concept of individuality and for the immortality of the soul, it is that of infinite duration. However, since every category can be rightly recognized only in relation to its opposite, we must first examine the relationship of individuality to universality, and subsequently, the relationship of time to eternity. In the course of which it will become evident that eternity and infinite time—far from being one and the same—actually constitute a distinct opposition to one another. Furthermore, as much as these two pairs of concepts must be correlated with one another and grasped within their mutual context, each pair will in turn contribute its share to the metaphysical foundation of the other inquiry.

By the term "universality," we now designate that which is truly existent in all things. For even if we adhere merely to the outward sound of the word, the universal is that which is common to all—that which is therefore not bound to the existence of this or that particular entity, but which, even with the disappearance of such an entity, still preserves itself within all the remaining particulars.

It follows from this that our knowledge—the more it remains immersed in particulars—becomes correspondingly more unreliable and limited: more unreliable because the particular entity, of which we possess knowledge, may cease to exist at any moment; more limited because, as long as it does exist, it represents only this specific mode of being in contrast to another specific mode of being, and thus becomes this specific entity precisely through the exclusion of a multitude of other determinations. If, on the other hand, I apprehend the universal, my knowledge is not only preserved in general amidst the perishing of particulars, but it also remains the very same knowledge in terms of its content; for with the passing of a particular entity, only those formal determinations perish by which it distinguishes itself from other particulars—not, however, that content which it shares in common with those that remain. Likewise, knowledge of the universal is more comprehensive than knowledge of the particular: for I apprehend a particular entity in and of itself only insofar as I have had specific, direct experience of it; whereas, by knowing the universal, I know all the particular entities subsumed thereunder, even if I have not previously observed them. Should new particular entities subsequently present themselves, I am able to subsume them immediately under the universal to which they properly belong. However, the more universal that knowledge is, the more individual particulars it also comprehends within itself.

The Nature of the universal is, accordingly, first and foremost its stability and imperishability amidst the constant flux of all particulars; and the more universal the object of my knowledge, the greater the degree of immutability that pertains to it. Furthermore, it becomes evident from what has been said that the universal encompasses within itself the fullness of being, inasmuch as the particulars are contained within it. The most universal of all is, therefore, that which is absolutely immutable—as well as the richest entity, or the highest principle of things—from which everything else is both most thoroughly understood and derives its entire existence. It is—to borrow a phrase from Leibniz—the sufficient reason for the existence of all things, whereas any universal subordinate to it provides this reason only for a specific sphere of particulars, one that is more or less comprehensive.

However, we must not conceive of these universals as existing *beyond* the particulars; for were we to do so, the universal would cease to be that which is *common* to all particulars, and would instead fall entirely outside of them. Such "transcendence" can be—and indeed has been—interpreted in two distinct ways: on the one hand, as something subjective; on the other, as something objective. If we wish to discover the true Nature of the universal, we must most emphatically reject both of these conceptions.

Whoever initially conceives of the universal solely in the subjective sense—as that which is common to all—perceives in Nature nothing but individual entities, which he then subsumes under certain classes. Each of these classes—for example, the dog, the plant, the human being, and so forth—constitutes a generality that we term a *generic concept*, and which we may indeed regard as a product of our own cognitive activity. The dog *in general*—as well as the human *in themselves*, the *archetypal* plant, and so on—does not exist in the Nature of things; rather, only the individual entities exist, which we, in this manner, merely arbitrarily group together within our own minds. If we stop at this level of generality, we find ourselves at the lowest stage of knowledge. Here, the universal is dependent upon the many individual entities we have observed. We may have misapprehended these individual entities and, consequently, abstracted the universal from them just as incorrectly. Or, we may have subjected only a small number of individual entities to our scrutiny; the universal we have derived from them is, therefore, merely a characteristic specific to *these* particular entities—a characteristic that may, however, be absent in those individual entities we have *not* observed.

The individual can never be experienced in an exhaustive manner since the number of particulars is infinite. No one would wish—or be able—to count even those that currently exist. What, then, are we to know of those that have passed away, as well as of those that are yet to be? Both of these series of particulars are just as infinite as the series of present particulars was innumerable. We thus find ourselves faced with the impossibility of correctly formulating such a universal and declaring it to be the objective essence of things. This arises from the fact that one proceeds from the individual and makes it the foundation of knowledge. The empiricists who take this path, for this very reason, deny the objectivity of this universal, asserting such generalities to be merely arbitrary products of the reflective intellect. In truth, such a universal—as Kant observes—is merely a relative or comparative universal; it applies only to certain particulars within a given class and is liable to be expanded or overturned by any subsequent experience. 

We may readily concede that such a falsely constructed universal is something merely subjective—something produced solely through an operation of our cognitive faculties. Yet it by no means follows from this that this universal is the only kind of universal that exists. Indeed, even a universal arrived at through this procedure need not necessarily be erroneous. For while we must acknowledge that the sum total of individual particulars can never be encompassed by experience, those particulars that *have* been observed may nonetheless have been observed correctly; and the universal essence inherent in them may likewise have been correctly distilled—such a universal. Yet we would not wish to assert it merely as something existing within ourselves. Rather, it exists within things as the objective Nature of the individual entities; the generic concept in our minds thus coincides with the creative power of nature—a power which, acting within all the individual members of the same species, perpetually sustains that species through the generation of new specimens, even as those currently living gradually pass away.

Here, one may fall into the opposite erroneous extreme: that of situating the Universal—not merely within the subjective thought of man, but as an objective existence—yet placing it nonetheless *beyond* individual things; specifically, within a transcendent, world-forming Intellect, or perhaps within a distant realm of spirits from which these forces emanate to animate, fructify, and perpetually rejuvenate the world of this side. This is the misconception to which the Platonic doctrine of Ideas was early on exposed—nor without a significant portion of the blame resting upon its originator, who frequently employed figurative and mythical modes of expression. It is not difficult, however, to uncover the contradictions inherent in such a conception.

In the first place, these formative forces of nature—conceived as general ideas and intelligible entities—are bound to no specific space; nor, consequently, do they exist *outside* of sensible things, for that would imply a spatial relationship between them. To whatever point, therefore, one might wish to assign the intelligible world—whether to the center of the universe or to its periphery, beyond the stars—such a placement would remain equally inadmissible. The forces belonging to it would then have to be dispatched, as it were—like sunbeams—into every part of the universe in order to act and to generate individual entities; yet, were this the case, they would inevitably interpenetrate, hinder, and disrupt one another. Furthermore, every effect produced by a spatial cause possesses only a specific spatial sphere of influence, which cannot transcend the continuity of space. Instead, however, we observe the most diverse forces acting at one and the same location, and one and the same force acting at the most diverse locations—a phenomenon that would be impossible were the effect mediated through space. The unity of each of these forces must not, therefore, be conceived as a *numerical* unity; for each force—notwithstanding its simultaneous presence at multiple locations (i.e., notwithstanding its numerical multiplicity)—remains, in terms of its underlying Idea, a single entity, and thus constitutes an *intelligible* unity.

Moreover, were these Ideas situated anywhere in space *outside* of the individual things themselves, they would stand as entities *other* than those things; they would thus be delimited by these individual entities and, by virtue of that very fact, would themselves become individual entities. For everything individual—precisely because it is individual—is a "this" and not a "that"; it is here, and not there. And because it is situated in a specific place, it excludes every other specific entity from its location—just as, in turn, it is itself excluded from the location of that other entity. Rather than honoring such a universal—whether it be a specific idea or even the absolutely universal principle of things—one instead degrades it, in this manner, into a wholly limited, finite being, should one attempt to endow it with a separate existence or to erect for it a wholly distinct throne in some particular part of the world—say, in a central sun.

Finally, if the general forces of Nature constitute the essence of sensible things, then one must not separate them from those very things. For how could anything exist if its essence did not dwell within it, but had instead become lost to it? Admittedly, the individual rose is merely a fleeting appearance of the general genus *Rose*; and this genus remains as the essence even when individual roses vanish—from which one might therefore conclude that the essence is indeed separable from the appearance.

We must now acknowledge both these opposing assertions; and from the resolution of this contradiction, the true Nature of the Universal will then reveal itself. For as long as this individual appearance endures, the essence is inseparably bound to it; the former is merely the essence stepped into existence, and the essence is the ground of this appearance.

>Appearance—what would it be, were it devoid of essence?

>And what would the essence be, were it not to appear?

By virtue of the fact that the essence is not swept away into oblivion along with the demise of any individual appearance, it offers the strongest proof that it is not bound to the spatial dimensions and sensory conditions of that particular individuality. Therefore, the Universal constitutes the essential being of every individual appearance, and the more it imprints and embodies itself within the latter, the more the latter is simultaneously assimilated by the former. The existence of the individual thus enters into a struggle with the universal species. The life of the individual ceaselessly strives to render itself adequate to its species. But since no one individual can ever completely express the species, the struggle of life concludes with the individual being assimilated by the species and reabsorbed into its bosom. The Universal is the substance that remains constant amidst the flux of appearances, negating them through ceaseless assimilation, but simultaneously preserving itself as the only positive, true, and substantial element within them as individuals of a species, and for which it is ultimately compelled to return.

Since the absolutely Universal has, no less than those forces of Nature, neither a merely subjective existence within our own understanding, nor an intelligence objectifying itself outside the actual world in space—there remains but one option: to conceive of this highest Being as the immanent essence and substance of the universe. What does not exist in or is limited by space is Thought, so the highest Being is Thought itself. Likewise, since Thought does not depend on or is limited to the subjectivity of any one human mind, it must also have objectivity or independence from it. Thinking is precisely the activity of the Universal revealing itself within the individual things themselves just as the individual things are revealed to us by our own thinking. In our very act of our thinking them, Thought serves as the essence of our mind within the sensible world just as, conversely, our mind is itself a product of this objective Universal. For within the mind, the Universal casts off the externality of sensuous existence and becomes conscious of itself *as* the Universal, thereby existing solely within the mind in a self-sufficient and adequate manner, whereas in sensuous things it had still retained the form of particularity.

As the inner essence of things, the Universal is indeed *within* them, yet it is also *external* to them, appearing as something separable and independent of them. This existent Universal is, as Augustine observes, bound to no specific place, and precisely because it is confined to no *particular* locality, it is present in *all* places, being simultaneously everywhere and nowhere. As a result, the absolutely Universal is not so much that which resides *within* all things than it is that *within which* all things reside—for all things they have forfeited their own autonomy and remain thoroughly subject to its activity.

Having briefly outlined the Nature of the Universal, we must now proceed to elucidate, in greater detail, its relationship to the Particular. A Universal existing *beyond* the realm of particulars would—depending on which erroneous perspective we chose to adopt—either degrade itself into something finite and particular, or else remain a mere empty, abstract universal. The eternal activity of the Universal consists solely in the ceaseless act of particularizing itself, never forfeiting its universality in so doing. The Universal is, in essence, that which has left no room whatsoever for any *external* relationships, rather all of its relationships are generated entirely *within* its own being.

The absolutely universal is the inner substance of all things, remaining self-identical amidst all distinctions that are unfolded as moments of itself. These distinctions do not lose the character of universality since the universal determines itself from within by unfolds into a circle of universalities. These universalities are precisely the realm of genera or ideas of things, and which can now be designated as the totality of particulars. On the one hand, particularity—as the universal posited with a specific content—is richer than the absolutely universal to the extent that it does not lose the latter's content, but adds something further to it. Yet on the other hand, when the particular is held against other particulars, it is simultaneously poorer than that universal since it is limited by them and excluded from them, whereas the absolutely universal encompasses them all. We must not, therefore, conceive of this universal as existing outside of particulars—any more than it could be posited outside of things in space. Hence, the system of particular ideas does not fragment into a multiplicity of independent forces, but all of them constitute the dependent moments of one and the same supreme Idea instead, which is precisely the absolute principle of all things.

Yet, even if this Universal has already further developed, realized, and manifested itself within its particularities, it cannot rest content at this stage, for the ultimate pinnacle of reality has not yet been attained. The particular ideas also lack reality in that they remain abstract universals that only become actual within the realm of individuality. We therefore encounter the ultimate pinnacle of reality only in the Individual. Indeed, the Individual appears to be the sole reality, for everything that has not yet advanced to the level of individuality remains a mere abstraction. The Empiricists have clung to this proposition so closely that they have asserted that individuality is the sole source of truth. Even Aristotle states that individuals are the *primary* substances, while genera are the *secondary* ones, since the latter are only supported by the former, which alone are capable of subsisting independently. The error inherent in the empirical standpoint (an error, incidentally, not shared by Aristotle) lies in conceiving the Individual as *purely* individual, thereby completely renouncing the substantiality of the genera. However, if individuality constitutes the pinnacle of reality—and if we choose to liken this reality to a pyramid—then we must surely acknowledge that its apex is nothing without the base upon which it rests.

Thus, while on the one hand the Universal remains a meager abstraction if it has not attained reality within the Individual, so too is the Individual a hollow existence without the content of the Universal. "The hardest thing to grasp," says Aristotle, "is that the principles of things are universal, and yet are not substances separate from the particulars." The true relationship between the universal and the particular is the absolute interconnection of both sides. To the content of the absolutely universal and the particular genera, the particular adds yet another determination—one by which it is distinguished from other particulars within the same genus, just as specific ideas exclude one another through their respective determinations. As a result, the particular is the richest in content, and the universal—precisely as it seems to lose itself in its descent toward the specific and the particular—also gains ever more content. For the particular individual is at the same time a specific instance of its genus, and by virtue of this, a particular mode of existence of the absolutely universal.

This excess of the individual is precisely its deficiency. That which separates individuals of the same species from one another consists of their inessential characteristics—traits that do not pertain to the species itself and remain indifferent to it. Herein lies the point where the inadequacy of the individual vis-à-vis the species becomes apparent. Its peculiarities may be more or less consonant with, or contradictory to, the species. Either they serve merely as the foundation upon which the species shines all the more brightly, or they obscure its substantial content, sometimes even to the point of unrecognizability. But the species can never be wholly suppressed even in the most hideous distortions of it, for even in such stunted existences, the power of Nature still shines forth, even if, due to external obstacles or defective material, it was unable to break forth in its purity.

The superadded element—joining the universal and the particular—that constitutes the principle of individuation, gives rise to the immense diversity and distinct characters of individuals. Since this element is inessential, everything great and true that individuals possess is instilled in them by virtue of their species. "As they possess anything peculiar to themselves," says Heraclitus, "they are in error." Thus, if the process of the universal consists in descending to the individuals, then the striving of individuals must ascend toward the universal. "The way up and the way down are, however, one and the same," as Heraclitus, albeit in a different context, likewise observes. For the more the Universal realizes itself within the Particular, the more the Individual conforms to its species until it flows back entirely into it.

The union of the universal and the individual is therefore not only the highest good, but is all that exists. A universal that does not have individual instances does not exist, and an individual that is not identified by a universal does not exist either. While on the one hand, the individual possesses the greatest content because it unites the universal and the particular within itself, this supreme value is, on the other hand, conditioned by the fact that the universal is the most clearly and distinctly expressed within it. As a result, it is the universal that ultimately possesses the greatest content, and if this value is ascribed to the individual, it belongs to the latter only to the extent that the universal has imparted the fullness of its content to it.

One consequence that follows from this relationship between the individual and the universal is that, while the universal can only ever exist within the individual, it simultaneously descends into the sphere of mutability. However, that which subsists through change is invariably the universal, while that which undergoes change is always solely the individual. The universal life-process of the various genera therefore consists in withdrawing from one series of individuals in order to manifest itself in others.

Since the union of the universal and the individual is the sole truth, there will always be new individuals for the universal to eternally realize itself. Hence the *Individual* remains eternal, even if *this* individual is destined to vanish. If Göschel attempts to prove that this specific individual—by virtue of its necessity—must possess an infinite duration in time, then here he commits a fallacy. Because the union of the individual and the universal is eternal, he argues, every specific individual in whom the universal has once realized itself must partake in this indissolubility. This assertion resolves the Klopstockian question—of whether the worm is merely dust or possesses an immortal soul—in favor of the latter, for since its genus has come into existence within it, the worm has thereby secured for itself an infinitely enduring existence. In his argumentation, however, Göschel conflates the *individual as such*—the *concept* of individuality—with *this* specific individual. Since this specific individual has come into being in time—and thus its union with the universal began at a specific moment—it will also cease to be, but the existence of the universal does not depend on the existence of *that* individual. If the universe was able to subsist for a long time as individuals come and go, then it will likewise be able to subsist after this union has been dissolved once again. In general, this specific individual is ineffable, for everyone is *this* specific individual. Even if language has succeeded—through the use of proper names—in capturing its uniqueness within a word, it still cannot, for that reason, prolong its actual existence.

While it is true that the concept of the Universal is necessarily linked with the concept of ​​the Individual, this does not mean that there will always be individuals in whom the universal is realized. Even less so can we single out any particular individual and accord it such preference as if the individuation of the universal were to take place primarily—or even exclusively—within it. Finally, the realization of the universal is not merely the sum of all individuals, for in that case, it would be endlessly fragmented into an infinite number of units. So how are we to conceive of the individuation of the universal such that—without making it a distinct, separate existence from the individual—it simultaneously remains something more than a mere abstract determination of the intellect? To grasp this, we might venture to say that it is even more difficult than fulfilling the aforementioned demand of Aristotle, or that it rather constitutes the deeper significance of that very demand, and only when we have answered this new question can we hope to have exhausted the subject.

Wherever we observe a multitude of individuals united into a single entity, this entity cannot manifest itself in a purely sensory manner—for were it to do so, it would also be compelled to exist as a distinct, sensory particular. This holds true in the taxonomy of animals, and even extends to the classification of inanimate objects. The sight of a city presents us with a unified whole, one that is constituted by the collection of its buildings, and yet the city is much more than the sum of these structures. Instead, even when viewed solely from a spatial perspective, we cannot deny this whole the intrinsic unity that characterizes individuality. We need only ascend a mountain or climb one of the city’s towers to discern, in that panoramic overview, its distinct characteristics and unique character. This universal quality—arising from the infinite diversity of specific localities—is by no means bound to the individual, sensory characteristics of the houses themselves. All the houses may be demolished and new ones erected in their stead, but the city as a whole remains.

If we now pass from the physical city to the spiritual city—that is, from the houses to their inhabitants—the same line of reasoning presents itself to us once again. The inhabitants possess their own customs, traditions, language, and modes of existence. Through all of this, the city is a distinct individual, it is what it is only by virtue of the collection of its individual members. But the individuality of the city does not consist merely of these individual members, it is an independent existence that endures even after every individual of the current generation has passed away and made way for the next.

The human race is also a great Individual—a living being that is neither merely distributed among the individuals currently alive, nor entirely limited to them, for were that the case, it would inevitably perish along with them. Instead, the individuality of the human race is its only true reality—that which lives and moves within all things, but does not dwell within them in a fragmented manner, but acts as one person: like an army, a city, or a state. Yet, this unity of action is more palpable in these latter examples than it is in the human race, for here the unity is more comprehensive and thus more difficult to survey. Moreover, in those instances, it is bound to a single individual—or to several—who appear to the senses, whereas the unity of the human race is represented solely by the invisible Principle of all things itself.

The actions of all these entities are, to be sure, executed by a specific individual: in an army, by the commander, in a city, by the mayor, in a state, by its head or minister, and in history, by the God-inspired heroes. Yet these deeds are not the deeds of these specific individuals, rather, these individuals are merely the instruments of that great, universal Individual—an entity that will, indeed, always select specific individuals through whom to bring itself to realization. But since this universal Individual remains the same—even when it must exchange its instruments once the previous ones have been worn out—it is not splintered into the separate individuals. This universal Individual is that very Person which, in Christian dogmatics, has been designated by the term "the Holy Spirit." Thus, when we hear of the Spirit of the subject matter, we may retain its expression—affirm the universal substance to be the true individual—without falling into the error of attributing to them a distinct, whether sensuous or spiritual, yet invariably fantastical individuality, for doing so would drag the universal individual down into the sphere of finiteness.

Thus, the individual entities vanish, while the universal individual persists. Yet, the relationship of the universal individual to the particular individuals is not always the same. For in lower forms of existence—those possessing only a sensuous mode of being—the universal individual is more of an abstraction; such is the case, for instance, in the previously cited example of the "sensuous city." Even the universal individual of the Earth constitutes a lower form than the concrete individuality of plants and animals; it represents merely the general possibility of their emergence, existence, and persistence. It is only within Spirit that universal individuality stands as the highest reality—the sole entity that truly *is*—precisely because Spirit alone—and specifically the individual spirit itself, as the concrete reality—generates within itself the consciousness of this universal Spirit. Such consciousness constitutes an individuality that remains eternally self-identical, regardless of which diverse particular individuals serve as the medium through which this self-realization is achieved. This eternal mode of being of the universal individual is the point at which we must take up our inquiry in the following lecture, if we are to plumb the innermost depths of the second of the metaphysical categories we have set out to examine: the relationship between Time and Eternity.

## Time and Eternity

From what was discussed in our last meeting, it follows, first and foremost, that the Universal—as that which is imperishable and indestructible—partakes of an infinite duration; whereas the Particular—though it be the constant actualization of the Universal—possesses only an ephemeral existence and is ultimately devoured once more by the power of time. However, were one to assert—on this ground alone—that the Universal exists *outside* of time, and that only the Particular is subject to the dominion of time, one would be gravely mistaken. For infinite time does not, by that token, cease to be *time*; indeed, the very *duration* of time expresses precisely this firm rootedness *within* time. The Universal, therefore, does not exist outside of time—no more than we were recently able to concede that it exists outside of spatial things. Rather—just as we asserted then that, although present *within* spatial things, it is not bound by their relations, but instead constitutes a realm of the intelligible independent of them—a realm that is, in fact, the immanent essence of the universe—so, too, must we now declare here that the Universal—though standing squarely *within* temporality—is not bound by its conditions, but rather guides and governs them. For were the Universal to exist outside of time, it would encounter a boundary in time; it would exist *before* and *after* time; consequently, it would have to cease to be precisely where time began, and begin to be precisely where time ended. Yet whatever begins to be does not yet exist at the specific moment in time immediately preceding its inception; thus, by the very act of attempting to exclude the Universal from time, we would—paradoxically—relate it *to* time, thereby rendering it, in the final analysis, something temporal and finite.

Just as it is impermissible to conceive of a limit to the Universal in its relation to time, so too must we refrain from assigning a limit to time itself; for to do so would fundamentally contradict the very concept of time. For time is, in essence, the abstract concept of change—of becoming—provided that we abstract away from the specific content of that which comes into being or undergoes change; it is nothing other than the intuition of this ceaseless transformation. The Being contained within the "Now" of time thus vanishes the very instant it comes into existence. The indivisible moment does not first arise in the present, persist for an immediate duration thereafter, and finally vanish at a third point in time; for were this the case, we would find ourselves with three distinct "Nows" standing in place of the single one under discussion. Thus, every "Now" comes into being at the very same instant in which it vanishes: and while its vanishing transforms it into the Past, its arising simultaneously brings forth the Future; and within the indivisible moment of the Present, these two aspects are one. Consequently, nothing possesses true reality save for this single moment; yet this moment, too, is eternal. For since time is nothing other than the ceaseless transition of Being in the Past into Nothingness, and of Nothingness in the Future into Being, there exists within it no Being that does not ultimately dissolve into Nothingness, nor any Nothingness that does not emerge from Being.

It is hereby demonstrated that time never began and will never cease, because in both instances, Being and Nothingness would not be conceived as being in a state of continuous transition—a notion that would, after all, contradict the very concept of time. For if time were to have a beginning, its existence would necessarily have had to emerge from a Nothingness that had not itself sprung from a prior Being; conversely, were time to cease, Being would flow back into Nothingness without the possibility of ever reconstituting itself therefrom. Yet both scenarios presuppose a separation between these categories—a separation that, in reality, does not exist at all. Thus, it follows from the metaphysical concept of time itself that it is of infinite duration. This conclusion flows even more immediately from the simple reflection offered by Aristotle: namely, that if time had begun, it must have begun *at* some specific time—implying that one would necessarily have to conceive of some earlier point in time when it did not yet exist. In other words, time would be posited precisely at the very juncture from which one intended to exclude it. The existence of time, therefore, necessarily presupposes its pre-existence in every instance; and its continuity absolutely precludes the assumption that this chain could have begun anywhere or could ever be severed.

Now, if time and infinite duration are absolutely identical—because time is nothing other than this very concept of pure duration itself, abstracted from whatever endures or vanishes within it—and if, furthermore, time and eternity are absolutely opposed to one another—as may surely be asserted with general consensus—then eternity cannot possibly be the same thing as infinite duration, but must differ from it in its very essence. But what, then, is eternity? Establishing this concept of eternity must be our primary concern here; for only in this way can we discern the relationship of the universal and the particular to this antithesis of time and eternity—and thereby, too, are we finally enabled to grasp the relationship of the universal and the particular to one another in its ultimate determination.

We have already seen that the universal is the eternal—and, as such, does not exist *outside* of time, even though it is independent *of* it; yet insofar as it *is* within time, it exists *at all times*. The character of the eternal thus consists in maintaining itself as the immutable amidst all the flux of time; and it possesses this character solely through thought. For:

>Whatever hovers in wavering appearance

>Is made steadfast by enduring thought.

Thought is the realm into which sensory illusion, the deceptive semblance of the phenomenal world, and the categories of origination and decay that belong to it, cannot penetrate. It is through thought that we find ourselves within the realm of the imperishable and the indestructible. Conversely, everything that is not erected upon the rock-solid foundation of thought is bound to perish. For thought is, in truth, the World-Judge that summons all things before its tribunal and pronounces judgment upon them.

Now, every phenomenon—even the most fleeting—provided it encapsulates a thought, finds in that very thought a pillar of its own imperishability, even though, as a sensuous existence, it cannot endure. The very preservation of a thought expressed within this or that historical phenomenon requires that it have shed its outer husk, and it is only in this way will it appear in its pure, unclouded, and transfigured form—whereas previously, it remained intermingled with the accidental and the inessential.

>What seeks to live eternally in the poem

>Must perish in life itself.

Thus, it has often been observed that the deeds of world-historical individuals—such as Socrates or Christ—were truly recognized, and their effects felt in their full scope, only after these individuals had departed from the stage of history, once their thoughts had divested themselves of the sensuous, individualized husk by which they were still enveloped during the lifetimes of their originators.

It is evident from the foregoing that the eternity of a form is by no means dependent upon the duration of its external existence, nor does it bear any relation to time whatsoever—least of all does it consist in an infinite duration of that existence. What, then, is the criterion by which the eternity of a form must be judged? Since it is not merely this formal relationship to time, the only conclusion remaining is that, in answering this question, everything depends solely upon the thought that constitutes the content of that form. And here we may even assert that precisely those forms to which such duration most readily accrues are often those that possess the least of this intellectual content.

If, by way of substantiating this claim, we now survey the entire realm of Nature, we shall primarily designate its creative forces as eternal—precisely because they express the conceptual content of the absolutely universal principle of things in its full purity, and constitute the necessary particularities into which the Absolute Idea unfolds itself. Yet, if these ideas are not to remain mere empty generalities, they must also have invariably manifested themselves in concrete individual forms. Thus, even if—in the strict sense—the only eternal element within Nature is the creative thought from which it originated, we must nonetheless simultaneously reject the notion of any moment in time during which Nature itself did not exist. This conclusion, in fact, follows directly from the premise that, since time itself never began, the process of becoming can likewise have had no beginning; rather, there must always have been some substantial entity in motion and undergoing change—that is to say, a sensible Nature must have existed. For it is impossible that there could ever have been a time when nothing occurred; such a time would be an empty time, and time is nothing other than the succession of changes in things.

Notwithstanding the infinite duration of Nature as a whole, we nevertheless observe the greatest differences in duration among its individual entities. Astronomical motions, to be sure, presuppose a continuity of succession as uninterrupted as that of time itself; for they alone constitute the measure and the generator of time. As these mathematical phenomena of Nature that govern time, Aristotle therefore designates them as "The Eternal" within Nature; thus, the celestial body—even as an individual entity—shares in this infinite duration, for it is precisely this that is commonly understood by the term "eternity." Terrestrial formations of Nature—which, as Aristotle says, live "beneath the changing moon"—are, by contrast, immutable only in their creative forces; yet, in their sensible appearance, they are caught in a perpetual process of coming-to-be and passing-away. And yet, it is precisely these that constitute the noblest and most perfect beings in Nature. Plants and animals perish, and other individuals take their place, while the mountains stand in enduring permanence, and the "eternal stars" have even become a common figure of speech. Yet, this hard, dark mass of stone—or that countless efflorescence of light presenting itself to our eyes in the nocturnal firmament—does not constitute an existence as exalted as the infinitely more artistic organization of even a mere worm.

Herein, surely, lies the best proof that eternity is not to be sought in mere duration. For otherwise, why would precisely the simplest, most elementary—and therefore lowest—creatures of Nature endure the longest, while the most developed and noblest prove to be the most transient?

Yet, were we to attempt—viewing eternity solely from the perspective of its mere appearance—to relate it, for once, to time (given that it must, in truth, possess infinite duration even within itself), then, even in this ordinary sense, eternity could not be ascribed to those enduring forms of Nature. At the very least, infinite duration is not necessarily encompassed within their concept—even if they were, empirically speaking, to endure for an infinite amount of time; a fact, however, that we can by no means know, since such time has never fully elapsed. Chimborazo, to be sure, has already endured for quite some time. But who can guarantee us that an earthquake will not shatter it? Could this not happen today, tomorrow, or on any given day? Indeed, is it not—in all likelihood—itself merely the remnant of a larger mountain, the summit of which was broken off during that great terrestrial revolution which gave rise to the present constitution of the atmosphere? In this respect, therefore, it would already fail to be eternal, inasmuch as we cannot assert that it is without beginning—even were someone willing to guarantee us that it is without end. Might not even the "eternal" stars lose one of their companions without suffering any harm themselves—and without, as the saying goes, a single soul being any the wiser? Did not Herschel claim to discern, within the nebulae, worlds actually in the process of formation? Indeed, in the case of comets, it has been established almost to a certainty that they are masses of vapor—some of which dissipate just as readily as they originally coalesced.

Thus, if it is not the duration of a thing that determines whether we may predicate eternity of it, but rather its content, then the next question is this: how must the thought underlying a natural object relate to the mode of appearance of that object in order to endow it with eternity? For the mere fact that the thought is present within it at all is surely not sufficient. Otherwise—since there is no natural entity that has been treated so shabbily by Nature as to be utterly devoid of any thought whatsoever (given that every individual instance invariably bears within itself its genus as the universal creative Idea of ​​that genus)—it would logically follow that *every* entity is eternal. The thought must, therefore, have found within the object a representation commensurate with itself, and must have unfolded therein in accordance with all its determinations; it must not be merely stunted or bound up in contradiction with such an existence. However, the degree to which individual phenomena partake in the universal Idea varies greatly; and the more powerfully, clearly, and pervasively the thought has manifested itself within a phenomenon, the greater is its claim to eternity.

Based on these preliminary observations, we can therefore define the concept of eternity as being nothing other than the *correspondence* of a reality to its own concept. Everything that corresponds to its concept is eternal.

Let us now return to our previous examples drawn from Nature. In those long-enduring entities, we shall find only a meager echo of thought—a realm in which the Idea of ​​Nature has not yet been able to secure for itself an adequate reality. The light that constitutes the substance of the constellations is, to be sure, a representation of thought—yet only of abstract identity, which still lacks all fulfillment and content. Intelligence, as Schelling observes, has indeed "turned to stone" within Nature, and yet, it can find no other corresponding means of expression. The organic forms of Nature, by contrast, express in a far more fitting manner the ceaseless activity, the life, and the process of thought, as much as they sweep inert matter up into its very vitality, such that the organism must be regarded as the most perfect image of intelligence within the natural world. For just as thought is nothing other than the process of the Universal constantly individuating itself—and thereby realizing itself—so too is the animal organism merely the process of perpetually bringing the Species to manifestation within the Individual, and of idealizing matter; whereas in the stone, matter—in its brittle singularity—resists thought, yielding only slowly through the process of weathering. In the process of the Species, conversely, the Individual continually emerges from the Species; and likewise, the Individual—within its own internal process—increasingly develops the archetype of its species, striving to become an adequate expression thereof, and ultimately—in death—dissolves its separate existence precisely because that existence is not, in every respect, fully commensurate with the universal Idea. Since, therefore, thought bestows eternity—and that existence partakes most fully of eternity in which thought has most completely expressed itself—it remains, in this regard, a matter of indifference how long its duration may have been, or was, or will be. This realization of thought is, solely and in itself, that which truly *is*; thus, to its forms we may apply the words of Goethe:

>They are not shadows that illusion breeds.

>I know it well: they are eternal, for they are.

From this concept of eternity—specifically, from the requirement that it correspond to its own Nature (a correspondence which, by definition, necessitates two distinct sides)—it follows that eternity is not a simple mode of existence, but rather the relationship between these two sides. Neither the concept *as such* constitutes the eternal, nor, conversely, does any mere *reality*; rather, the eternal emerges precisely at those points where the concept and its reality converge. A thought lacking reality is an empty ideal; indeed, it is all the less eternal the longer it merely drifts about in the minds of men in this disembodied state. For, admittedly, in the course of history, a thought may initially reside for a brief period within the minds of a few advanced individuals, before passing into the consciousness and will of the collective, there to find universal recognition as a concrete, actual existence. Conversely, an external existence—one that serves as no true embodiment of a thought, that is devoid of intellectual substance—is equally devoid of eternity, even should it endure for a long, or indeed an infinite, span of time. It may, perhaps, endure for so long *precisely* because no underlying thought acts as its driving force—a force which, were it to break through, would shatter the earthly shell containing it in order to give itself free rein. Thus, approaching the matter from a different angle, we arrive at the proposition that an existence endures all the longer the more mediocre or inferior it is—and the less it allows the true concept of life, consisting of the eternal interplay of forces, to burst forth from within itself. For such an existence remains indifferent to the process and progress of thought, while thought, in turn, leaves it lying by the wayside—untouched and left to vegetate—often all the more so, the longer that existence has already endured.

However, since eternity is neither entirely indifferent to time nor external to it—even if, for eternity, the duration of time is not the sole consideration—we must now establish the precise relationship between time and eternity.

First and foremost, we now recognize—not merely because of the perpetual incompleteness of infinite progress, but for a deeper reason—why infinite duration is not the same as eternity, even though the latter may well subsume the former as a subordinate category. For in the case of infinite duration, the focus does not yet lie on the content, but solely on the time—regardless of what the actual content of that which thus endures in time may be. Something can exist throughout infinite time without corresponding to its own concept; and thus, we could never possibly designate it an *eternal* form. Consequently, infinite duration has rightly been denied to that specific form—namely, the concept of the Devil—to which, in religious representations, this very reality (of not corresponding to its concept) has been ascribed as a preeminent characteristic. For this infinite duration is precisely the form under which the concept of eternity appears to ordinary consciousness—because, in truth, it constitutes the very element of that concept's appearance. Thus, according to ecclesiastical doctrine, the Devil is a being that fell away at a later stage, forfeited its divinity, and is destined to be utterly vanquished at the end of time, so that God may once again be All in All.

Generally speaking, infinite duration—when held in isolation—is a murky, indistinct notion that, upon closer analysis, disintegrates within itself, and at the very least, it lacks true reality, so it is the least suitable concept to serve as an expression of eternity. To be sure, the continuity of time compels us to refrain from positing an absolute beginning or an absolute end to the world at any point. In the ceaseless succession of states, one change follows upon another, just as one change has preceded another. Yet, however far we may trace the chain of causes upward, or the chain of effects downward, we shall never arrive at a first or last point where this thread breaks off. The infinity that we project into the future, or relegate to the past, is therefore no more real than the other. Two realized infinities would immediately contradict one another; for each would be limited by the other, and thus each would constitute merely a finite series. But even the assumption of a single completed infinity is equally nonsensical; for nothing would remain for the other series, given that an infinite entity tolerates no boundary lying outside itself. The future, then, is not complete—if only for the reason that it has not yet even begun. Were we, therefore, to assert that the past constitutes the realized infinity, this series would indeed have run its course; yet anything that has run its course is, as such, itself merely finite—not infinite.

Infinite duration is, therefore, strictly speaking, a mere possibility—never something actual. We possess the faculty, in our imagination, to proceed ever further backward or forward without ever encountering—within that imagination any more than we did previously in reality—an ultimate limit. Then if infinite duration is merely a possibility, how can we attribute eternity to it—given that eternity is, rather, the fulfillment of this possibility: the actuality that corresponds in every respect to the concept, and is, consequently, the absolute unity of possibility and actuality?

Thus, we have already subtly alluded to the contradiction into which one falls the moment one attempts to prove the immortality of the soul by arguing that the infinite duration of the individual is a necessity if the Idea of ​​moral perfection—present within that individual—is to be realized. One is, to be sure, entirely correct in asserting that the reality of the moral human being—a reality perfectly corresponding to the Nature of holiness—constitutes the foundation of their eternity. However, by assuming that the realization of this Idea is possible only within the span of infinite time—and by subsequently defining it more specifically as a *gradual approximation* toward holiness—it becomes clear that, if one merely *approximates* an Idea over the course of infinite time, one never actually *attains* it, that one rather remains forever removed from a goal that is, by definition, to be reached only within infinity. By positing the notion of infinite duration as the underlying premise, one has, therefore, rendered impossible that very eternity—defined as the correspondence between concept and reality—which was, in fact, fully realized in the state of holiness. Moreover, under these premises, it becomes entirely immaterial how near or how far one may still be from the goal; for an infinity still lies between oneself and that goal before one can ever hope to arrive at it. No matter how many epochs have elapsed, how many states have been undergone, or how many perfections have been left behind—I draw no closer to the goal, to this Infinite; for if I subtract what has been attained from the infinity of what remains to be attained, that infinity—according to Haller—still lies entirely before me. For otherwise, the subtracted quantity would serve to augment the quantity from which it is subtracted—forming its complement—and that latter quantity would therefore not be infinite.

If eternity is neither the infinite progress of effects into the future nor the infinite regress of causes into the past, then, of the dimensions of time, only the present remains as the worthy seat of eternity. And this would find its justification in the very fact that the present is the only reality, whereas the future and the past exist merely in the human imagination as hopes and memories. Nevertheless, the present—being limited to a single, fleeting instant that constantly vanishes the moment one attempts to grasp it—is, in fact, the exact opposite of eternity. Thus, eternity bears no relation to any single dimension of time, if we consider each in isolation; yet, since it is not meant to exist outside and beyond time, we can resolve this contradiction in the simplest—and indeed most adequate—manner by asserting that eternity is itself a relationship among these three dimensions of time: specifically, their absolute unity and mutual interconnection.

This means that the Eternal—conceived as the Universal which realizes itself within the particular—does indeed bear a relationship to the future; it is constantly coming into existence, and in this manner, it perpetually gives birth to the future. Likewise, it constantly draws other particulars back into its bosom, allowing them to pass away and shrouding them in that veil of the past. As that which destroys and brings forth forms, it is simultaneously the ever-present, the never-aging, the eternally self-same. The Universal thus persists amidst the shifting transitions of these dimensions into one another—as that which is, was, and will be; it is the thread running through these distinctions, possessing in the present the moment of its actuality, and in the future and the past, the infinite fullness of its potentiality. The absolutely Universal—which appears at any given moment in a specific form—harbors within this form of the natural and spiritual universe (which we designate as the "present") all past forms as well, as their causes, for this present form is the result of the labor of all preceding ages. Similarly, within its inner essence, the seeds of the future—as its effects—are already implanted, destined to emerge into the light with the rolling passage of time. Thus, notwithstanding this ceaseless flux of time, the absolutely Universal remains eternally self-identical, for within each of its manifestations, it bears within itself the entire substance of all past and future forms. It therefore rests, unaltered, within the present—no matter how fiercely the waves within the tides of time may clash against and displace one another.

This present—as the sole reality—never ends, it no longer holds the future and the past outside of itself, but comprehends them within itself as its own moments. This absolute present of the Universal—expressing itself in each of its forms, and notwithstanding the infinitely enduring flux of time, remaining eternally identical with itself—is Eternity. Eternity is therefore the true, perfected concept—or the very thought—of Time itself, it is the resolution of the contradictions inherent within Time, as much as Time’s ceaseless restlessness has simultaneously become absolute repose and self-identity, and a single moment has merged with infinite duration. 

>They say that Time is swift. Yet who has ever seen it fly? 

>For, within the concept of the world, it remains utterly motionless. 

Eternity—as that which is solely and truly present—is fully realized in every single moment, for in every such moment, the Concept corresponds perfectly to Reality, and for this correspondence to obtain, a single moment suffices. However—since, conversely, no moment can be conceived in which the infinite Idea would fail to correspond to its own manifestation—this correspondence endures for infinite time, and thus, this perpetual duration necessarily constitutes the mode of appearance of the Eternal—a mode which, through this very union, sheds its inherent absurdity, for it no longer requires completion in order to serve as the manifestation of the Eternal. The Eternal, therefore, holds the infinite series—stretching both forward and backward—beneath itself as its own constituent moments, without itself being swept up into this ceaseless flux.

Having, in the concepts developed today, resolved the opposition between time and eternity—just as in the previous lecture we resolved that between the universal and the individual—we must now draw the conclusion from this twofold metaphysical inquiry by reducing these two oppositions back to one another and defining their mutual relationship.

The universal—which has not yet descended into the process of individual life—we may term the *abstract universal*, it stands beyond living individuality. Insofar as it is thereby removed from the realm of transience, it retains only one aspect of the Eternal: namely, the aspect of its mere appearance—that is, a lifeless repose or infinite duration. To this category belongs the universal vitality of the Earth, which constitutes merely the general possibility of individual life, thus it remains the unshakable presupposition of such life—the ground from which individuals arise and into which they ultimately return.

In this sense—with its colossal limbs, the mountain ranges—the Earth serves as the tranquil ground of individual life, yet its own life is merely a life of the past, a life that has passed away. This universal life of the Earth is, in turn, itself but a single moment within the universal cosmic life, and the constituent elements of this greater life—the Sun, the Moon, and the stars—likewise partake in the inert repose and eternal monotony characteristic of the Earth's natural existence.

Just as the actual, singular individual—who, at the highest level, as a human being, rises to self-consciousness—finds the abstract foundation of his existence in that general individual, so too does he find, in the absolutely Universal—the creative Thought—the inwardly driving principle by which he is impelled into activity, yet also swept up into the flux of change. The individual thus, on the one hand, departs from the dead repose of the abstract Universal and participates in the eternal process of unfolding Thought, yet, on the other hand, he does not therein attain absolute identity with that Thought. Consequently, the actual individual—in his singularity—belongs most truly to Time, and specifically not to Time as infinite duration, but rather to Time as Kronos, who ceaselessly devours his own offspring. Once he has, for his part, embodied the realization of the Universal, he returns—in his mortal aspect—to the bosom of the general natural individual, yet—in his immortal aspect—he returns to the eternal Thought of the absolutely Universal, for he is but a single member of a single form of the Absolute—a form which itself, in the course of time, eventually vanishes. He is, therefore, eternal precisely because—as the aspect of reality—he corresponds to his absolute concept; and this adequate manifestation of the Universal within the individual constitutes the very definition of eternity.

>I myself am eternity, when I depart from time,

>And gather God into myself, and myself into God.

However, insofar as the singular individual, as such, perceives his own endurance only within this limited span of time, he embodies eternity within himself only for that duration.

Finally, the absolutely Universal—which realizes itself in every particular—adds to the process and eternal flux of these particulars the enduring repose of the abstract Universal. It is itself a process, just like the individual entity; for it unfolds itself into the infinite series of its forms. Yet, in this very process, it simultaneously preserves itself—much like the universal Earth-entity—because, unlike the particular, it is not merely a single, transient moment of this process; rather, as the totality of its moments contained within itself, it is that which is present in and governs every link of this process—such that, amidst this eternal process, it simultaneously rests eternally within itself. Thus, the lowest endures just as the highest does; yet with this distinction: that the former remains excluded from the process of the mediating center, whereas the latter—without losing itself therein—possesses that process within itself as the very mode of its own manifestation.

## The Human Spirit

Having discussed the metaphysical basis of our inquiries, we must now become more intimately acquainted with the subjects—God and Man—whose predicates, personality and immortality, we are ultimately tasked with explaining or rectifying. These subjects, however, can in turn be subsumed under the higher concept of Spirit. Thus, we must first examine the Nature of Spirit in general, in order subsequently to classify it into its specific forms: the finite and the infinite Spirit.

In order to define the concept of Spirit in general terms, we must delimit it against that which is other than itself, and determine its Nature in contradistinction to it. This "other"—to which Spirit relates and against which it distinguishes itself—is Nature. And if we wish to establish the relationship between the two in its most general form, we must state: Nature resides within the realm of Space, whereas Spirit manifests itself in Time. We shall be able to elucidate the meaning of this all the more readily, given that in our earlier investigations we have already had occasion to acquaint ourselves with these two forms of existence—and specifically with Time.

Space is the absolute *being-outside-one-another* of infinitely many points—points which, even as each absolutely excludes every other, nevertheless all coexist tranquilly side by side; Space is, therefore, this very contradiction: that while every "Here" negates every other "Here," this negation is, at the same time, never truly taken in earnest.

Herein lies the peculiar character of Nature. Each of its forms sprouts up calmly alongside the others, without concerning itself with them or having any knowledge of them; each exists absolutely outside of the others—the one here, the other there—and where the one is, the other simply cannot be. Nevertheless, each allows the other to exist peacefully alongside it; and only when one attempts to encroach upon the space of another does a struggle and a negation of forms ensue, wherein the one is shattered by the force of the other. Their relationship is, therefore, an external one, and their connection is that of a blind necessity; Thought—as the universal divine Reason—is indeed the bond that weaves the formations of Nature together into a single systematic whole; yet Nature itself knows nothing of this immanent unity of its own. The divine Idea—which in Nature is bound fast within the iron fetters of necessity—thus exists therein in a reality ill-suited to its own Concept. Absolute Reason has lost itself within Nature, has become alienated from itself therein, and thus exists only in the form of *otherness*.

Time is, of course, not absent from the forms of Nature, though because Nature is ill-suited to its own Concept, it cannot bear within itself Time in its true conceptual essence—that is, Eternity—but rather falls subject to the mere *mode of appearance* of Time. For in presenting to us the relationless coexistence of its formations, the element of Time that we encounter in Nature is merely Time posited as Space—or the element of lifeless duration—as is particularly evident in those forms we recently termed the "abstract universal": namely, the geological organism of the Earth and the starry heavens. While these forms of Nature endure as individuals, the organic forms endure only as species. Yet, insofar as all these formations are also swept up in the flux of time, their process is merely an ever-recurring cycle—partly of celestial motion, partly of the seasons, and partly of life and death—such that Nature nullifies each of its changes, returns time and again to the very same point, and thus remains trapped in that eternal sameness of which the wise Solomon could say: “There is nothing new under the sun.”

This duration is merely abstract duration, and change, likewise, merely abstract change, for it is the peculiar characteristic of Nature that the categories which manifest within it do not appear in a state of living interpenetration, but rather as isolated moments. But wherever a true development and a progression in time become perceptible within Nature, this always stands in relation to the free development of Spirit: just as the great catastrophe that befell the Earth—imparting to its surface its present form—was merely the condition for the newly generated life of Spirit, and thus a presupposition brought about by Spirit itself, and so too do the gradual modifications of Nature—still perceptible even now—signify Nature’s concomitant life that accompanies the progress of Spirit.

Conversely, Spirit stands preeminently upon the ground of Time. For just as Time does not leave any of its moments standing indifferently outside of one another—but sees future and past compressed within the indivisible point of the present—so too is it the peculiar characteristic of Spirit to compress its determinations not (as Hamlet would wish to do with the world) into a nutshell, but into a single, indivisible point which, by withdrawing from Space, thereby attains infinite expansion within itself. This constant self-presence and collectedness of Spirit constitutes its freedom. By virtue of this freedom, Spirit is never surrendered to a form alien to itself—and thus never falls prey to necessity—but remains self-identical and unchanged in each of its forms, since in each of them its entire essence comes to manifestation. Its distinctions lack external subsistence, they are always posited and subsequently superseded by freedom. This free activity is the very substance of Spirit that, like time itself, never rests and is in constant motion.

Since the forms of the spirit cannot in this way be placed side by side as static figures in space, nor be exhibited within this spatial externality, that which the spirit possesses has withdrawn into the absolute simplicity of thought—a realm where no element excludes another, but rather all have fused together into a single unity. Insofar, however, as these forms are nevertheless to be distinguished one from another and to appear as independent entities, this can take place only in the temporal succession of time—that is, in history. History is, therefore, the mode of the spirit’s self-revelation; yet it pertains solely to the spirit’s *appearance*. In truth, even here, the forms of the spirit do not exist externally to one another, inasmuch as we have already observed that, within any given form of the present, the entire series of past and future forms is encompassed in the mode of possibility.

In contrast to the *real* externality of Nature, the defining characteristic of the spirit is thus *absolute ideality*. Nature, at every point in space and at every moment in time, is invariably nothing other than this one specific entity, to the exclusion of all other determinations. Notwithstanding nature’s infinite richness, it can never survey or totalize that wealth; thus, wherever one turns to Nature, one finds it to be utterly impoverished. The spirit, however, embodies at every point the entire fullness of its being, and amidst its infinite diversity, it never forfeits its absolute simplicity; and this is precisely what we designate as *ideality*.

Thus, while in no single part of Nature does reality correspond to the Idea—but only insofar as a thinking intellect concentrates all the scattered members of Nature (of which Nature itself remains unaware) into the single focal point of cognition—the Spirit, conversely, constitutes a center in and through itself at every point, and from that vantage point surveys the entire periphery, indeed, in each of these points, the whole panorama of its circumference is reflected as in a central mirror. The Spirit, therefore, corresponds to its Idea at every point of its reality, and true eternity is consequently the heritage of the Spirit—whereas to Nature, notwithstanding the constant flux of its forms, we could ascribe only infinite duration as the mere mode of appearance of eternity.

If we now wish to isolate and single out from this general concept of the Spirit those attributes belonging specifically to the human or finite spirit, the very concept of a finite spirit would seem to harbor a contradiction within itself. For, according to all that we have established, the character of finitude belongs solely to Nature since it is everywhere bounded and limited, and thus each of its individual forms is finite. However, since in the Spirit all distinctions and determinations are invariably gathered back into a single living unity—just as the infinite wealth of its representations is preserved within the indivisible point of the *I* without collision or motion—the Spirit constitutes the very opposite of finite natural phenomena. Spirit the essence that remains eternally identical with itself, maintaining its integrity amidst the ceaseless flux of all its distinctions.

Before we confront the difficulty now presenting itself to us, let us first relate Nature and Spirit to the metaphysical concepts we previously discussed; perhaps in this way, we may pave the way toward resolving that contradiction. As for this relationship, it is evident that individuality pertains to Nature, while universality pertains to Spirit. In Nature, the universal divine Idea is always individualized; in Spirit, every individual distinction is always immersed in the simple ideality of the Universal. And if we previously proceeded from the premise that Time is the element of Spirit, we now know that it is so only as total Time—as the universal concept thereof; in a word, as Eternity—whereas Nature, precisely because it belongs to the spatial externality of Space, thereby also belongs to the mutually external moments of Time—that is, to Time in its individuation and finitude. Nature perishes in the particular and endures in the abstract universal, whereas only Spirit lives eternally. Yet were Spirit—as the Eternal and Universal—to constitute merely one side of the temporal and particular nature of the natural world, it would thereby itself become something finite and limited—in a word, a merely existent distinction. Spirit, therefore, transcends Nature; it is both itself and its Other, and only thereby is it truly infinite. It is at this point that we must take our bearings if we wish to comprehend the nature of the finite Spirit—and the necessity of its emergence from the Infinite—and thereby resolve the difficulties that hinder the proper determination of its concept.

For since Spirit is that which is all-encompassing, all-pervading, and universal, it constitutes the immanent essence of Nature—whose innermost core is, indeed, the divine thought. Viewed from the perspective of Nature, the latter stands in need of precisely this higher foundation; for, being finite, it would disintegrate and dissolve within itself were it to lack this support. Yet the relationship of Spirit to Nature—like every one of its determinations—is not a static, merely existent state; rather, if we conceive the substance of Spirit as its activity, then this activity consists in constantly projecting the finitude of natural distinctions out of itself, so that it may constantly idealize them and constantly reabsorb them back into itself. However, the mere negation of the external separateness of Nature would constitute a one-sidedness just as profound as that which would result were its distinctions left to subsist solely in their lifeless inertia. Thus, only the uninterrupted process oscillating between these two extremes constitutes the true life of Spirit. Now, since all determinations of Spirit are distinctions of Spirit itself—or distinctions posited by Spirit itself—Spirit, in its relationship to Nature, posits Nature’s distinctions as its own; and in doing so, it posits itself as a natural or finite entity—though it does so solely for the purpose of subsequently sublating this very determination once again.

From this, the nature of the finite spirit now follows. It is the ceaseless appearance, the becoming-conscious or breaking-through of the infinite spirit out of and within Nature as the perpetual redemption of Nature. In the birth of every human being, the blossom of Nature bursts forth into the flame of the Spirit. Every conception within the womb of the mother is a begetting of the spirit—a seed sown within Nature—so that from it, the seeds of eternity may sprout forth. Man is the spirit to the extent that as he arises out of Nature, proceeds from it, and is bound to it. But in man's current state, he is not yet truly spirit, for he still lacks freedom. However, this freedom *must* be lacking to the spirit at the outset, for it lies in the very concept of freedom not merely to *be*, but to *bring itself forth*. If freedom not to bring itself forth in time through its own activity—were it instead given immediately by some *other*—then it would, by definition, not be freedom at all. The finite spirit is therefore a necessary point of passage, enabling the spirit—through the sublation of its own naturalness—to liberate itself into infinity.

It becomes evident from this that the finitude of the spirit is not a fixed determination of its essence, but merely serves as an impetus—one which the spirit gives to itself—so that, through its own activity, it may elevate itself to eternity and thereby come to truly possess it. In itself—this perfect dominion of Thought over Nature—infinity is present even at the very beginning, though because it has not yet attained to consciousness, it has not yet stepped into reality. Instead, this perfected realization of the spirit's ideality must be articulated as the ultimate end of its journey. Thus, the contradiction inherent in the finitude of the spirit is resolved. For precisely because man is the activity of idealizing, he must perceive this very contradiction—this limit—within himself in order to subsequently transcend it, but this activity can only be developed in time.

Following this derivation of the finite spirit, we must now briefly outline its moments—moments, however, which we must not conceive as fixed determinations of that spirit, but rather view merely as so many stages through which it passes in order to attain its ultimate liberation and flow into the realm of the infinite spirit. The spirit, in rendering itself into nature, appears within nature precisely as that universal bond of necessity—or as the divine Idea, which, within nature, merely loses itself. This spirit we may term the World-Soul, which holds the entirety of nature together and animates the process of its life. Within this universal soul of nature, all of nature’s forms are contained as ideal entities; and, likewise, it is from this soul that they emerge—for nature cannot sustain itself. Yet we must not conceive of this World-Soul as an abstract universal existing apart from the individual entities of nature; for to do so would be to reduce it once again to something entirely limited. Nor, conversely, does it—in its universal aspect—attain to consciousness within any single, individual entity of nature; for within nature, everything appears under the form of individuality.

However, if the spirit is the constant idealization of the external differences of nature, then it cannot leave the spatial separation of nature as it is; rather, the activity of the spirit on nature can only consist in the gradual elimination of this externality of nature, and thus nature itself being increasingly raised up to the spirit. But since each of these transformations of nature must again become an existing difference, the series of these forms, which represents a gradual spiritualization of nature, cannot fall within time, but rather its members must exist side by side in space. Initially, the bond between these forms, which fall apart in space, is only their external relationship in motion: in the solar system, each member still has a complete material independence, which is not at all negated by their spatial relationship to one another. In the organism, as the highest branch of nature, all matter itself is drawn into the process and united to the ideal point of unity; life consists precisely in the fact that each member is absorbed by the others and sacrificed for them, while in doing so preserving itself and becoming an end in itself.

This point of unity of the animal organism, in which all the differences of the material body have been eliminated, is what we call the soul, the original and active principle of the organic body, which is formative for it. In the soul of the individual organism, the universal world soul has attained its highest realization within nature, thus its adequate existence, insofar as nature can offer such a thing at all, since it is precisely the divine idea in the form of otherness. This soul, which is the bond of the body, just as the world soul is that of the entire universe, is the lowest stage of finite spirit. Animate matter, unlike inorganic matter, is not determined solely from without, but develops from an inner principle. Since the essential determination of nature is thus suspended within it, it shows the first manifestation of spirit. But this spiritual point of unity is also still entirely dependent on and bound to matter, in that it only reveals the ideality of matter within its existence.

If the soul is that aspect of the spirit which is bound to nature, then at least this much is clear: that the spirit—insofar as it is merely soulful—cannot be immortal. For this soul is, in essence, nothing other than that which sustains the processes of nutrition and life; and since plants possess only this mode of soul, it would never occur to anyone to regard the individual form of a plant as immortal once it has withered away.

This soul—being still entirely immersed in nature—is, by its very nature, a singular entity, conditioned by the matter of which it is the form. Admittedly, the form—as that which is common to all individuals of the same species—constitutes the universal Idea of ​​that species, an Idea which maintains its self-identity within every single individual. Yet this formative principle can only realize itself within this matter to the extent that the matter itself does not prove resistant to it. Thus, depending on the specific nature of the matter, it will be shaped differently by this form-giving activity. However, the diversity of the matter itself stems from the fact that it already possessed a different form prior to being seized by the formative principle we call the soul; for matter that is utterly formless is a mere impossibility. Here we see the principle of individuation—that which makes every individual spirit what it is—emerging from nature. The universal World-Soul is the form that remains constant throughout all things—the archetype of nature—which, in the very act of realizing itself within matter, differentiates itself *ad infinitum* across a multitude of manifestations, driven by the diversity of material distinctions. The principle of individuation is, therefore, a principle belonging to nature, insofar as it establishes specific spiritual distinctions through the medium of natural differences.

The final stage of this principle of individuation is the individual itself. Yet, from the universal World-Soul down to the single individual, there exist many gradations of more general or more particular distinctions—distinctions which, to be sure, no more exist in and of themselves as independent entities than does the universal World-Soul; rather, as natural distinctions, they attain actuality only within the individuals. However, even if they are indeed merely distinctions inherent in the individual itself, they cannot constitute its ultimate determination, since the individual shares them in common with many other individuals. The most general natural distinctions of the Earth serve as the underlying basis for the spiritual distinctions among individuals. Within each continent, climatic differences—or differences in soil composition, and so forth—then shape the distinct character of the various peoples, the tribes within a given people, and these constitute the *local spirits*. Finally, the distinction of sex presents a general principle of individuation applicable under every clime. The individual participates in all these various distinctions, it is these very distinctions that cause the individual to differ from others—even if, in doing so, it still shares commonalities with many others. Yet all these determinations of the individual— established as they are by nature—are bound to the individual's physical body; consequently, they perish and vanish along with the passing of that natural existence.

Ultimately, however, the individual has also received from nature specific capabilities and aptitudes—talents, genius—as well as idiosyncrasies, quite particular sympathies and antipathies, and so forth; these now lend him his ultimate distinctiveness vis-à-vis other individuals and appear to complete the principle of individuation. All these elements constitute the mortal aspect of the individual. The best proof of this is provided by those specific traits of the individual which he gradually sheds even during his lifetime: thus, from birth to death, he traverses a succession of stages, passing from one to the next—permanently casting off the earlier stage and being unable ever to revert to it. These are the ages of life, wherein the various temperaments reappear—though now as a succession unfolding in time. The child, for instance, evidently possesses the sanguine, pleasure-seeking character and lives in tranquil unity with nature. With the advent of youth, the struggle of life begins—yet initially only as an *ideal* struggle; for the youth, much like the melancholic character, withdraws into himself and, in contrast to the actual world, constructs an ideal world—a product of his education and, simultaneously, the germ of the new reality destined to take the place of the old. The mature man embodies the choleric temperament, for he now engages in the active pursuits of life, taking his driving thought as the guiding principle of his actions—actions which, by directing the full force of their energy against the world, continuously reshape and transform it. The old man, finally—whose activity ceases because the opposition to the actual world, from which that activity had flowed, has become blunted—returns to unity with the world, yet does so through tranquil contemplation, much like the phlegmatic character. But as for those elements of corporeality that the soul has already cast off during life—how could these stages ever re-emerge within it, and that, moreover, after the complete decay of that very corporeality?

Yet if all this colorful diversity of individuality perishes with death, the question arises: what, then, of it is immortal? At this juncture, it might easily appear that the answer must be purely negative. Or is the principle of individuation not yet fully realized here? And must we look elsewhere—to something in which, perhaps, the eternal aspect of individuality might be contained? To this end, we must turn to the higher activities of the soul—those spheres where it ceases to possess merely a natural determination and to be solely dependent upon nature. Foremost among these are all cognitive activities, by virtue of which the soul—acting as the *I*—can liberate itself from every particular content and thus ascend into the ether of pure universality. In this capacity, it is Spirit; and even if the soul should perish along with the body, the question nonetheless remains whether the Spirit, too, shares this same fate as the physical frame.

It is at this point that Göschel and others have taken their cue, attempting to salvage individual persistence solely by this path—for they believed, admittedly, that they were compelled to abandon those aspects of the soul mentioned hitherto. Yet, even among these higher activities, there are several that remain intertwined with the body and thus appear destined to share its fate. To this category, I assign primarily the faculty of sensation. In sensation, the soul is indeed no longer a mere aspect of nature; the "I-ness" has cast off every vestige of entanglement in such natural determinacy. Nevertheless, the content of sensation remains a specific natural determinacy—albeit one now assimilated into the ideality of the spirit—with which the spirit henceforth knows itself to be immediately identical. The spirit does, to be sure, freely reproduce this content within its own interior, such that it subsequently appears as its own self-determination; yet, despite this freedom, the spirit remains dependent in the highest degree, for it is capable of sensation only when the object of sensation is actually present. Thus, even within the act of sensation, the spirit remains related to—and determined by—nature; consequently, with the cessation of natural existence, sensation likewise ceases for the soul, and we are therefore unable to ascribe the attribute of immortality to sensation.

Consequently, the question posed in a poem by Klopstock—whether animals possess immortal souls—must strike us as highly objectionable and prosaic. Just as the organic principle of life, which exists in isolation in plants, does not extend beyond the existence of that specific organism, neither does the capacity for sensation—which is superadded in animals—since sensation is bound to the organs of the body. And we are compelled to acknowledge this all the more readily given that, in accordance with the varying organization of sensory organs across the diverse genera of living beings, their sensations, too, must necessarily differ. Within this varying organization of sensory organs lies a new principle of individuation; yet this principle—originating from matter just as its predecessors do—can lay claim to no higher destiny than those aspects of individuality previously alluded to.

Furthermore, it follows from this that everything connected with sensation—and which must be traced back to it as its foundation—such as imagination, memory, and so forth, also perishes along with the body. For since memory is merely the reawakening of sensory images when new ones flow in upon me, and imagination is the generalization of these into sensory representations, how could these faculties function once the sensory aspect of the individual has died away?

While one may readily accept that theoretical sensation passes away, one clings—particularly in our own times—all the more fervently to feeling, or the inner heart; one asserts that this constitutes the eternal, the imperishable element that transcends all the vicissitudes of the earthly and the sensory. Religion, morality—indeed, everything of a higher nature that man possesses—is said to stem from feeling, to have sprung from that higher homeland to which man must ultimately return, taking these very qualities with him.

In this way, feeling is merely the form through which I adapt a content—one common to all—to my own particularity and render it consonant with it; indeed, any content whatsoever—the lowest no less than the highest—can assume this form (precisely because my own individuality acts as the judge here) and, within it, assert its own irrefutability. For inasmuch as feeling does not engage with reasons or arguments, it has, as such, immediately become something absolutely steadfast—without, however, ceasing to be something utterly groundless. Thus, the so-called faculty of feeling, too, does not originate from the higher homeland of the intelligible world, but rather clings to earthly nature—whose destiny is, therefore, its own as well.

If, then, the entire individual thus dissolves for us as something transient, the only enduring element within the finite spirit is that which, in the case of natural things, already constituted their immutable substance. This element is, however, *thinking*—which, in the human spirit, does not—as it does in nature—congeal into an external, objective form. For while nature invariably presents the ideas of things—or their genera—only in an isolated manner, in human thought this activity of the Universal attains to consciousness. The *I*, as the Universal, takes as its object only that which is itself universally self-identical—namely, the ideas of things—and in this act of contemplation, it is entirely devoid of individuality. Everyone must think the very same thing regarding the very same object; and insofar as individuals think differently about it, they have thought incorrectly—or rather, not at all—for their thoughts remain intermingled with the turbid matter of their sensuous sensations and representations, which they have not yet been able to cast off. In the act of thinking, however, the spirit is meant to liberate itself completely from this sensuous aspect, to dwell entirely within itself, and to bring to light nothing but its own substance.

Thinking is, therefore, not a property inhering in a sensible substrate—as is the case with natural objects, or even with man insofar as he is merely a soul—rather it constitutes the true substance of the spirit. If we previously termed it an activity, we now know that the ceaseless activity of thinking constitutes the sole substance of the spirit. The spirit is not, as it were, the substrate of this activity—a static entity to which this activity adhered—but thinking itself is the eternal, substantial bond that holds together the fleeting moments of individuality within life. For the finite spirit, to be sure, the body serves as the substrate of this activity, precisely because it is those bodily distinctions that thinking interlinks within this individual. However, since this connection is dissolved in death, death leaves nothing of man remaining save that which is the pure energy of thinking itself.

It follows from this that every thought flowing purely from this inner source is an eternal one; and that whatever humanity has acquired through thought remains an imperishable treasure—one that is by no means lost along with the corporeal individuality of the person who first conceived it, but rather, however belatedly, penetrates the minds of contemporaries, is transmitted from generation to generation, and bears manifold fruits in every newly unfolding spirit. Since thought still finds the anthropological basis of its genesis in the individual brain, it is true that the capacity to generate thoughts at all—and, more specifically, thoughts of a particular nature—depends upon the organization of that brain. Yet once it has emerged—like Minerva from the head of Jupiter, springing forth from this blossom of corporeal organization—thought casts off every vestige of earthly limitation and dependence, to work henceforth, unimpeded, within the realm of the spirit. Every religion, and every moral or political community of a people, rests upon thoughts that have shaped that people—thoughts which, through their subsequent transformation, will guide another people to a higher stage of culture. In philosophy, this thought finds its expression in its highest purity; and within the historical systems of philosophy, it constitutes those eternal truths that represent the supreme treasure of the human spirit.

If we now consider this activity of thinking in its highest abstraction—without yet reflecting upon its particular content—then we no longer have the *I* as identical with sensation, with representation, and so forth; rather, we have the *I* that is identical only with itself, or that constitutes universal thinking. As this Universal—which has only the Universal as its object—the *I* has overcome every element of foreignness; it has thus arrived at the pure concept of freedom, which, at the beginning of this lecture, we articulated as the attribute of Spirit in general, in contradistinction to Nature. The Spirit that wills only itself—or its own freedom—is the free will; and this freedom, contemplating itself as an existent reality, is what we call *personality*. Thinking—and the personality immediately bound up with it—is, therefore, the truly eternal element within the human spirit; yet, for this very reason, it is also that by which one individual does *not* distinguish himself from another. Rather, to the extent that every individual wills freedom, he wills the Other as a person, and seeks to behold his own freedom reflected in that Other. The concept of personality is, therefore, the same for all human beings; and for this reason—as has already been stated—all human beings are equal before the law. This personality constitutes the Universal—that which maintains itself within all individuals and remains independent of their particular individuality.

However, this immutable essence of the spirit resides in man—as a finite spirit—within the individuality of each person; and thus, man is a true person only insofar as he individualizes the universal concept of personality within himself. Every person, therefore, possesses a particular content by which he distinguishes himself from other persons; yet, equally, he possesses thoughts and moral actions in which only the universal spirit of his time expresses itself. Thus, the eternal and the transient aspects of human personality are inseparably bound together. To detach one from the other would be to reduce it to a purely abstract entity. The universal equality of persons—devoid of fulfillment and content—would constitute a completely empty concept of freedom, possessing no real existence. To cling solely to the individual aspect of personality would be to relegate the spirit back to the level of the soul.

Those deeds and thoughts flowing from the individuality of man—which do not belong to his finite and transient aspect, but are rather true moments in the realization of the ethical Idea of ​​the human spirit—such deeds and thoughts as possess an enduring existence in world history, constitute the true substance of the individual. These deeds and thoughts distinguish the individual from other, equally transient individuals, yet simultaneously relate him to the eternal life of the Spirit, inasmuch as they are necessary determinations of the substantial thought that is identical in all individuals. Even at this highest pinnacle of the finite spirit, however, the principle of individuation is not abandoned; rather, the individual is the series of his deeds and thoughts, and upon the content of these depends his worth or lack thereof. Yet we wrongly continue to ascribe these deeds to individuality as such; they belong, rather, to the universal treasury of the Spirit—which merely distributes itself variously among the different individuals, and makes their own freedom the very ground of its divine revelations.

The distinction between individuality and personality, therefore, lies in the fact that the former signifies those modifications of the individual spirit which it draws from the earthly source of natural differences. However, everything that the individual—in contradistinction to others—appropriates as his own from the universal realm of the intelligible—whether by thinking a thought for the first time, or even merely by reflecting upon it, or by performing a moral deed, and so forth—belongs to his personality. Yet this appropriation is entirely free; for while that which comes to pass is indeed necessary, its author has nonetheless always embraced it through free choice—since, in this very act of choosing, he manifests his personality as that which distinguishes him from others. Individuality and personality thus both pertain to the singularity of the spirit; yet the former constitutes its lowest stage of development and manifestation—namely, the "This," or sensuous singularity—which, consequently, vanishes with the cessation of its natural conditions. Personality, conversely, is the form of singularity befitting the spirit—that which constitutes solely the realization of the universal, and which, therefore—without enduring as a sensuous "This"—lives on nonetheless within the universal, infinite spirit.

Having hereby fully developed the concept of the finite spirit, we may simultaneously draw the conclusion that, in the aggregate, individuality is indeed mortal, but personality is immortal; for the latter constitutes the individual entity insofar as it has transformed itself into a mere moment of the Universal. The finite spirit has, therefore, attained within it its goal—the infinite spirit—to which we must now accordingly proceed. By virtue of this attainment of its goal, the individual human personality does not, in fact, stand in opposition to the universal spirit; rather, since it is itself merely a manifestation thereof, we are thereby naturally directed toward the necessity of expounding the concept of the infinite spirit—that very infinite richness from which human individuals draw their own particular personality. Now, since one part of our task concerns the personality of God—and since we have thus far identified the concept of personality solely as the culmination of the concept of the finite spirit—the question now arises as to the nature of the relationship between this human spirit and the divine; in a word, what, more precisely, constitutes the nature of the divine Being. This shall be the subject of our investigations in our next gathering; only in this way can we hope to fully resolve the problem of divine personality.

## The Divine Spirit

From what has been established thus far, it is clear that if the Divine is to be ascribed one of the two predicates—individuality or personality—that we attributed to the finite spirit in our recent reflections, this can at most be personality, but certainly not individuality. For the individuality of the spirit is constituted by all the specific determinations that derive from its natural constitution that arises with that Nature and perishes along with it. Would it not be utterly absurd and ridiculous to assign God to a specific race, gender, or to ascribe to Him this or that specific character, set of aptitudes, talents, and so forth? All these specific conditions which constitute the human individual, by means of which one individual is distinguished from another, therefore cannot apply to God. This is all the less conceivable given that such conditions would serve to separate Him from all other spirits—a separation which would constitute the clearest possible proof of His finitude. For that alone is finite which is defined in relation to something *other* than itself as its boundary—that which possesses specific qualitative determinations by means of which it is excluded from this "other," just as, by means of those same determinations, it simultaneously excludes that "other" from itself. 

The Greek gods, however, are individualities; for, beyond the spiritual power they represent, they are also bound to a specific natural determination—Jupiter to the ether, Juno to the air, Neptune to the sea, and so forth. Thus, it is entirely consistent that they, too, are subject to sexual differentiation. 

In monotheism, however—owing to the infinity of the divine essence contained therein—individuality is, by the very nature of the matter, excluded from the concept of God. For "individuum" is the Latin translation of what Greek philosophy termed the "atom"—that is, something that cannot be further divided within itself, something to which the stark category of *discretion* (discreteness)—as opposed to *continuity*—applies. The atom is an indivisible unity that repels every other unity from itself, and is likewise repelled by every other.

This category is, therefore, far from the defining characteristic of the *finite* spirit. Because the finite spirit—through its natural body—has itself become spatially distinct from all other finite spirits, and thus stands in resistance to them, egoism is the very quality with which Nature initiates the activity of every finite spirit. And only in that the dross of this finitude gradually begins to melt beneath the sun of the *infinite* spirit does the noble metal of love arise within the finite spirit. In friendship, for instance, the finite spirit perceives its own self as expanded into another self. This continuity among finite spirits is, in fact, an active force implanted within them by the infinite spirit; for the latter constitutes not merely the absolute ideality of all natural distinctions. Instead, since even finite spirits become individualities that mutually exclude one another only through natural determinations—God is, at the same time, the absolute continuity of spirits, in which all their particularities have been shattered and set into flux.

Following this, if we apply a contrast previously examined—that between individuality and universality—to the relationship of the finite spirit to the infinite spirit, we must conclude: the infinite spirit is the universal spirit precisely because it permeates the boundaries of individuality by which individual spirits are separated from one another. However, as that which permeates the boundaries of finitude, it does not reside *beyond* the individuals—for indeed, for the divine spirit, no "beyond" can exist at all. Instead, it constitutes the individuality-less essence hidden within every individual—that enduring universal element which persists even amidst the dissolution of that specific individuality. It follows from this that God is not an "I" standing opposed to another "I," such that He might distinguish Himself as an "I" from a "Thou," or that all finite spirits might similarly distinguish themselves from Him as a "Thou." For this very distinction belongs, after all, solely to individuals.

Now, since this proposition appears to emerge directly from the principles of philosophy, it is precisely upon this point that the penchant for distortion—characteristic of Leo and of those who incited him to take his public stand—has chiefly fastened itself, branding this assertion as one of the most pernicious consequences of so-called Hegelian pantheism. Let us, therefore, examine this proposition more closely, in order thereby to pave the way—proceeding from this negative conceptual determination of God (namely, that He is not an individual)—toward a positive definition of the Divine.

To begin with, however, the concept of the "I" is in itself nothing that distinguishes one finite spirit from another. Each is an "I," and in doing so, asserts absolutely the same thing about itself as all the others. Yet, by the same token, each is also a "Thou"—that is to say, simply the "Other" to the one who, in any given instance, calls himself "I"; whereby, therefore, each individual becomes a "Thou" whenever another calls himself "I." "I" and "Thou" are thus entirely relative concepts, through which individuals distinguish themselves from one another; such that each is an "Other" to all the others—and all are thus "Others" to one another—meaning that, viewed from the formal perspective, each has become simply and absolutely the very same thing as all the others. Distinctions can arise only from the particular content with which these "I"s are filled; and since this filling can be established only through the natural differences of individuality, it is clear that God—in this sense, at least—cannot say "I" of Himself. Thus much, then, is certain: that God is not a particular individual who has been constituted as such by this or that natural content.

Therefore, there remains no alternative but to say that God is the *universal* "I". However, while this could easily be the entirely correct solution to this difficulty, it cannot yet be immediately demonstrated, since the universality of the "I" is itself open to interpretation. For the moment, we can only state that the universal "I" must not be posited as existing *beyond* the individual "I"s. For were this "beyond" a spatial distinction separating it from them, then the "I"—which purports to be universal—would itself be merely a singular, spatial, even corporeal entity. God, however—as the infinite Spirit—has cast off and transcended all sensuous distinctions, all spatial externality. On the other hand, were the transcendence of the universal Self not understood in a spatial sense, it would be nothing more than the abstract concept of selfhood—a concept we form within our own minds which, although present in all individual selves, would nevertheless simultaneously fall *beyond* them, were one to conceive of this abstract notion independently of those individual selves. Such a concept, however, would be something utterly unreal—and thus equally unworthy of God.

Without here undertaking an investigation into the extent to which the universal I might be understood in a third sense—namely, as the universal I realizing itself within all individual Is—which would, indeed, constitute the true mode of apprehension, we must first address the preliminary question: does the very concept of selfhood—considered generally and in itself, regardless of how we might conceive of its realization in any specific instance—not fundamentally contradict the infinity of the divine Spirit? This does, in fact, appear to be the case. For when we utter the word "I," we distinguish ourselves not merely—nor even primarily—from other Is (which, in this context, we designate as "Thou"), but rather from everything that is not an I at all; that is, from all natural things—a category that includes the sensory manifestations of all other Is, or their bodies, within which we have not yet recognized the presence of selfhood. All such entities—everythingthat does not belong to the rigid atomism of this "I" of ours—we may, employing a Fichtean turn of phrase, designate as the "Non-I." While the activity of our I admittedly consists in constantly assimilating this Non-I—drawing it into the realm of our theoretical representations or practical ends—we must, nonetheless, also acknowledge it as a fixed object that imposes an insurmountable boundary upon our I.

Consciousness is this very standpoint regarding the finitude of spirit—a finitude that resides in the mutual limitation of the I and the Non-I. From this it follows immediately from this that consciousness cannot be ascribed to God. For consciousness presupposes a separation between subject and object—a separation that is entirely absent from the divine essence. Consciousness is commonly regarded as the highest stage of spirit, and is thus elevated to the status of an indispensable attribute of God. Yet even Göschel—who, among the three stages of Hegel’s doctrine of subjective spirit, views only the third stage (Spirit) as the eternal and thus divine aspect of man—assigns the first two stages (the Soul and Consciousness) to the realm of finitude, and must therefore exclude them from God. If he subsequently maintains that these lower stages are preserved as "moments" within the higher stage, it will indeed become evident to us in due course to what extent this assertion is entirely accurate. We must, however, deny that consciousness *as such* constitutes part of the inner substance of the divine essence—for this would imply the existence of a substantial Non-I standing in opposition to it. At most, it could only manifest within the divine essence as an *accident* pertaining solely to the mode of its appearance.

If the I now reflects upon itself as the opposition to the Non-I from which it turns away, then we arrive at self-consciousness. Therefore, self-consciousness is impossible without the consciousness of an object distinct from itself, and if we had grounds to exclude the consciousness of God, we must by the same token deny Him self-consciousness—for in possessing it, He would necessarily perceive Himself as something limited by an object, whereas, as pure consciousness, He would merely be bounded. Nor does it avail to attempt to remove the limitation inherent in self-consciousness by positing the objects confronting it as likewise being conscious entities—that is, as merely other instances of self-consciousness. On the contrary, the barrier that another self-consciousness erects against an I is far more rigid and impenetrable than the sheer objectivity of a non-sentient natural entity. For every self-consciousness, asserting its egoism against the other, seeks to realize its own ends at the expense of the other—and thereby to destroy the other’s ends, indeed, the other itself. It is only when, through love, one self passes over into the other—when one sacrifices itself for the other, and the identity of the individual self-consciousnesses is established—that the barrier of the finite spirit begins to dissolve. As this love—which constitutes the continuity of finite spirits—God is the substance of the individual self-consciousnesses. We now grasp with greater profundity why, in our initial reflections, we characterized God as the bond uniting all spirits—that medium in which all spirits constitute a single Spirit and strive toward a common goal. As much as we recently termed this identity of all individual particularities personality, the question now arises in what sense personality may be ascribed to God.

Spirit—which no longer finds its limit in the Other, but within the Other, becomes conscious of its own freedom and wills the freedom of all—has indeed, in this respect, abandoned the standpoint of finitude and naturalness upon which we previously observed human individuality to stand. Through personality, man takes flight into the infinite. For insofar as all men are equal before God and the Law, they have cast off the particular individuality that clung to them by nature, and have ascended into the sphere of pure spirituality—a sphere they hold in common with the Divine Essence. Personality is the bridge across which finite Spirit is led into communion with the infinite Spirit, it serves as the *medius terminus* between the two. But precisely for this reason, it still partakes of both extremes and cannot purely represent the nature of either one exclusively. To attain the highest stage of spirituality, we must therefore conceive of personality itself in a modified form. To this end, however, we must—above all else—delineate its own inherent distinctions. For only in this way shall we be able to distill its pure concept applicable to God as the infinite Spirit.

In personality, the individual or finite spirit already ceases to be merely a subjective spirit—one that, with its drives, inclinations, representations, desires, and thoughts, would remain confined within the circle of its own interiority. The person is a will that views its freedom as a thing—initially as the person’s own organic body, and subsequently, in a general sense, within the entire sphere of its property. In the same manner, the person acknowledges the existence of freedom in other persons, for the person’s own existence of freedom is secured only through the existence of freedom in all other persons. This concept of personality is that of the *legal person*, from which all other meanings of the term derive. Here, spirit has assumed an objective form; it transcends nature and transforms it into a fluid distinction of itself. Through his property, man appears as the master of nature—one who is entitled to subject its forces to his will and utilize them for his own ends. Thus begins the process by which Right dissolves the finitude of the spirit; for as a property-owner, man becomes akin to the infinite Spirit—to whom, after all, nature is so utterly subordinate that, from His creative "Let there be," nature appearing into reality as a divine thought, and continues to appear eternally.

However, the legal personality has not yet been able to cast off its individuality and singularity, and has thus not yet become adequate to the divine spirit. The proud master of nature has been able to extend this dominion over only a very small portion of it—over his own organic body, or his person, and the things that happen to be his property at any given moment. All other things, and the persons of other human beings, remain for him something sacred and inviolable—something to which, as this particular sensuous person, he possesses absolutely no rights or claims. Persons are, much like spatial points—absolutely discrete atoms, and the limitations of nature, which man imagined he had escaped through his entry into the spiritual realm of Right, reappear in all their severity, and by virtue of their admixture of spirituality, appear all the more unyielding and legitimate. Individuality renders this person a distinct individual, for because the person possesses this specific character, these particular aptitudes, and so forth, he thereby objectifies himself in his body, and secures for himself within the state this specific civil status. The legal existence of the person is limited by nature the person ceases to be a subject of rights in death and becomes—as a corpse—a mere thing, the property of the family. Yet even in life, the existence of my freedom remains bound to the existence of the things in which I choose, at any given moment, to invest it.

In this sense, then, we shall not wish to ascribe a personality to God either. Just as little as He is a soul individualized by natural determinations—which would distinguish Him from others—is He a proprietor who demarcates the sphere of His "Mine" from the "Thine" of all others, enters into contracts with them, and so forth.

However, the individual is not merely objective in relation to the sensuous objects of nature, rather the wellspring of inner vitality bubbles forth within him, and he constitutes—as we saw in the previous lecture—the activity of bringing the eternal Idea of ​​Spirit, in and for itself, to manifestation: that is, of shaping humanity in the image of eternal Reason. This constitutes moral action. Because Spirit possesses absolute freedom—and is therefore not complete at its inception, but must rather, through its own activity, elevate itself to become what it is destined to be—it is itself engaged in perpetual activity. We find the supreme manifestation of this activity—which realizes the ultimate purpose of humanity—in World History, which, for its part, advances through the progressive development of political life among the various nations. Spirit does not seek to realize itself merely as a sensuous, isolated individual—as the proprietor of its own private freedom. Instead, as much as every individual recognizes themselves as identical in their innermost essence with every other—notwithstanding their external differences—and as much as the Spirit of the Nation comes to consciousness within them as the very substance of all individuals, so does each individual strive, on the one hand, to give the purest expression to this Spirit of the whole within themselves, and on the other, to execute the will of this universal National Spirit most vigorously through their deeds. The best constitution is, therefore, that in which individuals are granted the greatest scope for their activity—in which they are free to move with the utmost liberty and to contribute, as fully as possible, to the realization of this universal will, but conversely, it is also that constitution in which this universal will encounters no impediment to its development in the self-interest of individuals, but finds in all citizens—notwithstanding their supreme individual freedom—willing instruments for its realization. Thus, the moral act does not flow from the self-interest of the individual, but rather takes place in the service and for the well-being of the whole. 

The primary moral entity to which the individual dedicates their energies is the family, the individual state constitutes the higher sphere of their moral activity, and in the highest instance, this entity is humanity itself—whenever the influence of moral action extends beyond the boundaries of this natural national life. Whatever each individual, by virtue of this idea of ​​the Good and as a moral personality, has accomplished constitutes an imperishable treasure for the human race—one that, even should the name of its creator be lost to oblivion, lives on immortally in his works and bears fruit a thousandfold. This, we observed at the time, constitutes the distinctive personality of the human being. The thoughts of Plato and Aristotle; the invention of Gutenberg and Fust—the four-hundredth anniversary of which the current year celebrates; the politics and military arts of Hannibal, Alexander, Caesar, Napoleon, or Frederick—these are eternal springs of insight, instruction, and progress for all succeeding generations, and in these ideas, their personalities live on. Plato is no longer a mere corporeal individual—a "sensory this"—yet he remains, nonetheless, a distinct personage, clearly distinguishable from others within the realm of the spirit.

Even in this sense, personality cannot be ascribed to God, for He is, rather, the universal spiritual substance itself—the source from which individuals draw the deeds that secure for them the immortality of their own persons. God is neither this nor that specific moment which individuals appropriate for themselves. He is the totality of spiritual Being, within which all moral deeds of the spiritual realm are preserved as moments and resonate together in a single harmony. Thus, if the person is the free, active objectification of the spirit’s inner determinations—their unfolding into a realm of the intelligible—then God is indeed a person; for the infinite Spirit, as the ideality of all distinctions, encounters nowhere the slightest obstacle to its own self-realization. Yet God is not *this* person in contradistinction to *another*—one who might have brought to expression only this or that specific aspect of the spiritual sphere. Rather, just as the individual personality consists of the series of its own deeds and thoughts, so too is the infinite Spirit the entire scope of His works—that personality, therefore, which emerges from the whole of creation, breathes within it, and—despite the multiplicity of its forms—remains eternally true to itself.

This personality is, to be sure, not a *particular* personality, but rather the *universal* personality—one that does not exist *outside* of particular persons, but rather unfolds within every single individuality the very blossom of its higher selfhood. Whatever any individual accomplishes for eternity, they do so solely by virtue of the indwelling of this divine personality within them. All moral deeds are, therefore, divine deeds; "it is no longer I who act within myself," says Paul, "but God." In this, we may discern the concept of Grace given full expression. Yet, as much as this divine personality constitutes the core that forms the innermost essence of every individual, it can come to fruition within each one, and it is precisely human freedom—exercised through one’s own choice—that brings this grace into active effect.

But if God were merely this general person, He would be no person at all, and this is precisely what those unversed in philosophy reproach philosophy by calling it pantheism—in that God is held to be this static, lifeless generality, akin to the Spinozistic Substance or the Parmenidean One. Nothing could be further from the truth, and when the narrow intellect—or that piety which, despite the awakening of critical reflection, clings forcibly to the standpoint of faith—reproaches philosophy by claiming that its God is merely an empty abstraction and a hollow concept, they find themselves, as so often happens, committing the very error they impute to their hated adversary. For if were God a particular personality—a specific "I" supposedly hiding somewhere within the infinite reaches of space, perhaps on Sirius—then such an "I" would indeed be nothing but an empty mental image and an abstract concept.

God, as active and real, thus manifests Himself constantly. He does not remain—as a mere personal substance—a hidden Beyond, distinct from the works in which He reveals Himself. Instead, within those works, His entire personality is brought forth into the light of this-worldly life. "The ordinary conception of God," observes Schleiermacher in his *Speeches on Religion*, "as a solitary Being existing outside and behind the world, is not the 'All in All' for religion, is merely a mode of expression—rarely entirely pure, and always inadequate—by which religion attempts to articulate itself."

Therefore, if God is neither mere particularity nor universality—since the one is just as unreal as the other—then He is the unity of these two determinations. Specifically, not a *static* unity, for otherwise He would once again fall back exclusively onto one side. God is the eternal activity of constantly constituting Himself as a distinct personality—for every individual act requires all the conditions of finite individuality—within this limited individuality, of constantly manifesting Himself through moral action, and thereby either allowing the boundary of this individuality to persist as something indifferent or incidental, or—should necessity demand it—breaking through that boundary in order to bring the Idea to realization through the sacrifice of this particular mode of being. God is neither merely the individuality that is sacrificed, nor merely the Idea that sacrifices. He is the living interplay of these two aspects—the process of the absolutely Universal which, as expressed earlier, eternally sustains itself as the totality of its moments. Everything that is sacrificed through the activity of the divine Idea belongs itself solely to the finite, phenomenal aspect of that Idea. However, the Idea itself does not succumb to this sacrifice of finitude, but lives on within humanity—even after it has withdrawn itself from individuality.

Universality and individuality are thus inseparable moments of the divine personality. The Divine is the Universal manifesting itself in individual persons, or that which, within a particular individuality, corresponds to this Universal. God is thus an Idea that is constantly realizing itself, and in this reality finds an existence adequate to itself. The individual is imperfect, and thus God’s realization would seem to share in this imperfection. However, anything in the individual that bears the traces of finitude and imperfection does not count at all as an essential existence. Only that counts in the individual—and carries lasting weight—whereby the individual serves as a manifestation of the divine Idea. This aspect of the human personality is its eternal element, for it is that whereby the personality is true to its own Concept. God is the eternal personality of man—of all men—the true Concept of personality that comes to manifestation in all individuals to a greater or lesser degree, and which—in whichever individuals it may realize itself—always remains the self-identical, universal, eternal Idea. Thus, God is the absolute personality that constantly personifies itself in individuals, and which, for this very reason, can neither be exhausted nor adequately appear in any one person.

As this unity of universality and particularity—which are thus superseded as moments within a third, such that there are no longer truly three entities, but are within the three, only the one process of self-moving divine life is present—God may now be articulated as Triune. God is not an individual person who can say "I" in opposition to another "Thou", rather He is—as Novalis puts it—the universal *I*, in which all individual "I"s come to a knowledge of the identity of their essence. Only now can the third sense of the universal *I*—which we previously asserted categorically—be clearly elucidated. For the universal *I*—into which God has matured—no longer faces a "Non-I" or any other consciousness standing over against it, since even the atomistic persons have been rendered fluid within the divine essence through love. God is the universal Idea—the unreal Absolute—which, in manifesting itself within the *actual* person, duplicates itself, and thereby stands as Father in relation to His Son. This Person is the coming-to-consciousness of the universal Idea, and precisely by relinquishing its own particularity, it simultaneously supersedes the simple mode of objectivity inherent in consciousness, and recognizes its substance as one with this universal Thought. Thus, this Thought becomes the Spirit, for in the Holy Spirit, the absolute ideality of all distinctions is posited, and the retraction of finite personality back into the infinite essence is consummated. But this must also be grasped as a return of God to Himself, and only in this return—as the Third—is He the true God.

As much as the Trinity is thus merely this movement of the conscious individual—emerging from the universal divine thought and transforming itself into Spirit—consciousness is indeed a moment within the divine essence, a point we likewise sought to demonstrate earlier. However, consciousness must also have elevated itself into Spirit, and thus it can continue to exist only within Spirit, insofar as it has transfigured itself into the universal Self. We therefore agree entirely with Göschel that the individual is immortal only as Spirit—not as soul or mere consciousness—for only as Spirit has it become a moment within the process of the divine life.

In light of this, if God is personal, He is nevertheless not *one* single Person, and Theism—which asserts this, and accuses the philosophy of antheism of heresy for denying it—thereby, for its part, overturns the fundamental dogma of Christianity. For within that dogma, there are three Persons—the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit—which coalesce into one single Essence. "Very well," benevolent men—those more favorably disposed toward philosophy—might then object to us: "Christianity teaches a Trinity—Father, Son, and Spirit: *These three, who bear witness in heaven, are one*, whereas you teach a *Multiplicity*, because every conscious person is a realization of the divine Idea and—through the manifestation of the Eternal within themselves—is one with the divine Spirit." Now that we have rid ourselves of our enemies, it will not be difficult to reach an understanding with our friends.

To begin with, I do not see many entities that seek to merge with one another—but only three: first, the abstractly universal divine Thought, second, the particular human personality, and third, the Holy Spirit, understood as the consciousness of the Idea living within the individual. Admittedly, this second moment is fragmented into an infinite multiplicity, but this is the very nature of particularity and finitude in general.

Furthermore, these three individuals possess no real existence in and of themselves. To the extent that their natural individuality is concerned, they are distinct entities, but this aspect is of absolutely no relevance here. Instead, they count only insofar as they serve as instruments for the realization of the one divine Personality, and in this regard, the fount of divine grace is open to everyone in equal measure. The particular human personality—which is the subject of discussion here as the second moment within the Trinity—is itself merely the *Idea* of this personality, not any actual, concrete person. It is the Primordial Man, the *Adam Kadmon*, the Archangel, or whatever name one might choose to give it. Were we to attempt to reduce this second moment to a mere sensuous particularity, we would be attempting to merge entities that are utterly incommensurable, for what we are dealing with here are three Ideas that move within one another and together constitute the process of divine life.

Admittedly, a process existing merely as an idea would constitute yet another abstraction—the converse extreme to the one into which we would fall were we to view God—the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit—as three distinct individuals dwelling like separate bodies in heaven (a notion Jacob Böhme already rejects), relating to one another as "I" and "Thou," and conversing with one another as they do in Klopstock’s *Messiah*. The activity of the triune Being—through which divine substantiality manifests itself within human nature and lives on eternally as Spirit—is, of course, replicated in every single individual. Everywhere, according to Böhme, is "the wellspring of the Holy Trinity; and the three Persons are born within *your* heart." Notwithstanding this infinite multiplication, the process within each individual remains, nonetheless, a triune one; and this is the sole meaning we can philosophically ascribe to the dogma of the Trinity.

Thus, this entire movement does not unfold solely in heaven—within the eternal Idea—but descends to earth and assumes flesh and blood. In other words, the doctrine of the Incarnation represents the actualization of the Trinity. Consequently, in the latter—the Trinity—we have not yet fully exhausted the concept of God’s personality, but have merely posited its abstract notion. The Incarnation, then, constitutes that aspect wherein God’s personality appears as an actual human person. This introduces an entirely new perspective, and the question here is this: Did a *single* human individual—solely and exclusively—give expression to the infinite fullness of every moment of the divine thought? And was this process a singular event—occurring but once in time, never to be repeated? Or is this process, too—in reality just as much as in the realm of ideas—entirely universal and eternal? This constitutes the innermost core of this doctrine—a point that requires clarification, which we shall attempt to provide in our next meeting through the exposition of a Christology. For it is clear that the doctrine of the personality of God can attain its full completion only once we have philosophically examined the nature of the Person of Christ.
