## The Justice System

As we encounter private law once again here, we must nevertheless view it from a perspective entirely different from the first part of the philosophy of law. We are no longer concerned with the exposition of individual freedom per se, with the assertion of personality as an ultimate end. Instead, private law has now become for us something social, a bond that unifies individuals together, just as the necessity of labor did earlier. Thus, even if one still hears the maxim "Fiat justitia, pereat mundus" (Let justice be done, though the world perish) proclaimed here and there in connection with private law, it now serves, on the contrary, to preserve the world. Having become a means to well-being, property now exists primarily for the sake of its use, for it is precisely through its use that we satisfy our needs. Indeed, justice itself constitutes the satisfaction of a need, namely, the need for justice. It differs from the satisfaction of other needs only in that the latter pertains to the specific aspects of individual well-being, whereas law establishes the general possibility of such satisfaction for all, and thus represents the true basis of the social drives. It is the justice system that first confers upon persons and property the protection and security without which the individual is deprived from the means to attain well-being. 

Nevertheless, even law must be deemed of a higher order than the economic objective to which it is intended to lead us. For within the sphere of law, we no longer conceive ourselves purely as beings who hunger or desire in a general sense, but always as free persons, individuals in whom the existence of the general will forms the basis for the specific content of our various drives. The superiority of this standpoint over the one we just left behind lies in the fact that previously, conflicting personal interests were only held together unconsciously by an internal bond of necessity, whereas now, the requisite consciousness of this interconnection is fully explicit. While the economic sphere, the needs of individuals generated sociality only implicitly, a process in which the self-interest of one was simultaneously and repeatedly infringed upon by the self-interest of another, the sphere of law, acting as the dispassionate arbiter between the conflicting parties, pacifies the will that stands in stark opposition to the will of another. The law achieves this by leading each party to recognize the necessary limits imposed upon each individual, limits that exist, under the condition of reciprocity, so that no one may inflict harm upon another.

This discovery now imparts a fundamentally different character to the law. Since the source of the law is no longer merely from pure reason, but also from the needs of citizens who actually interact with one another, it follows that positive law is not, in this context, an eternal and immutable law. Instead, the law changes in step with changing needs, assuming ever-new forms across different climates, nations, and eras, amidst the rise of luxury and culture. Yet by no means does this imply that the demand has been abandoned that the law should correspond ever more closely to the dictates of reason. On the contrary, precisely by ceasing to be only possibly rational, its rationality becomes truly actual. Indeed, the law becomes actual precisely by being transformed into something demanded and established by the public welfare, into a valid standard acknowledged by all within a given political order. This is the first point that must considered by the justice system. However, on its own, the law is merely an unreal universality, so it secondly requires an active agent to realize it. The law thus comes to life in the judge, who is in a sense, the guarantor of that realization, and not as of yet, the actual enforcement and execution of the law. That the judge, as the very source of the efficacy of law, must give concrete expression to the mere potentiality of the law, is thirdly the judicial process that arises whenever a right has been infringed.

### Legal Code

#### Customary Law

Although it will always remain essential to the well-being of states that what is established by law be simultaneously the existence of right in-itself, it is a matter of contingency in any given instance whether or not this correspondence actually exists. It is for this reason that no philosophical determination can be discerned regarding the content of positive law. That content falls within the scope of the philosophy of world history, whereas the task here is simply to develop the necessary forms under which the specific content of any given era in world history manifests for the end of protecting and fostering the system of human needs. In this regard, the standpoint here is that law is essentially customary law. For the true source of this law is not reason descending from the heavens, but from the actual needs of the people themselves. Thus, this law indeed grows upward gradually from below. The manifold legal determinations that arise among different peoples from the diversity of their customs are nevertheless reducible to a single fundamental principle that constitutes precisely what is meant by the spirit of their laws. This customary law is "unwritten law" in a sense that it is neither the product of the dialectical unfolding of reason, nor the result of the deliberate reflection of a legislator, but arises as an expression of the natural drive of a people directed towards their own well-being.

#### Statutory Law

Just as language germinates from below out of the immediate life of the people and becomes codified into firm, written principles, so too is customary law destined to be codified. This marks the transition of customary law into statutory law, the written law that records and brings to consciousness what had initially lived only unconsciously through custom. But through this process of becoming conscious, the prevailing law does not cease to be customary. The law must be a possession of the people, one that is inherent to all members. However, as juristship continues to shape customary law, then it is precisely through this exclusively academic treatment that it becomes something external to the people. Therefore, the people must become their own legislators, just as they were the originators of their customary law. The law must be the product of all, the expression of the general will. Laws must not be enacted for the purpose of granting advantages to one social class over another; rather—when custom and habit no longer suffice to uphold, amidst the complex interplay of needs, those legal norms established since time immemorial. When individuals violate custom and acting out of impulse to satisfy their own drives, it is then that the community as a whole brings to conscious awareness what has already long been valid in order to transform it into an external bond that binds those who would violate it. Only when the law is violated is the statute enacted, but the law existed long before it became a statute.

On the one hand, if law becomes an object external to the individual by virtue of being a written statute, then on the other hand, the legislator’s endeavor must be to ensure that it does not lose its character as something unwritten and rooted in the spirit of the individuals even as he renders it accessible to their understanding. such that this objective law becomes, for them as well, a subject of their own consciousness. Accordingly, the primary requirement of a statute is its promulgation, a process bound to a solemn form upon which the validity of the law depends, without that form itself becoming the substance of the law. Just as this holds true for statutes in general, so too does it apply to specific legal transactions. Contracts, testamentary declarations, and marriage vows must—in order to be recognized by society—likewise be couched in such solemn forms. However, according to Hegel, the promulgation of laws also entails their ready accessibility, and so they must neither be hung so high that the citizens cannot read them, nor be completely written in a foreign language.

#### Civil Law

The legislator’s highest aim, however, consists neither in creating a new body of law, nor merely in confirming existing custom, but in increasingly adapting and aligning both customary and statutory law with the demands of rational law. For although a large portion of the law currently in force remains entirely positive in nature (i.e. the application of the law to the individual case, and everything that flows from the specific circumstances of a people), elements of natural law can shine through to a greater or lesser extent. This occurs when the content of law is elevated, through an appropriate structural arrangement, to accord with the form of its concept. For even if customary law exists initially in an unformed manner, is subsequently collected and committed to writing, this process does not by itself eliminate the chaotic, disordered nature of its form. It is futile to attempt to impose order upon this chaotic jumble by enacting a new statute as a remedy. Such legislative amendments just serve to further augment the body of customary law, for while a new written form may be given, the underlying content remains devoid of structural form. The inherent rationality of the subject matter demands, rather, that the boundless material of existing positive law be distilled, through a legal code grounded in scientific order, back to the general principles of law. Just as Justinian subsumed imperial decrees and scholarly rulings under general legal titles, so too has codification done the most to foster the elevation of positive law to rational law. However, the legal code must not elevate a multitude of individual cases to the status of general principles, and thus, purely for the sake of completeness, make it more difficult to master the informal subject matter of customary law. In this respect, the Code Napoléon stands far higher, for despite its moderate scope, it comes closest to the ideal of rationality precisely because it reduces diverse material to simple principles.

While the one hand, the evolution of customary law periodically demands a new legal code, and the existing legal code guides the further development of customary law on the other hand, it becomes evident that the form of rationality that the legal code has imparted to customary law will increasingly improve the rationality of its content as well. This progressive approximation of positive law to rational law initially manifests itself in the very form of custom that gains precedence over statutory law. When a statute remains in force, yet the custom of the people has already rendered it obsolete, this contradiction is initially resolved through a more lenient practice, through a milder interpretation of the law. It is not only judges who, in this manner, continually act as legislators, the people themselves do so by circumventing the laws until the contradiction is finally resolved, and a new statute becomes the expression of a people's higher legal consciousness. Therefore, all law originates from the customs of a people, initially in an unwritten form, only to then acquire objectivity through written codification. 

It is from this constant interplay between written and unwritten law that the mutable nature of the legal code is explained, and in this process, standing in contrast to the eternal immutability of natural law, the true character of positive law is fully realized. For since the legal code, as the intermediate ground between the two forms of unwritten law (custom and reason), not only imbues customary law with greater rationality but is itself rendered ever more rational by the influence of that very custom, the legal code serves a nation as the mirror of its character and the surest indicator of its level of cultural development. This holds true to an even greater degree for the penal code than for the civil code, as the rules of the latter tend to be more enduring.

### Court

Now that statutory law has taken the place of natural law, the individual who believes their rights to have been violated can no longer depend on the standard of unwritten law within their own conscience. The state of nature, in which everyone uses violence or rejects the violence imposed upon them with violence, and in which law has no recognized existence, has given way to the social state in which individuals have recognized certain general legal norms in law to satisfy their needs and to protect their person and property. In place of self-interest and vengeance as means to atone for civil wrongs and crimes, there must step an impassionate authority: one that, unlike the disputing parties, holds no vested interest in the matter at hand, and that will, in lieu of the arbitrary action that exceeds all bounds, render the calm and dispassionate judgment of the law. This law come to life, this justice imbued with spirit, is the court. Through a new division of labor, the court it attends to the administration of justice just as the other professions devote themselves to the satisfaction of humanity’s other needs, and is likewise subject to mutual exchange. While the vocation of administering justice must indeed appear as the specific task of members of that profession, the task of justice is the common good, so it effects and demands the participation of all other professions. Justice is applied to every sphere of civil activity, and the proper determination of what constitutes justice requires precise knowledge of the specific domain that is in question.

#### Adjudication

In considering the activity of administering justice, if we look first at the relationship between those who seek justice and those who dispense it, i.e. the nature of jurisdiction itself, we find that it resolves into two components: the right of the individual whose right has been violated to receive protection from the court, and the duty of the court to grant them this protection. However, since right and duty no longer diverge in this context, it follows that the litigant not only has the right but also the duty to seek the vindication of their disputed claim exclusively from the court, and to renounce all recourse to private vengeance. Conversely, the court not only has the duty to protect the individual, but also the right to exercise its jurisdiction. Yet having established in the same context that right and duty are simultaneously united with the highest form of moral freedom, we are confronted with a major question: How can the court’s authority over the individual be reconciled with their self-governance and freedom, the principles which we have posited as our supreme guiding tenets?

This difficulty is further compounded if we evaluate the matter purely in light of the political economic concepts we have previously established. For if justice is a need, the satisfaction of which every individual is free to procure for themselves or obtained through another party, provided only that the latter is willing to render such services, then the court should not to compel anyone to seek the vindication of their rights exclusively through its own agency. However, by asserting this proposition, we do not by any means intend to suggest that blood vengeance or private feuds are a better or more ideal. This was merely because the authority of the courts was neither recognized nor sufficiently powerful to protect the individual who, in the absence of general protection, had to depend upon their own resources. However, if amidst stable social conditions, an individual fails to acknowledge the authority of the courts, then they would incur reproach for seeking to subvert justice. Even if society willingly permitted this course of action, it would naturally be compelled to withdraw from them the protection of their person and property provided by the courts. At that point, this person would very quickly calculate, simply from an economic perspective, that it would cost them far more to maintain private guards to protect his person and property than it would to willingly embrace the protection provided for them by the public. On the other hand, they would also be acting inconsistently, for having once entered into the social compact, they ought to recognize that the justice system constitutes a moral activity by which the principle of communal engagement with others, embraced as a freely assumed duty, is the very expression of the rights of the individual. 

#### Jurists

To derive the composition of the court entirely from the concept of the matter, we must examine more closely the professions who exercise the function of adjudication. The class of jurists evidently belongs to the class of scholars, to the extent that the scientific knowledge of the law is the means that qualifies the person for the exercise of their profession.

The primary function of the legal scholar is that, if someone wants to know what is legal in order to safeguard their rights and consults a legal scholar, then the latter should assist them with his counsel. Therefore, the legal scholar is first and foremost a legal counselor (juris consultus). Furthermore, when contracts are concluded, marriages are entered into, or when last wills and testaments are to be drafted, it is crucial to ensure that all the formalities required by the applicable law are met. Thus, the parties voluntarily call upon an expert to draft the document for them, ensuring that it complies with all formal requirements. These constitute acts of "voluntary jurisdiction," which are executed before notaries without their execution being strictly mandatory. Conversely, in the case of other legal transactions, such as the sale of immovable property or the mortgaging of land, a formal judicial proceeding constitutes an essential part of the formalities of the transaction. Finally, should a legal dispute arise, a party—who does not wish to appear in person, and who believes they can better vindicate their rights through the agency of a legal scholar can, by means of a contract of agency, authorize such a legal scholar to appear in court on their behalf, i.e. the attorney.

Judges differ from the types of jurists mentioned thus far solely in that they are not selected by chance to serve as legal counsel for this or that specific individual in any given case. Instead, they are designated in advance as adjudicators for all who wish to settle their legal disputes in the court of law. It goes without saying here that geographical proximity constitutes the most natural basis for this association. It is likewise self-evident that preference will be given to jurists who have already established their reputation through years of providing legal counsel. Because of this, it is entirely appropriate for a young legal scholar to begin their career at the bar, thereby making the practice of law a proving ground for the judicial office. However, in order to safeguard the independence and impartiality of the judge in the most complete manner possible, the most rational approach is to ensure that neither the state nor the general populace, but that the free association of jurists elects from their own ranks the individuals whom they deem most worthy to hold judicial office. Hence, the principle of judicial irremovability would be made necessary under the method of selection just proposed. A judge who has proven themselves capable would certainly be re-elected by their peers, but should this not occur, they would always be free to return to their practice as a lawyer and notary.

Furthermore, since in the case of criminal offenses in general, the additional consideration of the danger posed to the community as a whole comes into play, it is thereby justified that major crimes are not brought before a judge for adjudication solely through the complaints of private individuals (as is the case with civil wrongs). Instead, a public prosecutor steps forward to hold the criminal accountable in the name of the entire society that the criminal has wronged through their deed. However, this arrangement must not preclude the right of private individuals to file complaints, should the public prosecutor happen to decline to press charges, a contingency that is far less to be feared when the prosecutor is an elected official than when they are an appointee.

While up to this point, we have dealt exclusively with jurists in the task of determining the law in matters of case law, the application of the law to all spheres of labor often gives rise to a situation in which the judge, lacking insight into the specific fields of labor, is unable to administer justice adequately. In such instances, they must either summon assessors, acting as expert witnesses, for each individual case to provide their professional opinions on the matter, thereby enabling them to render a decision with full technical competence. Alternatively, should such cases recur frequently, it is even more appropriate to appoint experts as permanent assessors and members of the court. These constitute the specialized courts—such as mining courts, industrial courts, commercial courts, courts-martial, and the like. In the past, under the feudal system, peasants were compelled to accept their estate lord as their judicial authority, and there still persist are attempts to reinstate the traditional, historical estate owner as the de jure police authority of the village, where such a specialized court differs fundamentally from those previously mentioned. This form of patrimonial jurisdiction extends to the entirety of common law, encompassing both civil and criminal matters. The judicial lord, even if they are required to appoint a bailiff to act on their behalf, nevertheless remains the judge in their own affairs. A hereditary judicial lord, one not chosen through the free election of those who seek justice, stands in absolute contradiction to the very concept of a free constitution.

#### Venue

Given the diversity of courts and matters to be adjudicated, the question ultimately arises as to what particular court that a particular case must be brought before. This is not always immediately obvious at first glance, and so we must here address the issue of proper venue (forum). It goes without saying that while the person of the accused is certainly factor to be considered when determining jurisdiction, it cannot be the only determining element, as is the case with the so-called "personal venue", which typically also entails a privileged status. This completely runs counter to the fundamental principle of the equality of all citizens before the law. For this reason, a person may not, by virtue of their status alone, be summoned before special courts in matters governed by the general law, they must seek justice before the ordinary courts like everyone else. There are exceptions of course, for example, if a specific military offense (falling under the jurisdiction of military courts) is to be adjudicated. Finally, no state official can claim any special venue. The rule that the venue of the defendant rather than that of the plaintiff should be the decisive factor finds its justification in the fact that the legal inquiry centers upon the action performed by the former, the lawfulness or unlawfulness of which is to be adjudicated. 

As for the substantive grounds of jurisdiction, the decision is straightforward when the defendant's domicile and place of birth coincide. However, if the forum domicilii differs from the forum originis, then priority must undoubtedly be accorded to the former, to the extent that the act in question must be adjudicated in the place where the defendant currently resides. But if the defendant neither committed the act at their domicile or place of birth, nor was apprehended there, then the forum delicti commissi prevails, i.e. the court of the place where a crime was committed, a contract concluded, etc. Where multiple parties are involved, their common venue can be established only through the unity of the act committed, given that it is not feasible to summon every guilty party before each of their respective domestic courts. Secondly, crimes and legal transactions must necessarily be adjudicated in accordance with the laws of the country in which they were consummated, such that the place of the deed becomes the place of atonement. This principle also explains the application of the forum rei sitae in disputes concerning immovable property. As for the objection that a defendant possesses a right to be judged according to the laws of their own domicile, rather than those of the country in which he happened to be sojourning, the proper rejoinder is that one is bound to submit to the laws of the country in which one currently located. Should the question then arise as to which authority is empowered to resolve such conflicts of jurisdiction, the answer must be the very court before which the matter has been brought. Thus, the primary inquiry for any court is whether it possesses the requisite jurisdiction. Just as the court is guided solely by its own discretion, so too does the authority to render a legally binding determination on this preliminary inquiry reside with that same court.

### Rules of Procedure

The union of the judge with the legal code, as the union of pure activity with pure possibility, is the judicial process as prescribed by the rules of procedure. Just as an individual right, such as a contract, becomes one recognized by all only through the form to which it is bound (precisely because both judge and parties can readily identify its content by means of that form), so too must the contested or violated right to be restored be recognized by its form. Before the court, right acquires the character of having to be provable, so the judicial process places the parties in a position to furnish this proof. If I have a right that I am unable to prove, then my right is unable to be recognized by others. It lies in the very nature of the subject matter that right, if it is to be valid for all, must secure this recognition through the observance of legal forms.

#### Court of Equity

Since the rules of procedure prescribed by law, as forms that remain subordinate to their substantive content (the principal matter), the parties must be permitted, before embarking upon the course of formal litigation, to vindicate their rights by a more expeditious means. Such an institution is a court of equity, though not in the sense that legality is meant to yield to equity. The true purpose of this abbreviated procedure is to ensure that the substantive content of a legal right is not sacrificed to its form. An initial attempt must be made to determine whether the parties have attained their just due, whether through a mutual settlement or through the legal judgment of a justice of the peace in whom they have placed their trust, thereby saving time and money. Even if the parties are required to pay the justice of the peace, it goes without saying that the brevity of the proceeding significantly reduces its overall cost. Moreover, since it is generally only services that are compensated, it follows naturally that judges of every kind are remunerated through fees (*sportulae*), and much like lawyers, do not receive a fixed salary. The requirement that parties first appear before a justice of the peace could even be a mandatory duty, or at the least, whenever one of the parties requests it. Nevertheless, such a requirement by no means constitutes a waiver of the right to pursue formal legal proceedings. However, the provisional enforcement of a judgment could be justified by the fact that it is crucial to determine who is to be placed in possession, for it would be inequitable for a party who has already secured a favorable judgment to be deprived of possession until the matter is finally resolved. Nevertheless, the arbitrator must not be selected by the parties themselves, for to allow this would make arbitrariness the sole decisive factor in the justice system. Instead, just as the legal scholars of a specific municipality elect the officers of the court, the members of a specific community elect their justice of the peace.

#### Legal Procedure

As for the formal legal proceedings themselves, should the justice of the peace fail to bring about a settlement, or should a party (or parties) remain dissatisfied with their decision, the initial question arises: who is the plaintiff responsible for bringing the matter before the ordinary courts and calling the guilty party, as the defendant, to account? In a civil dispute, the matter concerns only the injured party. Since they are at liberty to waive their rights, the judge has no duty *ex officio* to investigate whether or not an injustice has actually occurred. For as much as the injured party may, in relation to the wrongdoer, waive the very right that has been infringed upon, no actual wrong is deemed to exist so long as the plaintiff does not lodge a complaint. This condition evidently necessitates an *accusatorial* process for civil disputes, a system that has, for this very reason, been favored by nations for such matters. However, since a crime affects *everyone* by its very nature, the right to prosecute cannot be restricted to the injured party (which would be impossible if the victim was murdered), nor to their relatives who constitute the legal extension of their person. Instead, reason demands that *anyone* be empowered to bring such charges. Criminal cases are, as the Romans termed them, *actiones populares*. Alternatively, the court itself may intervene *ex officio*, in which case it would be an *inquisitorial* process. Situated between these two extremes, whereby either any member of the public may prosecute, or only the judge himself is authorized to initiate proceedings, lies the intermediate institution of the *public prosecutor* or *state attorney*. The institution of the public prosecutor's office is evidently the superior arrangement because a system where any individual from the populace can bring an accusation, arbitrariness can never be entirely ruled out, since any and all people can simply choose to refrain from acting. In the inquisitorial process, the judge acts simultaneously as the accuser, and thus may easily be led to investigate into existence precisely what they have already come to regard as suspicious. By contrast, with a public prosecutor elected by the people, all elements of arbitrariness and contingency are eliminated. On the one hand, the public prosecutor cannot pursue the prosecution all the way to the final verdict, and on the other hand, it does not even rest with them to decide whether the accusation is actually to be formally pursued. Nor may it be left to the court that pronounces the verdict to decide whether to accept the accusation, for otherwise, the functions of accusation and judgment would once again be concentrated in one person. Instead, a jury must first deem the accusation to be well-founded before either the public prosecutor or a citizen is authorized to present it before the adjudicating court.

However, even if both civil litigation and criminal proceedings are accusatorial in this sense, the inquisitorial aspect of both cannot be overlooked. In civil litigation, this aspect can come into play during the course of the proceedings if the plaintiff, or their counsel, misconstrues the underlying legal relationship. To ensure that the court’s legal assistance does not become futile, and that the substance of the law is not sacrificed to form, the court has the duty to adopt a different interpretation of the relationship, one distinct from that advanced by the plaintiff, and to make that interpretation the ground truth. It is a shortcoming of English judicial procedure that this option is denied to the judge, for as a consequence of this, even if the judge is totally convinced of the plaintiff’s entitlement on grounds other than those pleaded, they are nonetheless compelled to dismiss the claim should the specific legal ground advanced by the plaintiff prove untenable. In criminal law, however, this inquisitorial element is all the more self-evident, given that the offender will naturally have a vested interest in obscuring the true legal nature of the situation in order to evade punishment. Thus, it is inconsistent with the very concept of criminal justice that, in ancient Rome, certain offenses were classified as *crimina privata* (private crimes), which could only be brought before a judge for adjudication by the injured party themselves. There were four such offenses, and of these, we can regard this procedural restriction as justified only in the case of *injuria* (personal insult), an offense which is not prosecuted *ex officio* (by the state), unless the insult is directed against a public official, in which case the state deems itself to have been offended. Therefore, it is entirely consistent that in ancient Greece, any insult directed against a citizen was regarded as a *causa popularis* (public cause) since every citizen was a constituent member of the sovereign body, and any insult directed against an individual citizen thus immediately assumed the character of *lèse-majesté* (an offense against the sovereign). 

After making these preliminary remarks regarding the initiation of legal proceedings, we find that the moments of legal procedure constitutes a complete logical syllogism in the first figure (I-P-U). First, the task is to present the case under adjudication, viewed as a pure matter of fact, in terms of its legal significance (the minor premise: I-P). Secondly, the case must be identified with the applicable law (the major premise: P-U). Finally, the law must be applied to the case at hand (the conclusion: I-U). The three terms of the syllogism, any two of which forms a judgment, are: the individual facts of the case (I), the particular legal nature of the case (P), and the universal law applied to the case (U).

##### Instruction / Inquisition

The first moment of legal procedure is determination of the facts, which is termed *instruction* in civil litigation and *inquisition* in criminal proceedings. This terminology serves to demonstrate that, in criminal proceedings, the judge conducts the investigation independently, whereas in civil litigation, they proceeds primarily on the basis of the parties' motions, even though they are permitted to refine their legal arguments through well-intentioned advice or formal rulings. This initial stage of the legal process is itself subdivided into three distinct aspects:

The first of the aspects is the unilateral assertion made by the plaintiff through the filing of a statement of claim, known as the *information*, or alternatively, the bill of indictment, if it is submitted by the public prosecutor. In jurisdictions where a purely inquisitorial process prevails, this initial phase of criminal proceedings is designated as *denunciation*. In this context, the accuser assumes a role more akin to that of a witness, even if they are the injured party, and is required to swear an oath regarding their report, explicitly acknowledging that the personal interest they undoubtedly hold in the matter must not be permitted to induce them to testify contrary to the truth. The accused must then undergo a general interrogation, which constitutes a mandatory prerequisite before they can be formally placed on trial.

The second aspect, specifically termed *instruction* or *inquisition*, consists of the adversarial proceedings, wherein the defendant files a response to the complaint. This phase involves the exchange of a *replication* and a *rejoinder*, meaning that both parties present their conflicting legal arguments and claims of title before the judge. In criminal proceedings, a parallel process occurs during the specific interrogation of the accused, who is at this juncture referred to as the *inquisitus*, during which they are examined for the purpose of offering a detailed defense against the allegations set forth in the bill of indictment, either personally or through legal counsel. The conclusion of the adversarial proceeding is reached when each party has presented all points, both legal and factual, that serve to support their legal position. The judge notes the legal points provided by each side as material for their future judgment. Regarding the factual points, it must be precisely established what is admitted and what is contested as evidence. Thus, the conclusion of this second phase is the presentation of the factual situation (*status causæ et controversiæ*), so that the proceedings may now advance to the determination of the facts of the case.

Thirdly, this constitutes the evidentiary proceeding. Anyone who puts forward an assertion that the opponent is in denial is under the obligation to prove this fact, whereas mere denial naturally requires no proof (*neganti nulla probatio*). For this reason, for example, the possessor holds the advantage over the non-possessor, for they need only deny the right of the other party, whereas the non-possessor must prove their own right. Likewise, the public prosecutor must prove whatever the accused does not voluntarily admit, and as long as this proof has not been furnished, the accused is entitled to claim the presumption of innocence (*quilibet præsumitur bonus, donec probetur contrarium*). The means of proof to be employed must follow from the nature of what is to be proven. Since the matter at hand is not scientific truth, but is factual certainty, the truth bears only the meaning of the correspondence between my personal conviction and a sensory object, which can never lay claim to the character of unconditional objectivity and universality. Depending on whether the means of proof leans more to the subjective side or the objective side, or succeeds in bringing both sides into unity, they are themselves more or less accurate.

##### Verdict

The second moment of the court's activity is the verdict, the major premise (P-U) whereby the fact, having been reduced to its particular legal significance (P), is now subsumed under the universal law (U). In the first instance, the verdict no longer concerns itself with the facts particular to the case, but is rather a completely general pronouncement, which can only then be applied to the factual circumstances. This was, for example, the case in Roman procedure, where the court, much like the logician, began its verdict with the major premise rather than the minor premise. The Praetor presupposed the facts without investigation, and having first set forth the subsumption under the law, he called upon the judge (who in this capacity was effectively a juror) to pronounce an affirmative or negative minor premise depending on the findings regarding the facts: *si paret*, if the fact is proven true, to *condemna* (condemn), or *sin minus*, if the fact is not proven true, to *absolvito* (acquit). In criminal proceedings, if the factual circumstances are in accordance with the law, then no crime exists, and so the verdict that results is one of acquittal. Conversely, if the act does not correspond to the law, the verdict is negative, for the penalty prescribed by law for the crime is thereby linked to it within the major premise. In civil litigation, the prevailing party is the one whose legal title is found to conform to the law. However, the court may also render a split verdict, granting partial relief to each party based on their respective legal titles. While in criminal law, no third option seems possible between the guilt or innocence of the accused, every specific fact has such a particular nature that it entails a greater or lesser degree of culpability. From this lies the dependence of the major premise upon the minor premise, in as much as the judge must choose a penalty that falls somewhere between the maximum and minimum measures of punishment that the law establishes as its outer limits. These considerations, which prevail when applying the general law and are termed "mitigating circumstances", belong to the minor premise when we inquire into their existence, but when we consider their influence upon the specific measure of punishment, which is left to the discretion of the judge, they pertain to the major premise.

##### Enforcement

The final judgment, the third moment of the legal proceeding, which constitutes the execution of the law in the individual case (I-U),must once again be determined by a person entirely distinct from the one who rendered the verdict, for it is through this separation that the liberty of the citizens will be best safeguarded. In a civil dispute, if the parties do not voluntarily submit to the judgment, a situation arises demonstrating that legality is essentially coercive. Though this does not alter the truth of Rousseau’s assertion that he who is thus coerced is compelled to be free, to the exten that the law, which prevails over his arbitrary will, constitutes his true freedom. In this context, the enforcers may be subordinate officials, but only their superior, however, must necessarily be a jurist. Even at this stage, the matter still revolves around legal considerations, i.e. the implementation of the rules of court procedure.

#### Trial

While the execution and maintenance of the legal process just described is the province of the legally trained judge, the citizens must nonetheless make it their own through their conviction and trust. To this end, it does not suffice that the citizens only has knowledge of the laws, simply submitting to this judicial order as an external, objective power acting upon them. Instead, just as they were able to bypass these formalities in order to obtain justice as their own right through an arbitrator of their own choosing via an expedited procedure, the further requirement is that, even within the formal legal process, their own active engagement and intellect, i.e. the right of self-consciousness, must be fully present. As Hegel aptly puts it, the citizen must not only have the right to appear physically before the court (*in judicio stare*), but to also be present with their inner disposition, knowledge and will (*ex animi sententia*).

##### Testimony

If the citizen is to be involved in the legal process at all, they must be involved in every aspect of the legal procedure. For while the subsumption of the case under the law is the judge’s true and proper function, the determination of the facts is strictly the business of the accused. Indeed, it would constitute a hardship, even a form of serfdom, if the establishment of the facts were to take place without the participation of the party concerned. Of what avail is it if two witnesses claim to be testifying to the truth? Might they not, notwithstanding their apparent trustworthiness, harbor a hidden interest in the matter to falsely incriminate the accused?

At first glance, this line of reasoning might lead one to deem a confession a prerequisite for the imposition of punishment. For it would seem to be the criminal’s most sacred right that no punishment can be delivered without a confession. The person who confesses consents to the punishment and acknowledges the law as their own. However, were the criminal’s confession truly indispensable, any guilty party could evade punishment simply by persisting in a stubborn denial, despite all cross-examinations and the most irrefutable evidence. Thus, in cases where full proof exists, a confession can be regarded as immaterial. Strictly speaking, if there is full proof, then the full penalty should be imposed regardless. The only remaining question is whether, in the absence of a confession, due regard can be paid to the right of self-consciousness through other means, a consideration that becomes imperative when only circumstantial evidence is available. On the other hand, in the absence of any other evidence, a confession alone is insufficient to warrant the execution of a punishment. We are indeed willing to accept the maxim: *volenti non fit injuria* (no injury is done to one who consents). Nevertheless, it is a substantive requirement of the law that injustice must not occur, and that the punishment of an innocent person who confesses constitutes a miscarriage of justice no less than its execution against an innocent person accused by two witnesses. Therefore, it would indeed be preferable to require full evidentiary proof and a confession if it were not possible to satisfy the right of self-consciousness in some other manner.

Since it must be the judge’s constant endeavor to fully uphold the right of self-consciousness, to ensure that the confession remains linked to objective evidence, only from this correspondence does the highest degree of certain arise. To this end, in uncultured times, judges did not hesitate to employ unnatural means to force  confession when no unforced ones were at their disposal, and the cruder the state of the people, the more unnatural were the means employed. Thus, if the objectivity of factual evidence, provided by documents, witnesses, or the judge’s own inspection, was lacking, then, in their own perplexity during times of simple faith (such as ancient India and no less than in the Middle Ages), people initially entrusted the decision to a higher spiritual power, one reaching beyond mere visual observation and personal experience, and which seemed to bridge the gap between objectivity and subjectivity were the so-called *ordeals* (Judgments of God). Whoever was sewn into a sack and did not sink, whoever could grasp a glowing iron without suffering burns, whoever emerged victorious from a judicial duel — such a person was deemed innocent. In the case of the judicial duel, it was be argued that the consciousness of one’s own innocence, bolstered by faith in that higher power, instilled in the victor the necessary courage to win. In barbaric times, Montesquieu observes, where virtue was synonymous with valor, the hardened skin of the hands, itself being a consequence of such valor, would tip the scales of victory in favor of the brave man over the coward, by ensuring that the glowing iron left his hand uninjured. It is exceedingly obvious how unreliable these forms of proof are, for every conceivable argument becomes nonsense.

Since this substitute for objective evidence proved to be extremely deficient, attempts were made to coerce a confession through the use of torture. In Rome, only slaves were permitted to be subjected to torture, even when they did not testify against their masters. In the Middle Ages, however, anyone could be subjected to it. Moreover, the practice that a confession made during torture had to be repeated voluntarily in order for the confession to be valid is inhumane. Firstly, the victim of torture, their senses dulled by the agony they have endured, may confess to a crime they did not commit. Secondly, even if they retain their faculties amist being tortured, they are all the more likely to confess simply to escape the more severe degrees of torture that would inevitably be inflicted if they were to persist in their denial, a fate they would vastly prefer to the endurance of the penalty prescribed for the alleged offense.

When torture was abolished in the more enlightened centuries that followed, as Frederick II did upon his accession to the throne in 1740, and trial by ordeal had long since faded from the popular imagination, then what was to take its place? Even if a more refined judicial procedure allowed for the more reliable establishment of objective evidence, a confession could indeed be deemed superfluous in cases where full proof already existed. Furthermore, if one wished to rule out the death penalty, so as to avoid imposing any irrevocable punishment, then by opting for a revocable sentence, one could reasonably believe that the individual's right to vindication would be preserved to some extent through monetary compensation, should the innocence of the accused were to come to light. However, if only circumstantial evidence was available, a confession became essential to complete the case. And if such a confession was withheld, the authorities long resorted to forms of "indirect torture", since direct torture had been prohibited, employing such methods as starvation, beatings for obstinate denial, threats, and promises of leniency in exchange for a confession (promises that were rarely kept). Even when the steady advance of civilization eventually put an end to these practices, the judiciary adopted a dual approach, depending on the varying degrees of probability inherent to the circumstantial evidence available. If the probability of guilt was so slight that the judge was left with a prevailing conviction of the defendant's innocence, the court would issue a provisional acquittal due to insufficient evidence (*absolutio ab instantia*), a ruling that did not carry the weight of a final judgment, but allowed for the proceedings to be reopened at any moment without further formality. Conversely, if the judge’s conviction of guilt prevailed, they were nevertheless unwilling to let the accused go free, and so the right of self-consciousness seemed to be safeguarded by the imposition of an "extraordinary" penalty less than the customary one.

Yet, even these substitutes for a missing confession prove to be quite meager, if not worse. For a provisional acquittal leaves the Sword of Damocles perpetually hovering over the accused, whereas they have the right to demand a definitive declaration of their guilt or innocence, and if found guilty, to serve their sentence so that they may eventually return to society with their name cleared. The "extraordinary punishment", by explicitly pronouncing a guilty verdict guilty, does indeed satisfy this demand for a definitive resolution. However, even this guilty verdict is unjust with respect to objective justice, just as the provisional acquittal runs counter to the rights of the individual. For just as the individual is entitled to a declaration of absolute innocence, not merely a provisional one, which is a contradiction in terms, so too is society entitled to demand the full penalty once guilt has been established. Thus, the extraordinary punishment is unjust in both cases of innocence or guilt. Though, this punishment is at the very least consistent, on the condition that the only proof possible in this context is, by its very nature, neither a geometric nor a philosophical demonstration, nor an apodictic conclusion. It is always more or less uncertain and contingent, and must therefore be supplemented by the personal conviction of the judge. Indeed, even the standard requirement of full proof falls within the judge's purview to the extent as they are required to decide upon the credibility of a witness. Thus, it is a perfectly consistent to let a "half-proof" be followed by a "half-penalty." However, since both the "extraordinary penalty" (notwithstanding its logical consistency) and the "provisional acquittal" (due to its cruelty) fail to take into account the right of self-consciousness, the right to be present with one's full knowledge and volition, these expedients violate the principles of humanity.

One might argue in favor of this institution that judges, by virtue of their legal education, are by no means rendered incapable of deciding on matters of fact as well. It is argued that, on the contrary, precisely because of their legal education, they are able to do so with greater confidence, making juries superfluous. Nevertheless, due regard must be given to the demand of self-consciousness, namely that the law should not be imposed upon the individual by a narrow profession acting as the "barons of the law." Thus, Gans rightly opposed this newly emerging institution in Prussia the moment it came into existence, although it has since been rendered obsolete with the introduction of jury courts. This surely constitutes the clearest proof of how indispensable jury courts are, particularly in cases involving press offenses and political crimes, and that they simply cannot be dispensed with if the right of self-consciousness is to be fully satisfied. For in such matters, everything hinges upon conviction, and it is here that the people as such, rather than the learned judge, must decide for themselves what constitutes right and wrong, since these categories acquire their validity only through the medium of public opinion. Moreover, it is not difficult to recognize that juries, chosen from among the people rather than from the ranks of legal scholars, fully satisfies the dual requirements of objective proof and the right of self-consciousness, thereby providing a satisfactory resolution to this complex problem. For they furnish, through giving a guilty verdict of, a substantive confession. The jury speaks from the very soul of the accused, and once all the circumstantial evidence has been presented to them, having been unencumbered by the motive for concealment that the accused possesses, they internalize the deed in its entire objective reality as a matter of their own conviction.

If the accused has confessed fully, and if this confession is substantiated by sufficient circumstantial evidence, then the verdict is rendered without a jury. Yet the right of self-determination remains safeguarded even in such cases, provided that it is the accused’s peers, their equals in social standing, rather than the learned judge, who effectively makes this confession on their own behalf. Gans is entirely correct in deriving the institution of the jury from the oath-helpers rather than from that of the lay assessors or associate judges. Since an accused person was deemed innocent if twelve men within the community could be found who would swear to their innocence just as they did themselves. However, when I speak of "peers" or "equals in social standing," this should not be interpreted to mean that merchants, for instance, should be judged exclusively by merchants serving as jurors, and so forth for other professions. Instead, in civil and criminal cases governed by common law, and one fails to see why, in civil disputes as well, jurors should not be the ones to decide matters of fact, just as is actually the case in England. The true peer is the one who, like any other ordinary citizen, seeks justice, not the one who, as a legal scholar, determines it. As a result, legal scholars are not permitted to serve as jurors. In civil cases falling under the jurisdiction of specialized courts, it naturally follows that the expert assessors, drawn from a specific profession, will be peers of the defendant. In this context, specialized technical knowledge is indeed essential for the reliable ascertainment of the facts. Similarly, the courts of honor, serving as disciplinary tribunals for various professions, will naturally be constituted by members drawn from their respective associations.

Having established the logical basis of the jury system, as a necessary prerequisite for liberty, we need not concern ourselves greatly with adducing arguments on the basis of utility. Although, the most rational will invariably prove to be most useful as well. Thus, even if judges were theoretically capable of determining questions of fact with equal impartiality, this does not imply that the same capacity does not reside in jurors. On the contrary, jurors are free from the professional biases that are inherent to a strictly legalistic perspective. Moreover, the accused will likewise invariably perceive them as possessing a greater degree of impartiality. The most compelling proof that jurors do, in fact, possess this superior impartiality lies in the observation that governments, when acting as partisan entities seeking to forcibly maintain their power against the will of the majority, find it impossible to rely on juries in trials involving press offenses or political crimes. Such governments demand verdicts that align with their own partisan agenda, a demand to which judges, by virtue of their dependence on the government, are far more likely to accede, whereas jurors, drawn as they are from the ranks of the majority, will naturally give voice to the will of the people in their verdict. The fact that we previously advocated for the election of judges by their professional peers does not render jury courts superfluous, for judges do not represent the entire populace, but solely their own profession.

The last, irrefutable benefit of the jury system to be mentioned lies in the division of labor, a principle that serves to foster impartiality. However, the premise that the juror is to adjudicate solely on matters of fact, while the judge soley determines points of law, remains a highly contested issue. Indeed, in an effort to maintain a strict separation between these two spheres, jurors in France were reportedly even prohibited from consulting the legal code, such that they are unable to frame their verdict accordingly. If the jurors, along with answering matters of fact, also answer the legal nature of the act contained within its context in order to decide upon the existence or non-existence of such a legal concept, then in so doing, they also indirectly determine the punishment as well. Nevertheless, it depends on the manner in which the court frames its questions, whether the answers are intended to address merely the purely factual aspects or not. In many cases, it is not at all readily apparent what specific legal act the facts in question actually entail. However, the decision as to which law and measure of punishment are to be applied in any given case is always reserved to the court. Admittedly, to the extent that mitigating circumstances that determine the measure of punishment are affirmed or denied by the jurors, their verdict exerts considerable influence in this regard as well, just as the legally trained judge exercises the greatest influence upon the determination of the facts through the manner in which they frame their questions. 

##### Confidentiality

If free self-consciousness has now secured its right to be present with through the institution of the jury court, then this must now also come to pass with the subsumption of the individual case under the law. In order that the parties, the accused, and the entire populace may know whether the individual case is subsumed under the law, they must first witness the course of justice unfold before their very eyes by making the administration of justice a public matter. However, to the distinction that in the latter, the parties may, for the sake of their own interests, elect to exclude informing the public. However, in criminal proceedings, the pronouncement of the verdict always takes place in public, whereas the deliberations of the jury or the court, during which its members exchange their individual opinions before the majority arrives at a collective conclusion, must necessarily remain confidential as it strictly pertains to the individuals involved. Since only a few members of the public can be directly present as observers, the remainder of the populace will learn of the proceedings only through the press. There exists no valid reason why the publication of court proceedings should not be permitted to occur piecemeal, without the necessity of waiting until the final verdict has been rendered.

##### Closing Argument

The highest pinnacle of freedom in the justice system, whereby the entire course of legal proceedings becomes a matter of immediate consciousness for the self, is the closing argument. Written argument, it is argued, is more precise, secure, and therefore superior to oral argument, wherein there is always the chance of words being omitted or misinterpreted. However, unlike in antiquity, where presumably only the accusation itself was committed to writing (*graphē*), one must not rest content with oral arguments alone. Instead, in the modern context, everything that takes place during the preliminary investigation and during the actual judicial inquiry is first committed to paper: the interrogations, the confrontations, the witness testimonies, and the expert opinions. Through written argument, being necessarily confidential, the court acquires a completely clear overview of the entire sequence of events, and it cannot acquire such an overview in any other way. Yet it is imperative that the court possesses this overview *before* it can present the case to the public, acting as the audience, and to the jury, acting as the representatives of that public. Through the subsequent oral arguments, the deed itself is once again reconstructed in its entirety within the realm of mental representation. In a purely written procedure, the rapporteur must laboriously piece together the comprehensive picture from the individual records of the proceedings. They assimilate these records in a manner far less effective than one might fear would be the case if one or another of the jurors were to overlook a point during oral testimony. The adjudicating members of the court, who are unacquainted with the actual proceedings, remain bound by the specific interpretation adopted by the rapporteur. Even if a second rapporteur is appointed to ensure greater security, one is ultimately left with no choice but to decide between their two respective interpretations, and the verdict will vary depending on which party presents the case more effectively. In no instance do the judges possess a direct, firsthand view of the deed, rather they invariably receive all information only secondhand or thirdhand. In oral arguments, by contrast, the jurors, prosecutor, defendant, witnesses, experts, and attorneys all observe the matter directly for themselves. The deed is thereby retrieved once more from the shadowy realm of memory into the reality of immediate inner perception. Thus, guided by whatever traces may still remain, it is conjured, like a departed spirit, into the magic circle of the courtroom, to come alive once again. The spirit of the deed seizes hold of everyone participating in the judicial hearing, and when the presiding judge, through their summation of the entire proceedings, once again presents the essence of this spirit to the jurors, and they are then able to render a decision on the matter as if drawing upon their own experience. One need only have participated once as a juror in such a proceeding to recognize that both rationality and utility are harmoniously combined in this process. 

If the justice system serves to restore the law where it has been violated and to protect the existence of property, then it has only resolved the former difficulty that arose from the dialectic of political economy. For what good is a justice system if people do not have any property for it protect? Thus, in order to establish prosperity for the people, provisions must be made to ensure that everyone has property as a means to secure their well-being. In as much as man relinquishes his absolute self-sufficiency in order to become a member of a collective body, the mutual responsibility of all for all must extend not only to matters of right, but also to matters of well-being, encompassing the law of associations and public welfare.

## Public Welfare

It has hitherto been the prerogative of the state, apart from the question of disputed legal rights, to concern itself with the well-being of individuals by means of the police and to exercise a paternalistic guardianship over them. However, since the measures for securing this welfare is not confined by the strict boundaries of the law, therein lies the element of arbitrariness for which the police are reproached, an arbitrariness that renders them odious in the eyes of individuals. This is all the more true the further the police, in keeping with the progress of society, believe they must extend the sphere of their activity.

Since we lay claim to the right of self-governance in all spheres of public law, we here posit, as a counterweight to the state's duty of police supervision, the right of individuals to form associations for the purpose of securing their own well-being. Since 1848, the right of association has been recognized in principle even though its practical exercise remains, in many instances, stunted. This is to be expected, for the state would loathe to relinquish its authority to exercise police-like guardianship, even though its citizens are no longer willing to have their well-being arbitrarily granted and withheld from them. Hence the maxim has been established: "Help yourself, and Heaven will help you." This is why, in our own time, the police and voluntary associations find themselves in such fierce conflict with one another, since both seek the very same objective opposite paths. In principle, these two concepts are one and the same to us, and everything that political economy has hitherto presented as "police law" must now be understood as the result and ultimate purpose of the right of association.

The maintenance of the legal order, whereby a judge punishes wrongs already committed and affirms the right to compensation, promotes the public welfare only in a very general and highly incomplete manner. Firstly, it often remains an open question whether a particular individual actually has a particular right, and how that right might be secured for him. Secondly, the very exercise of such a right may itself jeopardize the public welfare, such that the demands of society periodically necessitates limits to its exercise. Indeed, it is evident that man who lives in a state of nature as a solitary being has much less to fear from the arbitrary will of their neighbor than the the communal life of human beings, where the dangers arising from the caprice of neighbors, the unpredictability of natural events, and the social conditions of the populace are infinitely. Not even mentioning how easily does communal living give rise to crime, the risk of fires, and the spread of infectious disease. Thus, it becomes the duty of society to redress the harm for which it is responsible, specifically through the principle of mutual liability among all its individual members, and to safeguard them against the perils they face. The focus of the associations will, therefore, be twofold: positively, to promote the public welfare, and negatively, to neutralize the consequences of those contingencies—arising from both nature and the will—by restricting each individual in the exercise of his rights only insofar as is necessary for the preservation of the common good. The particular welfare of each is treated and realized as a right, not as a coercive right enforced by an external authority, but through the free, ethical consensus of the parties involved. Only in this way is right safeguarded as the concrete existence of freedom.

These contingencies can, in the first place, prove detrimental to the existence of the person and their property. To the extent that there are associations that eliminate such contingencies, they align themselves with the justice system as a subsidiary branch of it. They both have the protection of rights as their common goal, but instead of punishing and restoring, they act to prevent and avert harm, and this is what constitutes public safety. But secondly, it is not only the legal person that must be safeguarded, but also the complete scope of its expressions and activities, i.e. its labor. With this, the rights of labor attains its due recognition. Specifically, labor is recognized in the sense that every individual should be able to acquire property through labor, and through property, secure the satisfaction of his needs, a task that can only be undertaken by worker associations in ensuring that labor is always utilized. Finally, we can conceive of both elements in combination, the safeguarding of the legal person via the source from which all labor originates, i.e. the worker themselves. Implicit in this is the right to existence, and we may designate the protection of this right as the task of mutual insurance associations. Admittedly, however, all these functions of authority must invariably originate from within society as its own form of self-governance. Hegel speaks primarily of the public authority as an external order and arrangement effected through the provident care of the state, and only secondarily of the corporation, as the medium through which ethicality returns to society as an immanent force. By contrast, we remain consistently situated within this sphere of immanent ethicality in that the exercise of authority are entrusted to individuals who owe their office to the electoral choice of voluntary associations of every kind. In this way, the two fundamental principles, the freedom of the individual and the community, are finally united on equal footing of, and only here does Proudhon's maxim that "order is the daughter of freedom" attain the status of truth.

### Public Safety

#### Civil Protection

Even if sufficient justice has been done by the punitive court, the well-being of the individual is not necessarily satisfied. Since the poor are coerced to commit legal violations out of necessity, it is impossible for them to provide restitution to the injured party. Therefore, the moral will of society must not only rectify the internal contingency within the will of the criminal through just punishment, but must also seek to prevent the external consequences of their actions. Hence, a specific sphere of activity is assigned to certain individuals who are trained and tasked with identifying criminals and their misdeeds, to arrest them, and to bring them before a judge, thereby preempting the contingency that a crime might remain undetected. Though since the apprehended person has not yet been judged guilty, but is still under suspicion, this very situation serves as an example of the odiousness of law enforcement that we alluded to previously. For who is exempt from suspicion, or has not, at some point, been suspected? In times of political upheaval, the arbitrary power exercised by those in authority when determining who counts as a "suspect" becomes exceedingly dangerous. Indeed, the Convention even went as far as to enact a "Law of Suspects". But the true "Law of Suspects" is in fact the principle of personal liberty: the rule that an individual may be arrested, or have their home searched, only on legal grounds and pursuant to a judicial warrant. As a result, those vested with public authority must act strictly and exclusively in the service and under the mandate of the judicial system. An individual caught in the very act of committing a crime may, of course, be arrested immediately and without further formality. But even in their case, and likewise in the case of those provisionally arrested due to a suspicion of flight, the judicial system requires that the individual be brought before a judge and that a warrant for arrest be issued by that judge. If this condition fails to be met, the arrested person must be released within twenty-four hours so that their right to personal liberty is not sacrificed to the welfare of individuals, a welfare that is otherwise promoted by the punishment of criminals.

#### Crime Prevention

However, since the primary objective here is to prevent the harm that is caused by a crime, officers must also endeavor to prevent the crime itself. Public safety no longer degrades the human being to a mere means, as it initially appeared to be. To the extent that the threat of punishment serves to deter criminals from future offenses, and to deter others in general through his example, such that the punishment ultimately effects the common good, and the punished individual becomes an end in themselves. The well-being of all individuals, including their own, is pursued through a collective societal arrangement. While we certainly do not wish to dismiss the importance of influencing the criminal's intent for the purpose of their rehabilitation, the officers are charged with preventing unintentional harm that may potentially arise from the otherwise permissible exercise of individual autonomy in lawful acts, given the physical proximity of human dwellings and the general conditions of communal living. Though we have observed these possibilities to evolve into punishable acts, transgressions that fall under the purview of penal jurisdiction, we cannot, within this restriction upon the arbitrary exercise of right, perceive this as a curtailment of freedom. For such an exercise of my right, such as smoking a cigar in a smoke-free zone or driving at high speed through busy streets, by virtue of possibly endangering others, constitutes actual endangerment. As a result, the negation of such conduct represents nothing other than the freedom of the individual, a freedom that asserts itself in the interest of the rights of all.

#### Disaster Response

It is not only the damage caused by the arbitrary will, viewed as an internal contingency, that must be prevented through public regulations, but also the damages caused by the external contingencies of nature. Since every people is bound to specific natural conditions and inhabits a particular environment, they are in many respects dependent upon this environment. Nature can prove favorable to a people, promoting their agriculture through climatic conditions, just as it can be detrimental to their well-being. As a result, for the protection of agriculture, farmers must form worker associations and work together to prevent their hard work from being squandered by extreme weather conditions such as droughts or flooding. To this end, they must construct maintain dikes, drainage canals, and similar structures at their joint expense. Through other associations, provisions must be made for street lighting, well-maintained roads, street paving, bridges, emergency services, and the like. Just as in the case of crime, where the primary objective is to prevent harm and to make restitution for it (should it occur), so too must this principle apply to damage caused by nature, regardless of whether such damage could have been averted or not. Since, in accordance with our fundamental principle, all are liable for the individual, any harm done to single person must be remedied by the entire community. This is accomplished through the insurance that the association provides, whereby a small sum, the premium, is collected from the insured in order to cover any incurred losses using these funds, whereby each individual, through the payment of the premium, acquires a right to compensation. Alternatively, everyone within a community contributes annually, in proportion to the value of their respective property, an amount sufficient to indemnify those who have suffered damage caused by fires. In the latter case, the premium is paid after the fact rather than beforehand, and it is never greater than the actual loss, whereas in the case of associations, the premium tends to be a fixed amount.

### Worker Associations

Up to this point, if we have viewed public authority from its negative aspect, to ward off harm, then its second task consists in promoting the general welfare directly and in a positive manner. This constitutes the second of the supplements to the economic system that we outlined at the conclusion of our discussion on political economy. It is not sufficient to only secure the property of those who already possess it, rather, property must be also be procured for those who do not possess it, so that, through such ownership, they may attain their own well-being. In essence, the fulfillment of this demand is what has come to be termed in modern times the "Solution to the Social Question", a solution consisting in redressing the excessive disparity between wealth and poverty. Two means have been proposed or applied to this end: the latter is the traditional system of poor relief, while the former is the public ownership of property. 

In the administration of poor relief, there has been an attempt to apply a paternalistic approach to negative aspect of public authority, specifically, by the state levying a "poor tax" upon its citizens, thereby ensuring that the entire community contributes to the welfare of the poor. However, that the poor have fallen into misfortune as a result of the complex interdependencies inherent in society and that the community as a whole is therefore obligated to compensate them is not entirely accurate. In the theory of political economy, we have just witnessed how the contradictions of the division of labor, machinery, competition, and so forth, impoverish workers despite their best efforts, and society must therefore answer for the harm for which it is responsible. Though, this by no means proves that the satisfaction of needs should be granted to the poor without work. For through the paternalistic guardianship of poor relief, society strips the individual of securing their own well-being through their own efforts. This subservience breeds within them a bitterness toward the very society that has cast them out from the fabric of its operations like a worn-out tool. When society then feels the obligation to take them in and provide for their sustenance, all this does is provokes their defiance toward the community at large, leading them to demand this sustenance from them as a right. This chronic unemployment generates what Hegel (*Philosophy of Right*, § 244) calls the "rabble," the creation of which must be prevented at all costs. Yet, this is precisely what the current system of poor relief is completely incapable of achieving, as the example of England has taught us. While taxes are increasingly imposed upon wealth in an attempt to curb this social ill, the problem has only continued to grow all the more, for the ease with which one may enjoy sustenance without work entices many toward idleness, with more abandoning any relief "to leave the poor to their fate and consign them to public begging" (Hegel, *Philosophy of Right*, § 245). 

To escape this circular reasoning, modern scholarship has proposed a different remedy: the community of goods. Admittedly, this doctrine does not seek, as the administrative apparatus of poor relief does, to provide the poor with work devoid of any attendant benefit. However, even within a community of goods, labor is not made the standard of well-being, but each worker is allotted from the common property only as much as they require. The liberty of the individual to work as much as they desire is inverted into a state of unfreedom and tutelage, wherein each is compelled to subsist at the collective’s expense. The great social task we are called upon to resolve is this: given that prosperity and freedom can only be achieved through labor, we must ensure that labor is rendered remunerative by endowing it with the capacity to generate property. This is possible only through the principle of distributive justice, whereby, at every moment, each individual receives for their labor precisely the equivalent of what they have contributed.

If this question arose unresolved from the inherent contradictions of political economy, abandoneding the individual to fend for themselves in a blind struggle for survival, we have now discovered the path to its solution: voluntary association. For through such association, as Lipke observes, the value of labor will invariably be rendered equivalent to money, and through it, as we have previously cited from Proudhon, every craftsman will possess a mint within their own workshop. We can break down this requirement into the following three formulas:

1) Supply and demand are in equilibrium
2) Labor and products are proportional
3) Use-value and exchange-value are equal

Once all three formulas are satisfied, the universal basis of prosperity will reveal itself as unimpeded circulation, a state in which no stagnation occurs anywhere, restoring the health of the economy. However, to demonstrate how this is to be achieved, we must now elaborate in greater detail on how the contradiction of wealth and poverty, deepening at every stage of progress, can be resolved only through the universal mutual guarantee of all for all. And thus, we shall move out of Proudhon’s doctrine of the inevitability of human misery to the economic harmonies of Bastiat.

#### Solution to the Division of Labor

First, we must resolve the contradictions brought about by the division of labor:

##### Cooperative Agriculture

If the division of labor secured victory for the large landowner over the smaller proprietor, as much as the latter had not even reached the point of adopting it while the former had already consolidated it, then the simple resolution of this contradiction is for the farmers to voluntarily unite and pool their landholdings in order to reap the same benefits of large-scale farming. They allocate among themselves the labor of the plowman, herdsman, hunter, brewer, distiller, and so forth, they jointly acquire threshing, mowing, and sowing machines, and they then distribute the collective profit in proportion to the labor contributed by each, as well as the quantity and quality of the land pooled. Their distilleries, starch and sugar factories, and blast furnaces will be able to compete with those of the large landowner, and even if several families together earn no more than a single one, they nevertheless come out ahead in the distribution because they carry out the labor themselves and thereby have less in wages to pay out. By contrast, the large landowner, since they only perform the work of supervision, takes on the costs from a multitude of families outside of the land's yield, and thus the disparity between large and small landholdings becomes narrower in this respect as well. Thus, Hegel is mistaken when he posits the necessity of the corporation, a form of association, only for the artisans (*Philosophy of Right* § 250), and not equally for the farmers and the scholars. For the distinctions between city and country must be reduced to a distinction in scale.

##### Cooperative Artisanry

It cannot be denied that the division of labor, alongside its attendant drawbacks, is far more pronounced in trades, thus demanding a stronger remedy through concerted efforts. Small masters of the same craft must join forces, rent a shared workshop, and jointly purchase raw materials in bulk, thereby saving on heating, tools, and rent. Similar trades must also combine their efforts so that, should one have an excess of labor while another suffers from a shortage of labor, they can transfer the members between them, thereby bringing the supply of labor and products into proper balance. Furthermore, related trades, such as all contributing to the construction of a house, the manufacture of agricultural tools, and so forth, can unite to produce a final work. In so doing, they eliminate the need for an entrepreneur to advance capital and siphon off a lion's share of the profits. They are able to produce the work more cheaply and distribute the proceeds from the sale among themselves in proportion to contribution. To avert further detriment arising from the division of labor, they integrate the functions of the merchant into their own operations. They rent a sales hall where they jointly trade the goods they have produced, a practice frequently observed among associations of carpenters and tailors. Admittedly, each individual must wait for payment until their specific work has been sold, yet even here they can also find a solution by incorporating the functions of the banker into their own operations. They issue shares of small denomination, raising the necessary funds through weekly contributions. It is truly astonishing how easily funds accumulate in this manner, in accordance with the principle: *concordia res parvae crescunt* (small things grow when united). The sums paid in are credited to the workers who earn interest and share in the profits. Moreover, the workers enjoy access to credit against these deposits, for borrowers and lenders are one and the same persons, thereby meeting and complementing one another even more cheaply and profitably than was the case previously with a separate banker. It was in this spirit that Schulze-Delitzsch established his artisans' banks, institutions that have branched out across the whole of Germany and have found imitators in France and Italy. In this way, the artisan is best able to help themselves and no longer stands in need of the capital of the wealthy. Still less do they need, as Lassalle would have it, to call upon the assistance of the state. For such a course would invariably amount to a form of public ownership of goods, inasmuch as the state would necessarily have to be constituted as the sole capitalist.

##### Cooperative Manufacturing

Since the division of labor exerts both the most pernicious and salutary influence upon manufacturing, everything here depends on preserving the latter while eliminating the former. If large-scale capital increasingly oppresses its workers, depressing wages by recruiting from the excess of labor, and if capitalists are permitted to collude in determining wage levels, then workers must be granted the very same right, and the cessation of work cannot treated as an offense. In this regard, the strikes undertaken by workers in England and America are well known. Alongside the freedom to strike, the demand for equality also arises, ensuring that the worker need not be content with the meager wage that barely sustains them, nor the capitalist, in addition to the interest yielded by their capital, carry off a "capital premium" or "capital profit", as Lassalle puts it, "is formed by the difference between the selling price of the commodity and the sum of the wages and remuneration for all forms of labor, including intellectual labor, that have in any way contributed to the production of that commodity." This disparity is an evil, for it redounds solely to the benefit of the factory owner. Once mechanical labor, intellectual labor, and capital labor have been remunerated, not according to Fourier’s fixed formula, but according to the price that happens to be deterined for these activities at any given moment, then the cost price effectively becomes the selling price, were it not that competition frequently drives the latter either above or below the former. Since the worker receives a fixed wage, one might assume that it is only fair to allow the factory owner to retain the surplus, the capital profit, just as they bear the risk of falling prices. The ideal solution, of course, would be to prevent these fluctuations altogether, and we shall soon see how this is possible. But even under the existing system, the relationship of employment should not be one of hostility, but an ethical bond. The cooperative guarantees each employee a share of the profits and retains a portion of the collective surplus as a reserve fund to weather adverse economic conditions and losses. Thus, the worker becomes an equal partner in the enterprise, resulting in the disappearance of the conflict between capital and labor. Furthermore, since labor also receives its "bonus", the cooperative forms an ethical community with its workers, using its funds to build houses near the workplace, where they provide housing to their members and engage in initiatives similar to what Eugène Sue so beautifully describes in his social novels. Naturally, all of this must be based on voluntary association. For the so-called "truck system", in which workers receive goods to satisfy their needs instead of money, is even more oppressive for them, as the factory owner can arbitrarily determine both the price and the quality of the goods they have purchased, thereby unjustly shortchanging their workers on their wages.

##### Cooperative Commerce

Only genuine commerce, not speculative trading, serves to prevent the price fluctuations alluded to earlier. Speculative trading consists of using the sheer magnitude of one’s capital to disadvantage both producers and consumers by purchasing goods from producers as cheaply as possible, stockpiling them when a surplus exists, and selling them at the highest possible price to consumers once a shortage sets in. This practice is particularly reprehensible, criminal to the highest degree, in the case of grain hoarders, for they refuse to open their granaries even when the populace begins to suffer severe deprivation, holding out instead for higher prices. Should they be compelled to sell? Should one go,as Hegel suggests (*Philosophy of Right* § 236), to the extreme limit of unfreedom by employing a so-called "market police" to mandate fixed prices even for "the most common necessities of life"? Everything hinges upon countering the arbitrary fluctuation of prices through the very freedom of commercial exchange. Genuine commerce serves only as the vehicle for the circulation of goods. As means of transport and communication become increasingly efficient, making connections ever easier and market bottlenecks ever rarer, the merchant should not seek to profit from the artificial rise and fall of prices, a veritable game of market speculation that is in no way superior to the trading of financial securities. Instead, the merchant is merely the broker, the intermediary between producer and consumer, and eliminates the division of labor, along with its attendant disadvantages, that is typically imposed by such a specific middleman. Producers entrust goods of every kind to their safekeeping without actually purchasing them themselves, and upon selling them to the consumer, they charge (for labor, storage fees, insurance, and the like) only their actual expenses. Let the "great merchant" scornfully label them a mere shopkeeper for this, if they will. Even these trades, specifically the freight forwarders responsible for transporting goods, maintain the closest ties with them, and within their warehouses, he establishes a general marketplace where consumers and producers meet, the latter even receiving cash advances from them, much as they would from a banker, thereby abolishing this particular division of labor as well.

Through such a comprehensive integration of supply and demand, a "Goods Credit Society", first established in Paris and subsequently modeled in Berlin, seeks to abolish the use of money entirely, enabling any individual to procure the products of others simply by issuing drafts against their own output. The aim was to revert to an improved system of barter, much as Proudhon envisioned the establishment of an exchange bank in every municipality, as a sort of communal bazaar. But what went unconsidered was that, no matter how greatly these commercial connections were facilitated, and no matter how rapidly goods might realize their value through circulation, human needs and desires had become too numerous and diverse to allow for the exchange process to be confined to a limited circle, as was possible in the simpler conditions of primitive society. Since such payment orders retains their value in only to specific locality, money intervenes as an intermediary in wholesale trade conducted from place to place. For although every individual is legally capable of issuing bills of exchange, the confidence placed in such instruments, and by extension, even more so in the payment orders issued by small-scale producers, will nevertheless not extend beyond the confines of their community.

#### Solution to Mechanization

Like the division of labor, machinery also loses its pernicious effect when free association serves as a bulwark against the dominance of capital. Small masters, even journeymen, join forces to gradually acquire more advanced tools and machinery by pooling their savings in people's banks. The owner of a machine enters into an alliance with the worker, and every task enhanced yields a fair income. Alternatively, the machine owner may lease out its power, in part, to several independent workers of the most diverse trades whose workshops are located nearby. In this scenario, the machine no longer creates slaves, but liberates the worker from the dominion of money, enabling them to effectively compete against large-scale capitalists.

#### Solution to Competition

In this context, has not even competition itself lost its intimidating aspect? When associations vie with the solitary, large-scale capitalist, the latter realizes that victory is no longer as easily won as it was against isolated, small-scale masters. To safeguard their property, and perhaps even to secure still greater profits, they will inevitably join the association, thereby putting an end to the struggle between capital and labor. As a result, the rivalry between associations need not cease, for without it, industrial diligence would stagnate and grind to a halt. Such rivalry is essential for the determination of values ​​which, no less than the value of money, must never be subject to legislative decree. Even in the case of communal sales outlets, rivalry is not precluded, even as much as individual workers may affix their names to their work, and the prices of their products may vary according to their quality. The struggle should not be a fight for survival, but a contest for one's standing within society. Given the ease with which one may transition from one trade to another, the vanquished party simply exchanges one sphere of labor for another. Here, Fourier’s "butterfly instincts" find ample gratification. Initially, one would certainly transition to a kindred trade until, should all else fail, one takes up the spade to return to humanity’s primordial state, to that paradise not yet entirely lost. Just as in the pursuit of refinement and culture, labor-power flows away from agriculture toward other trades, so too,should overcrowding occur, must it flow back to the land, and this perpetual circulation must encompass both labor-power and goods alike. Competition is not precluded among artists, physicians, and trustees, so it must also be introduced among teachers of religion and science. A guaranteed salary and lifelong tenure often causes professional vigor to flag. Conversely, the university professorship already finds a spur to progress in the form of an honorary stipend. However, dependence upon the state must be eliminated in all spheres, for the teacher, like everyone else, should be answerable solely to their own association. By forming free associations, teaching and learning assume the same unconstrained relationship of labor and exchange that prevails in all other professions. There are no civil servants standing in opposition to non-civil servants, rather, everyone is simply a functionary of their respective cooperatives and associations, giving each individual their specific work through the mediation of their own free will.

#### Solution to Monopoly

The state must not maintain exclusive monopolies, even for the purpose of encouraging industrial diligence, because such measures invariably entail a form of bureaucratic tutelage, impeding the path of the creative efforts of others, whereas unfettered competition, along with the success it yields, constitutes the best form of encouragement. But how is the victor in this contest to be awarded the laurel they deserve without the retention of some form of exclusive privilege? Were the state to attempt to purchase an inventor’s secret from them, awarding them a public bounty in exchange for making that secret available to the general public, such a policy would border on eminent domain, for it would entail granting a benefit drawn from the public purse to producers who had done nothing to merit it, not having made an invention of their own. This difficulty resolves itself, as is so often the case, in the simplest manner imaginable: through voluntary association. If an inventor were to succeed in keeping their invention a secret, their reward would arise spontaneously, as they would be able to sell their goods at a higher price. However, this would occur at the expense of the common good, for innovation is a cause that is truly advanced to its fullest extent only when a new invention is widely disseminated, thereby becoming available for use by workers across the entire globe. How can this be achieved without detriment to the inventor? It would always prove difficult for them to prevent others from independently deducing their secret, and any legal claim for damages against an individual who had illicitly divulged or misappropriated that secret would remain highly uncertain. In either scenario, their exclusive privilege would be irretrievably lost. Therefore, in order to safeguard their own interests, the inventor chooses to align them with their fellow producers, allowing them to share in their exclusive privilege in exchange for investing in their invention. The spirit of rivalry would have spread from individual to individual, and from association to association, and a further sale, for a much lower sum, to all the remaining workers not yet privy to the secret could bring the matter to a complete conclusion, elevating the invention to the status of unrestricted common property.

In the realm of intellectual works, in order to address the conflict between the prerogative of the author and publisher on the one hand, and the right of the public to access new ideas (constituting the common intellectual heritage) on the other, there is no need for the expedient proposed by Louis Blanc: that the state should offer a reward to the author in order to purchase from them the exclusive right of reproduction. As we have already noted, such a measure smacks of eminent domain. The injustice of such a compulsory buyout would be all the greater given that author retains the ability to produce revised editions, since their intellectual property still bears the distinct imprint of their individual personality. But even once the intellectual property has, upon the author's death, become fully part of the public domain, why should the *material* right of exclusive reproduction be revoked from their publisher, given that this right is in no way inferior to others form of property? In the case of industrial inventions, whether appropriated legitimately through the intellectual effort of an independent invention, or illegitimately through theft, is invariably more difficult to achieve the unauthorized reprinting of books. For this very reason, copyright law has come to enjoy the fullest judicial protection against such piracy. The only remaining question is how the matter should be handled when a society, having acquired full intellectual ownership of an author's ideas, finds itself confronted by a publisher who either fails to produce a new edition out of reluctance to incur the costs, or sells the existing edition at an exorbitant price, thereby impeding the dissemination of knowledge and culture into society. 

However, the matter presents itself differently in the instance where the public stands opposed to the publisher. If the publisher does not infringe upon the intellectual property of the public by selling the works at a low price, then there is no justification for depriving them of their legitimately acquired tangible property. But if the publisher has no competition, they will be all the more inclined to exploit their privilege. In this conflict, tangible property must yield to intellectual property, though not without compensation. Even if the state were to provide this compensation, it would be less susceptible to the criticism of being made into a public good, since it is, after all, paid in exchange for a common asset. However, to avoid even the mere appearance of such a system, given that individuals might always argue, "We derive no benefit from this collectively funded compensation, for we did not purchase the works of this author", the law of association remains the only real solution to this question. To avoid both the disadvantages of unauthorized reprints and the threat of expropriation proceedings, the publisher will deem it most prudent to cede their privilege to the booksellers' association. The association would then grant the publisher payment in return for an arrangement whereby all booksellers profit from the sale of the book, and buyers are served at even lower prices than would be possible under the regime of exclusive privilege. By virtue of the granting of a price discount among publishers themselves, it is only the buyer who is disadvantaged, since it is a matter of chance whether the bookseller consents to pass this deduction on to them. If neither the state were to offer compensation, nor an association a purchase price, then this tangible property would pass into the public domain solely through the mechanism of prescription arising from the publisher’s non-use, and the author’s work could then be published by any bookseller. In such a scenario, every specific edition with its particular textual variants and annotations (or translation), would once again be the distinct property of the publisher until it has passed into the public domain via the very method described.

Likewise, authors are well advised to cede the proprietary rights of their intellectual property to an association of their fellow professionals, in order to avoid the risk of failing to find a publisher. Such a writers association would, in turn, enter into negotiations with a booksellers' association regarding the works approved by the majority, with the intention of bringing them to sale. Indeed, the collectivization of authors, booksellers, and printers would yield the most significant benefits, not only for these professionals themselves, but equally for society as a whole. All too frequently, to the detriment of publishers, authors, and the general public, important works remain unpublished while trivial ones are printed, simply because the individual bookseller lacks the right judgment. Yet even here, it goes without saying that any such associations must be founded solely upon voluntary agreement, if one wishes to avoid the accusation that we are turning publications into public property. Commission publishing, by contrast, constitutes a form of exploitation of the author by the bookseller, an exploitation that stems directly from the total isolation of the individual author in the absence of the very cooperative associations just described. At a meeting held in Leipzig on August 20, 1865, attended by one hundred German authors, a "German Authors' Association" was founded and issued the following appeal: 

>"The German author currently labors under the burden of manifold and severe grievances. The strength of the individual is insufficient to eradicate them. Only through the solidarity of the many—only through the firm and close union of German authors within a single league—can these ills be overcome through sustained collective effort; only thus can the entire German literary profession be elevated to the position of prominence to which it is rightfully entitled. The successes achieved by the Association of French Authors in Paris stand as proof of what a tightly knit community is capable of attaining."

#### Solution to Taxation

As long as the drive for knowledge has not yet become a powerful force among the people, such that the state must compel children to attend school, the necessity of paying salaries to the teachers remains evident. However, if the provision for the satisfaction of these needs is made by the people themselves through associations of all kinds, along with the salaries of these and other state officials, the necessity of levying taxes to raise the funds for them also ceases to exist. Each individual pays only for the services they want to purchase, whereas a tax deprives me of a portion of my property whether I claim a reciprocal service or not. This applies primarily to indirect taxation, as it exploits consumers at the expense of producers. The progressive income tax, which every association imposes upon itself to meet its own needs, is not only the most rational form of taxation, but it is also the most beneficial form that most effectively fosters prosperity. Collective and individual interests are perfectly balanced since my contribution to the common fund is made to the benefit of my own property. Whereas every tax on basic necessities weighs most heavily upon the poor, under a progressive income tax, each individual only contributes in proportion to their income and the specific needs of their association at any given time. Thus, the burden of taxation is distributed equally among all, as the varying size of each individual’s contribution, reflecting their varying income levels, feels neither heavier nor lighter to them than it does to anyone else. In this system, each individual evaluates their own income, and is evaluated by their fellow members who know one another personally. Thus, to the extent that taxation, the declared enemy of property, serves only to benefit that very property, transforming it into a voluntary reciprocity of contribution and labor, we can already see how the dominion of labor, what Proudhon identifies as the true aim of society, is achieved - and what follows will serve to establish this dominion even more firmly.

#### Solution to Trade

In order to allow labor-power to attain its full value, and thus to bring the human personality, which constitutes the sum of its deeds, to a state of infinite significance, labor-power must be able to place its products into completely unhindered circulation for the purposes of domestic and foreign trade. Initially, cities walled themselves off from one another, later, only districts did so, and finally, states did the same. These very states have create trade treaties to bring about partial alleviations in mutual commerce, to mutually reduce tariffs, and so forth. However, these barriers must be abolished entirely, specifically among all the nations of the globe, so that the Earth's geographical circumference simultaneously becomes its customs frontier. Not only prohibitions and protective tariffs, but also fiscal tariffs must be eliminated, for they also constitute a form of public ownership of goods and can be entirely replaced by the aforementioned income tax. All objections against unrestricted free trade cease to apply if, through cooperative measures, the struggling and nascent branches of industry are supported, the transition of workers from one sector to another is facilitated, and so on. Only under a system of free trade can industrial diligence truly gain strength and learn to withstand foreign competition. By virtue of the fact that each nation produces only that which it is best and most cheaply able to produce, each nation will also be able to purchase the best goods at the lowest prices, causing price fluctuations to increasingly disappear, to the benefit of everyone. Furthermore, the ever-increasing improvement of transportation routes will contribute most significantly to the ever-expanding volume of trade. Highways and railways do not merely facilitate circulation, given the vast quantity of goods to be transported, they also render customs clearance all but impossible, and the abolition of the customs duty likewise causes smuggling to dry up.

#### Solution to Credit

Since the less values ​​fluctuate, the more exchange value is consistently embodied within use value, the worker finds a mint within their own workshop and can be granted credit all the more readily, for the very basis of credit, originally the exclusive prerogative of the property owner, has become generalized. If personal credit is already something the worker cannot be denied, since labor-power, by virtue of its inherent mobility, will always be capable of generating the collateral for debt, and will much more readily receive advances against the labor they have already performed. The great contradiction of credit, which Proudhon brought to the fore, is thereby resolved. And the credit banks for artisans, of which we have spoken, will only attain the pinnacle of their perfection when they simultaneously function as associations for cooperative production. This is the very goal with which Schulze-Delitzsch sought to conclude his efforts, whereas Lassalle proposes to *begin* with it on the grounds that it is precisely the production association that provides the credit bank with its secure foundation. Just as with an artisans' association, so too will every local community possess its own bank, one that embodies the threefold characteristics of banking, and whose banknotes (if they remain necessary at all) need never exceed the bank's actual reserves of cash. However, the more secure exchange values ​​become, due to the stability of prices, the less the worker will need credit at all. In any event, capital would increasingly enter into a fraternal alliance with labor, for given the certainty of repayment, it would be assured of its own equally stable rate of interest, since the price of money would partake in the general stability of all values.

#### Solution to Population Growth

Yet one final contradiction remains to be resolved. For if the increased efficiency in the satisfaction of needs is brought about by the resolution of all previous contradictions, and the consequent ease in establishing one’s own household causes the population to grow at a faster rate than the means of production, then the resolution of those earlier contradictions once again serves us well in resolving the present one. We need only transform the mobility of labor from one branch of industry to another into a mobility of persons from place to place, just as goods have done. By virtue of the fundamental right to complete freedom of movement, emigration, and so forth, the surplus population of one country may relocate to another where foreign workers can still find an adequate livelihood. However, should all countries advanced in their development become overcrowded, possessing within themselves a sufficient supply of labor, then the surplus population will migrate to new, uncultivated regions of the earth. In migrating to new lands, the spirit of a people is rejuvenated, and the individual returns to agriculture, the primordial occupation of mankind, acquiring both property and a new homeland through the cultivation of the soil. But what happens when the entire habitable earth has been populated? It would seem that the social question rests upon this last contradiction. For if wealth consists of the surplus of products, a surplus that is repeatedly consumed by a population growing at an ever-faster pace, this wealth is by the very wealth of surplus labor. However, there is no need to fear that the entire globe will become overcrowded and render Malthusian proposals a grim reality. For once prosperity has become more widespread, the drive to reproduce becomes tempered by other, nobler drives, such as those of art and science, thereby tempering population growth. Then, man will no longer need to be directed by the clergy toward some distant celestial realm, but will instead attain true happiness here on Earth. In this way, not only would property, and gainful employment, be secured, but the very person of the worker themselves would be safeguarded against adversity. To achieve this is the task of mutual aid.

### Mutual Aid

Initially we observed that the safeguard of person and property, to the extent that the security of both, initiated by the justice system, was perfected through the life of worker associations. The person in their legal existence entrusted themselves to the care of these associations, which strove to ensure that their labor would indeed result in both property and enjoyment. One last question remains to be answered: how does this labor arise throughout the life of the person? Since labor is not a product of nature, but rather of spirit, the person must be rendered capable of performing it. Therefore, the livelihood of the worker must be secured, clearing away those obstacles that lie not in objective circumstances, but in personal conditions. Associations of every kind must be formed to transform the natural person into the socialized worker. Not only must the developing human being be educated to become a worker, as well as provisions made for aspiring workers, but society must also counter the natural contingencies that might compromise the activity of the established worker. Lastly, society must brighten the twilight years of the worker who has grown old and seeks retirement. Just as the family revealed itself to be the complete legal person, caring for its each of its members out of mutual aid, so too does the worker possess the right, as a member of society, to be taken under its protection through the medium of the worker association. Only then can all stand for one and one stand for all. For this reason, we term this concept mutual aid, which logically cannot be an institution externally imposed by the state, but can only takes the form of mutual assistance that has grown organically out of the association itself.

#### Child Care

While it is initially the duty of the family to provide for the future worker and to support them in the choice of their vocation, the family itself may be dissolved by contingent circumstances before the worker has become independent and capable of earning their own livelihood. In such cases, public poor relief has hitherto intervened as a public institution, typically by placing the destitute child in an orphanage. The guardianship court appoints a guardian for minors, and does the same for a person who can no longer take care of themselves, acting in the interest of the individuals themselves. To ensure that this relationship assumes a truly ethical character, the family council mentioned previously appoints guardians for legal dependents who do not have the support of a natural family. Associations, whether organized locally or along vocational lines, take measures to ensure that those deprived of their natural family are provided with a new one. This is not achieved by a public authority arbitrarily assigning children to isolated families, a practice that might be termed "sporadic family care", nor is it achieved by raising hundreds of children together in an orphanage, where they are deprived of the ethical bonds that are forged by family life. Instead, in order to bestow this benefit upon orphans, associations are formed in which families adopt and care for them. 

Similarly, a worker association, which is also a form of spiritual kinship, can collectively provide for the children of its own profession. It goes without saying that this association is a voluntary one, and that there can be no question of a closed guild system of the Middle Ages. These associations also take charge of the professional training of aspiring workers. It is in this context that the dispute arises whether apprentices, journeymen, and masters should be required to pass examinations before the association's board, or whether anyone may practice a trade, perform a job, or hold an office, even if they have absolutely no proof of their competence to do so. It would be appalling if any fraud could step forward and present themselves to consumers as a qualified practitioner of one sort or another, only to satisfy their needs in the most predatory manner possible. To this, the opposing side counters that it is entirely the consumer's own fault if he turns to an unexamined fraud and further argue that no examination is truly effective anyway, since fraudsters usually manage to slip through the cracks while honest and brilliant candidates fail. Then how does one surpass this contradiction? The only answer here, as everywhere else in the realm of law, is freedom. It must be left to the associations of the profession to conduct their own affairs as they see fit. The members of an association, especially the scientific ones, are entitled to demand that newcomers prove their worth. The profession as a whole thereby gains in prestige if it admits members who have produced something that proves their worth (such as an examination or dissertation), for in doing so it can offer those seeking its services a greater guarantee of competence. However, anyone who decides not to join retains the full right to demonstrate their skill through their work, even without an examination.

#### Health Insurance

If a worker finds their labor hindered or rendered impossible, whether by natural accident or by conditions within civil society, even the paternal police state takes this "son of society" under its wing. However, workhouses and poorhouses represent coerced labor and charity. The worst scenario arises when the state, favoring a particular religious persuasion, stipulates the imposition of religious belief as the price to be paid for physical care. Assistance must be a free and mutual undertaking, and the worker must know that, when they receive aid, they have freely contributed to providing it. If a worker becomes temporarily incapacitated, they are immediately severed from their means of livelihood, leaving their dependents to face destitution, compelled to incur debts and pawn their most essential possessions, only to return to work in such a depleted state that they may be unable to recover from the blow for years to come. It is precisely against this plight that health insurance associations offer a remedy: the worker pays a small contribution into a sick fund for themselves and their family members. This fund employs one or more physicians to treat the sick, and provides treatment free of charge. Then, question arises whether health insurance should be local in nature, or organized by trade. In this case too, everything must be left to free agreement, and whatever course is most beneficial in any given instance will naturally come to pass. In large factories, such funds are best established directly among the workers themselves, even though they may belong to various trades, such as machinists, locksmiths, carpenters, etc. 

Furthermore, children may be included in the health insurance of the specific trade or factory, or alternatively, a separate fund may be established specifically for them through the public health insurance of the community. Provisions must likewise be made for the children and family of deceased workers to which they have contributed during their lifetimes. Every worker they must possess the certainty that, following their death, their loved ones will not be left in need. Life insurance also falls into this category, where premiums paid entitle the insured to a sum of capital that is disbursed upon the insured's death, either to a designated beneficiary or to a legal heir. However, a capital payment is not always sufficient to provide adequate assistance to a family. In this way, the worker association has the duty to protect the families of their workers from destitution. Finally, to ensure the worker’s peace of mind regarding the future, provision must also be made for funeral benefit funds to cover burial expenses, which may be integrated with the aforementioned health or life insurance.

#### Pension

Finally, when a worker is no longer able to work, they must have the right to spend the twilight of their life free from want. Just as families have the duty to provide for their oldest members, society also has the duty to provide for those rendered incapable of work by old age. If civil servants must accept deductions from their salaries to serve this ultimate end, then so do workers contribute to their pension fund. Furthermore, a person who has the means to do so can secure an additional annuity by entrusting a sum of capital to a society, thereby enabling themselves, after a certain number of years, to enjoy a higher rate of return than their current capital would otherwise yield. The annuity institution is, in this sense, the converse of life insurance, yet both serve to eliminate the uncertainty and contingency inherent in the future and in old age.

Now, the fact that all these associations do not stand in isolation, but form mutual alliances with each other, in accordance with local conditions, to form larger federations that span across communities, municipalities and provinces, is the specific task of civil society, understood as the organizational form that gives concrete reality to the content of public welfare.

## Civil Society

Humans beings draw ever closer to one another in order to generate the general will out of the harmony of all individual wills. In the contract, arbitrary choice brought two persons together, in the family, it was love, and in the association, it was the conscious reconciliation of self-interest and sociability. Those associations requiring local proximity unite to form a whole within the community. for when the common interests of a community are administered collectively, it becomes evident that they can be managed more effectively than if they were handled in isolation. Secondly, the federation of many communities constitutes the municipality, specifically the composite municipality, since we may already  term a community a "small" municipality. Thus, the community may be regarded as a smaller municipality or as a constituent part thereof, whereas a city, being a locality of greater scope, will often itself constitute a composite municipality. Thirdly, the union of many municipalities forms the province, which aligns itself with the natural geographic regions within a nation.

### Community

While rural families, typically more scattered in their arrangement, may coalesce into a single community in the form of a village, a large city, as a complete municipality, subdivides into many communities. Conversely, a small city may constitute a single community, which subsequently joins with other rural communities to form one greater, composite municipality. All the various associations that form a community hold their meetings in a single communal building called a clubhouse, or alternatively called a town hall, that is collectively funded by each association and realizes significant savings on overhead costs. This building serves simultaneously as the seat of the entire community's governing board and as the meeting place for associations within the community. The community is akin to a cell of the animal organism, participating in ever-higher forms of association, uniting with each other without ever forfeiting their intrinsic nature, and thereby mirroring the greater whole into which it has been integrated.

#### Community Assembly

If we first consider the constitution of the community association, we find that it serves as the model for the constitutions of all other associations, from the smallest to the largest. This constitution demonstrates how the blind necessity of interconnection has fully evolved into the clear consciousness of the moral bond. All inhabitants of the community, to the extent that they voluntarily register to join, constitute this association, for no one can be compelled to participate. All members entitled to vote convene in a general assembly to establish ordinances, elect the association’s officers, authorize expenditures, receive financial reports, and so forth. This assembly is, in the logical syllogism (I-P-U), the predicate of the major premise, the terminus major (U), that expresses the general will of the entire body as such and makes it a binding guideline for all. The administrative council, elected by the general assembly for a fixed term (usually a year) and comprising a chairperson, secretaries, and treasurers (P) attends to the day-to-day affairs of the association and performs the actual work of administration. Lastly, the chairperson acts as the active will of the association (I), convening the assemblies, executing resolutions, and so forth.

As for the specific functions of the association, the most effective way to understand them is to observe them in practice. The associations align themselves with the division of the city into smaller communities, whereby communities that are geographically contiguous invariably join forces to form a single association. Members gather as human beings, citizens, and members of the community, not as members of a specific social class or trade. People meet not to collectively pursue their economic interests, but to pursue their common humane and civic interests as neighbors. The highest objective of these associations is to influence the municipal authorities, which constitute the very center of the community's power, by seeking to oversee and help administer municipal institutions as they fall within the community's boundaries, thereby channeling the living source of activity in every direction and distributing it from the center outward to the periphery. During the association's regular meetings, lectures on scientific, religious, economic, political, and other subjects are presented in rotation. Current affairs and matters concerning the municipality are discussed. In a designated question box, those seeking knowledge deposit their inquiries to later become the subject of open discussion.

Finally, in our concept of civil society, the police do not supervise these associations. By virtue of the principle of self-government, the associations supervise themselves, and harmoniously reconciling liberty with order through the agency of stewards elected from within the assembly itself. Whenever municipal, provincial, or state elections are held, it goes without saying that these elections are conducted under the auspices of these associations. In addition to these assemblies dedicated to public affairs, there are also social gatherings for families.

#### Mutual Associations

The mutual associations that are necessary for the generation of prosperity, for the resolution of the contradictions of the national economy, for the new emerging society, join the general community association as its subordinate members.

Foremost among the mutual associations is the credit union or mutual savings association, which functions as a kind of community bank. In such associations, a collective capital fund is accumulated that does not constitute the private property of the members and which is administered by individuals who do not themselves intend to borrow. The general assembly, a body of trustees, and an executive board constitute the three organizational components of the association. The trustees investigate the circumstances of the loan applicant, specifically their capacity for work and their solvency, as well as the reliability of their creditor. Both the creditor and the debtor bind themselves to one another by mutual obligation, and the latter repays the debt gradually in small installments. The executive board decides upon the approval of the loan, and the chairman carries out the board's resolutions. 

Following this are mutual associations responsible for ensuring the health, safety and security of the community. Examples include mutual aid associations of all types, those which are better suited to having limited their scope to a single community, municipality, or province. Further mention must be made of associations for the collective purchase of common supplies, such as potatoes, fuel, and the like, which are bought wholesale by the executive committee, stored in the association hall, and are acquired by community members at a lower cost than if they were purchased retail. Next, this category also encompasses associations that pertain to public safety, like traffic regulations, maintaining peace, street lighting, road maintenance, street watering, and so forth. All these associations, though entirely autonomous in managing their specific affairs, function as committees within the overarching community association, and in turn coordinates their interactions by attending to the matters that they have in common.

Finally, there are also the associations that relate to the highest needs of humankind, to art, religion, and science, such as the church association that has its meeting house, prayer house, or church in the community. The schools located in the community are subordinate to the general community association, grow out of the self-government of the community are entrusted to its care. Nevertheless, the teachers' associations, like all worker associations, do not belong to the community as such, but to the municipality, since there are usually not enough members of that profession in a community to form their own association among themselves.

#### Community Representative

Since in a self-governing society, wherein the formation and enforcement of law are one and the same, there can be no question of any official externally imposed upon the community by the state or the municipality, the community officer has this responsibility and stands at the apex of the community as its executive chief (I). This stands in contrast to the present situation, wherein police authority is usually exercised by the municipal executive, or is even a matter for the state, a system which functions as a secret police for the security of the state and more broadly, the complete paternalistic tutelage of the citizenry. While the justice of the peace and the jurors are elected by the general community assembly, the community representative is elected held by the association committees. Conversely, the cabinet who assists them in executing the resolutions of the community associations, who organize the various tasks among themselves according to the principle of the division of labor, and who oversee all operations are likewise selected through elections held by the respective committees. The community representative is also charged with directing the community's external activities, its relations with other communities, and all other matters the community is integrated with these others into a higher organizational entity, i.e. the municipality.

### Municipality (City)

In a collective municipality, and likewise in a large city consisting of many communities, we once again encounter the three constituent bodies known as: 1) the municipal assembly or city council, 2) the municipal council or city administration and 3) the mayor. While the former authorizes the funds, the municipal council oversees the specific departments of labor, and the mayor executes the resolutions. The division of labor within the municipalities is nevertheless not as strictly delineated as they are in higher administrative divisions. The city councilors also exercise administrative functions, as the business of the municipality is typically handled jointly by mixed committees composed of members drawn from both the municipal assembly and council.

#### Municipal Assembly

The first term of the syllogism, the terminus major (U), is constituted by representatives of the municipality, a body referred to in Switzerland as the "Grand Council" for each canton. As the embodiment of the municipality's general will, this assembly holds legislative power. It drafts the municipal charter, approves taxes for the municipality's common affairs and needs, appoints municipal officials, receives annual financial reports, and so forth, much like the community assembly does. It goes without saying that the municipal representative body is also the product of elections held among all eligible voters within the municipality. The electorate consists of the communities of the municipality. In instances where an electoral district, which is tasked with electing a single municipal representative, is smaller in scope than the corresponding community, the latter does not conduct the election directly, rather it only oversees the process within each individual electoral district.

What does not go without saying is the question of why the general assembly should consist solely of the representatives of the citizenry, rather than the citizens themselves. In Switzerland, even within the smaller cantons, the "Grand Council" is supplanted by a primary assembly that comprises all inhabitants. While this institution, which was even attempted in France during the era of the Convention, ultimately had to be abandoned as unsuitable for a large-scale state, it would nevertheless seem plausible that, within the context of a single municipality, a full assembly of the people, convening much like the assembly on the Pnyx in ancient Athens, could itself make all decisions. But even at this level, the general assembly can scarcely, as is the case in the community, consist of all citizens. Indeed, how could all citizens have the time to deliberate on everything themselves? Each citizen exercises their sovereign rights through the act of voting, freely returning to their own private affairs while entrusting matters of general concern to the people of their choice.

#### Municipal Council

The departments of labor, serving as the second element of municipal life, manage the administration of municipal affairs. The municipal council is formed through elections held by worker associations within the municipality, for in this sphere, insight into and knowledge of specific fields is absolutely indispensable. Thus workers of all trades elect council departments to administer their respective affairs separately, yet these departments also convene as needed to pass resolutions in plenary session.

Thus, within the municipal council, we have a department of education, a department of commerce, a department of agriculture, a department of manufacturing, and so forth. The legal professionals elect a committee to the municipal council that constitutes the court of the municipality. Adjudication and administration belong together, and must once again be unified as they were in the past, so that the arbitrary decision-making (as opposed to decision-making on the basis of law), a charge leveled against administration ever since the separation of the two functions, may finally cease. If all branches of administration are conducted in accordance with the principles of law, then the last vestiges of paternalistic state supervision will also vanish. Under this scheme, the court is for us a branch of administration charged with the administration of private law, whereas the other departments administer the various spheres of public law in the same spirit. Should a legal disputes arise within a certain fields, the department also functions as a specialized disciplinary court. To ensure the legality of their decisions, they may then call upon legal professionals to assist in their deliberations, just as the court, conversely, draws upon jurors from the populace, the very people who elect its municipal delegates. Finally, these social departments also function as disciplinary courts in their respective areas, such as when the school board not only deals with school-related disputes, but also with the personal misconduct of teachers.

#### Mayor

The executive authority, which in the third instance is charged solely with implementing the resolutions of both deliberative municipal bodies, may be vested in the hands of one or more individuals, and in the latter case the work would be divided between them. As the head of the entire structure, the mayor is chosen from an election conducted jointly by city’s worker associations that convene for this specific purpose. This method of election ensures that the municipality’s executive authority will neither blindly follow the dictates of a higher supervisory power, furthering the self-serving agendas of the state’s incumbent rulers, nor be able to transform itself into an absolute ruler over its own fellow citizens.

While this arrangement naturally entails the complete abolition of the external police-style supervision over the municipality, it was nevertheless deemed necessary to retain a form of oversight, exercised by the mayor and their officials, over the city’s various worker associations. Admittedly, the realization has taken hold that the collective affairs of the various estates within civil society are best administered through the mechanisms of self-government. Consequently, bodies known as "Trade Councils" were established, elected directly by the respective estates, specifically the artisans, manufacturers, and merchants, and were organized into corresponding sectoral divisions. They were supposed to serve as mirror images of the magistracy’s own specialized committees, but in reality, they led a merely shadowy existence, for the mayor invariably cast themselves as the fountain of superior wisdom, constantly seeking to infantilize and overrule them. Moreover, perpetual disputes arose between the Trade Council and the magistracy, conflicts in which the former was inevitably bound to come off worse as it possessed absolutely no official authority of its own. And so it perished from its own impotence, whereas in a society governed by reason, by virtue of the self-government of its members, the Council of Trades and the magistracy must constitute one and the same authority, an authority that would certainly not suffer from such a duality.

The mayor, like the magistracy, must act solely as the free head of a free municipality, giving expression to its will without allowing themselves to be swayed by extraneous considerations of any kind, and yet in doing so, they often retain greater influence and a broader sphere of action than any state sovereign. A municipality is, in essence, a state in miniature, just as in the Greek language, the very same term (polis) is used for both "state" and "city." Yet the task at hand is precisely to integrate all these small free states into the greater life of the state. And the freer each municipality is within itself, the more readily will it embrace the free will of the people and reflect it back from its own smaller mirror.

### Province

The association of the municipalities belonging to one and the same regional people completes civil society through the formation of the provincial association. The province constitutes a league of the towns and cities within a specific region, charged with managing its common affairs. The province thus presents itself as the intermediate link between the municipality and the state. It is conceivable that every regional people should possess, much like its own distinct dialect, its own particular forms of law, the continued development of which must be safeguarded.

#### Provincial Assembly

The organizational structure possessed by the municipality must also exist at the provincial level. While it is desirable that a rational provincial order be universally introduced, room must nevertheless be left here for the freedom of self-determination, in accordance with historical necessities. However, the logic of the matter demands, firstly, that the legislative assembly of the province be constituted through the universal suffrage of all its inhabitants. Here, representation through elected delegates becomes an absolute necessity. Just as the municipality drafts its own local ordinances, so too does the provincial assembly formulate its own provincial law. The question may then by raised whether state law takes precedence over provincial law. It is without a doubt that, with regard to all general legal norms, the laws of the state takes precedence first and foremost. Yet, the specific customs and statutes of the provinces may very well coexist as exceptions dictated by the unique character of the province.

Naturally, the funds required for general provincial affairs are authorized by the provincial assembly, just as municipal and community representatives authorize the funds for their respective spheres. In accordance with the general principle of self-governance, every association has the right to levy taxes upon its members and participants to meet its specific needs. The one and only correct tax, let it be stated once and for all, for associations, municipalities, provincials, and the state, is the income tax. It is the only tax, whereas indirect taxation burdens everyone equally, according to whatever means is subject to taxation.

#### Provincial Council

The administration of the province, which by analogy with the municipal council we may term the provincial council, is elected in a manner entirely distinct from the provincial legislative assembly. For while the latter represents the political, civic element of the people, the councils, so precisely named because they rely upon their members' specific counsel, knowledge, and experience, represent the specific, social elements of the various professions. As a result, these councils are not chosen through direct election based on universal suffrage, but through indirect election by members of that profession, drawn from the corresponding worker associations of the municipalities. However, worker election and universal suffrage constitute a contradictory dichotomy only when applied to the same object of political election. In our rational state, this conflict is resolved, as each electoral method possesses its own distinct and proper sphere. Insofar as indirect election by estates represents the social element of the people, this electoral method is highly aristocratic in nature, for its aim is to consistently filter out and select, by process of elimination, the very best individuals.

In our constitution, the provincial council, much like the municipal council, is composed of various departments corresponding to the worker associations of the municipalities. However, the committee responsible for the province's religious affairs, the church council, is by design not permitted to consist exclusively of the clergy. Religion is a matter that concerns everyone, and the preacher is, after all, just a spokesperson appointed by the congregation, a role that can easily be rotated every Sunday. The education council is no longer responsible merely for the administration of elementary education in its respective municipalities, but also for the administration of secondary and higher education, along with specialized trade schools for commerce, agriculture, and the like. As for the provincial court, it serves as the second instance relative to the city court. For if the latter has decided a civil dispute between two private individuals, or in a criminal proceeding, has sentenced a defendant to a penalty, and the losing party or the convicted individual feels that their rights have been infringed upon, then a new legal dispute arises between that individual and the sentencing court wherein the higher provincial court subsequently adjudicates between the two parties. Likewise, the provincial court may serve as the court of first instance when an entire municipality brings a legal action against an individual, or when two municipalities are engaged in a dispute against one another.

#### Governor

The executive power of the provincial resides in the hands of a governor, serving as the head of the provincial, who acts as the presiding officer of the provincial government. However, the governor can by no means be appointed by the State, but must be elected by the legislative and administrative bodies of the provincial in joint session. Just as the execution of all provincial affairs in the civil sphere falls within their purview, so too do they stand at the head of the provincial’s military forces. But these forces do not stand in opposition to the citizen militia, rather they are exactly what constitute it. Thus within the provincial, this military force acts in support of the governor in the implementation of the resolutions passed by the provincial bodies, just as the citizen militia of every municipality and every community is subordinate to the mayor and the community officer, who represent the civil authority. Particularly in times of war, the very purpose for which the army is intended, the community officers, the mayors, and the governors shall organize the able-bodied fighters at the disposal of the head of state.
