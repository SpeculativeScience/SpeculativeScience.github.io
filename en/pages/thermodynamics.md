## Continuum Mechanics

### Density

Density only establishes the general concept of the acoustic relationship since we consider each body simply in its resting state without it yet entering into collision with others. If we relate both bodies to each other, this remains only subjective comparison since it depends our choice of bodies that are compared. The different quantity, which implies a different quality, is the standard by which we measure two qualities against each other. Since quantity is the aspect of externality, i.e. the appearance of quality as the essence existing in itself, quantity itself has a twofold form. Firstly, the mass of the body, and second, quantity of space that this mass occupies, the volume. In pure mechanics, the tacit assumption held that equal masses occupy equal spaces. But even if the same mass occupies the same space, this is only the case if there exists a body that has this specific ratio of mass and volume. But if we have two bodies of different qualities, such as gold and liquid, the specific relationship between mass and volume of the body varies according to the quality. That is, the two different bodies that are identical in mass are differ in volume, or conversely, that two different bodies that are identical in volume are differ in mass. Quality is thus the ratio that unites and determines how mass and volume change with respect to each other. 

In this measurement of densities against each other, liquid is often assumed to be the unit, and it is irrelevant whether we equate the weights or the volumes in the comparison to determine the inequality of the other side. It turns out that a cubic foot of gold is nineteen times heavier than a cubic foot of liquid, or that a pound of gold occupies nineteen times less space than a pound of liquid. Thus, the quantitative ratio 1:19 applies here, and we already know from the Logic that this is also always something qualitative. We can just as well say here: quality determines quantity as that quantity determines quality, they interact with each other. Everything depends on explaining the nature of this relationship, so first of all, it is clear that a mass of gold is compressed into a space nineteen times smaller than the same mass of liquid. Likewise, this also means that liquid is in a state nineteen times more expanded than the gold. What we saw in gas as an alternation of compression and expansion in one and the same body is now distributed into two separate individualities. But if the gold is nineteen times more compressed than the liquid, that means nothing other than that at one time there are nineteen points. One point, and then one point are nineteen points, the nineteen are penetrated into one, the one is pulled apart into nineteen. In other words, the impermeability of matter is most obviously refuted by density. This relationship of greater or lesser permeability of matter is the truth of density, as the manner in which density manifests itself. The less space the same weight occupies, the denser the body, whereas the more space it occupies, the less dense. 

The difference in the qualities of matter vary by different degrees of extension or intension since it is precisely in degree, as in the Logic, that the quantitative becomes the qualitative. Therefore, one should not really speak of the different ratios of the points of liquid and gold, since points are not spatially extended and so do not actully exist in them, and one would run the risk of presupposing atomism through this expression. By differing in greater elasticity or laxity, without there being any need to think of material differences in quantity, so the intensity of quality has a quantitatively different appearance, without also needing to invoke a greater or smaller number of atoms. This transformation of the quantitative by the qualitative goes so far that, while in gravity the energy of attraction and repulsion is inversely proportional to the squares of the distances from the center, now compression and expansion are not determined by spatial distance, but are equally strong in all parts of every body. This is because with the materialized form, as the center that and resides equally in every part of the body, no longer needs to be determined by the periphery. That this immanent form is not only compared with another, but that the comparison of the densities of different bodies in the concept becomes a real comparison of the bodies themselves as cohesion.

### Cohesion

While in density, the immanent form, as the center holding all points together, only signified the victory of quality over quantity, in cohesion, each point, in relation to all others, has made itself into this principle of unity in order to, as the center, maintain the body in all dimensions as a coherent whole so that all points resist any attempt at separation made from outside. We can call such a state of matter solidity or rigidity, and this applies to those bodies that are in the aggregate state of solidity, which are primarily called solid bodies. But even this qualitative intrinsic being, which gives all material parts of the body a greater affinity with one another than with external bodies, has only a quantitative appearance. There is no such thing as absolute solidity, even a solid body can only resist external forces up to a certain degree, and once this degree is exceeded, it breaks apart and falls apart into pieces. We can therefore call this quantitative coherence. The extent to which it maintains its independence against external influences depends on the body's internal structure, on the interconnection of its individual parts through its form.

The more the solidity of a body decreases, the less it coheres with itself, and the more it disperses, due to the inherent nature of its form. If we first consider the fluid body, is its intrinsic characteristic that its parts do not cohere very strongly, or they are not as coherent as solid bodies are. But just as the coherence of solid bodies is a spectrum, so is the incoherence of the parts of fluid. There may be some degree of coherence in the fluid, but they can also separate just as easily, even without much external force. And since they lack strong cohesion with one another, they always seek to cohere with parts of other bodies, which is the property of adhesion. It also rises higher at the edge of a glass tube than in the center, because it prefers the foreign matter to its own. Oil also exhibits this adhesion, clinging to everything, making stains everywhere. Liquids do not always adhere to to other liquids, if they are of the same polarity or nonpolarity they mix, but if they are of different polarities, like liquid and oil, they separate from one another. One might think that bodies in the third state of matter, gaseous bodies, would have even stronger adhesion than liquid bodies. Indeed, their dispersibility is taken to its highest peak, occurring in all three dimensions, thus also counteracting gravity. But precisely through this, gas also emancipates itself from the heavy matter to which liquid, despite its horizontal position, remains subject. The fluid therefore exhibits passive adhesion, the first negation of cohesion. But gas, as this element that creeps in everywhere and therefore leads back to identity, no longer has passive adhesion. It transforms the foreign to which it clings into its own substance. Gas is therefore the negation of the negation of cohesion, it restores to itself the cohesion that it dissolved in others. As active adhesion, it is inherence: energetic adhesion of its own parts to one another when it condenses in the process of evaporation. Since, however, it also expands again in the formation of rain, it alternately diminishes and increases its cohesion within itself. Cohesion has thus passed from the state of rest and into that of movement, and this is qualitative cohesion.

In qualitative cohesion, the influence of the external body, the force exerted on an internally cohesive body, has itself become the source of the immanent activity of the yielding body, its cohesion disturbed. Qualitative cohesion is the negation of cohesion itself, that the body still displays its particular, specific cohesion — activity appears in the midst of passivity. In cohesion, form is that which establishes the spatial connection of the material parts. If this connection of the whole is abolished by the magnitude of the force, form reveals itself through the specific connection of the remaining parts that form a new whole. Since the formative elements of space can, in a sense, be regarded as its qualitative differences, form, in the collapse of its cohesion, reveals the qualitative determination of space according to which it formed the inner connection of the body's parts. 

The first class of bodies is therefore those that disperse in the form of a point, such as glass or cast iron, here therefore, only point is connected to point. And since the point is precisely the negation of connection, pure discreteness, such a body appears to be the least coherent. Water, which splinters into liquid points, i.e., drops, therefore belongs to this category. However, the point here is no longer the mathematical point, but the material point, as a small sphere. This, as a material whole, has also retained its connection, its cohesion, albeit more as the intrinsic being of form, as an abstract being-for-itself in a small spheroid. The coming outside of itself of form in its security, on the other hand, is secondly linearity, this is the form in which the bodies to which we attribute toughness shatter. While the brittle remains in itself as a point, the tough stretches its cohesion outwards, it maintains it in one dimension by having to sacrifice the other dimensions. Thirdly, bodies that yield to the impression of surface form, such as gold and metals in general, do not actually lose their cohesion at all, or at least only very relatively. They maintain themselves as kinematics and surface: and depth is also negated only relatively, only quantitatively, in that they gain in extension what they lose in thickness, they merely become thinner, as is the case to a high degree with gold leaf. We call this malleability or extensibility. Such bodies change, thus, only the position of their parts, without abandoning their connection: whereas ductile bodies still diverge into several linear bodies, and brittle ones even diverge into an infinite number of points. In extensible bodies, the negation of material connection has become identical with its persistence, and this is vibration.

The reciprocal transformation of softness and hardness into one another as relative concepts is vibration itself. In this alternation of both activities, cohesion changes, and density with it, so that both concepts are restored. Compression and expansion are a play of forces established by the inner activity of the form. When the negativity of position, as an inner change of position, is abolished, and position is restored, movement returns to rest. Since the two states of greater contraction or greater extension are no longer merely distributed between two bodies, whether we compare them, as in density, or they compare themselves to one another, as in cohesion, it is the inner structure of one and the same body that reciprocally negates its material position through contraction in order to regain it through expansion. Since in this way the ideality of matter immediately reverts to its reality, the ideal principle already powerfully breaks into nature, and thought triumphs over matter in a certain sense, yet this victory is very short-lived. The external appearance, the being-for-other of this ideality of matter is however, as the transformation of material space into material time, a trembling of the entire materiality. Since this trembling is now a movement of the concrete body, it is also bound to the conditions of time and gradually returns to rest, as material time then transforms back into material space. This activity of form, not only confined to the inner space of the body but also appearing in the succession of time, is the expression of vibration in sound.

### Sound

Mechanical contact has a dynamic effect when a body makes a sound, the vibration that escapes its body then encounters other bodies surrounding it. As it trembles, it also causes others in contact with it to tremble. The communication of sound, however, differs substantially depending on the cohesion of the body it interacts with, since each reacts dynamically in its own way. The more brittle a body is, the less it will be able to tremble and sound since the internal structure of its parts interrupts the signal. By contrast, continuous bodies more easily allow the transmission of sound. Metals and stretched fibers are capable of being quite loud, gas and liquid less so because of their fluidity and the mobility of their parts. A pile of sand least of all, because its points are connected entirely loosely, brittle glass, on the other hand, much more so, because its points are firmly connected. However, if the dispersibility of the gas is prevented, for example, by a whip that quickly cuts through it, it acquires a strong, shrill sound, as the separated parts come together again just as quickly. The qualitative determination of the sound, which is produced by the diversity of the pressed form, as this reveals its individuality in its trembling, often suffers accidental modifications, so the vibrations of a body can even be irregular due to its internal structure.

This regularity, however, is not a mere recurrence of the vibrations of one and the same space filling at one and the same time. Each vibration is different, whether different materials vibrate, or the same materials have different thicknesses, lengths, or tensions. But if in two bodies of the same material, this material also has the same thickness, length, and tension, the sound is identical and in each body, it then also has the same number of vibrations. Since unequal numbers of vibrations at one and the same time produce entirely different tones, it is here again the quantitative that produces entirely qualitative determinations. One and the same quality and form will therefore, in its successive expressions, produce a completely different qualitative tone by repeating this expression less or more often, faster or slower, according to number as the measure of time, the tones differ from one another according to their pitch or depth. The longer, or thicker, or slacker the strings are, the fewer vibrations the body makes in the same time, the more tense, or thinner, or shorter, the more. Higher frequency vibrations indicate a higher tone whereas lower frequency vibrations indicate a lower tone. Therefore, the principle of sound is time, and for this very reason it can be called materialized time. 

In the musical scale, notes exhibit distinct qualitative determinations relative to one another. Those whose vibrations are produced by simpler numerical relationships are the harmonic ones, in contrast to the dissonant ones, whose relationship of vibrations is more complex. Here again, the quantitative aspect results in entirely qualitative differences. For example, if the first struck note is called the fundamental, then the second, sixth, and seventh are more dissonant than the third, fourth, fifth, and octave. This is simply because the vibrations of the latter, as Pythagoras already knew, arise from the four simplest numerical ratios formed by the first five numbers 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5, while the other tones have larger numbers as members of their ratios. The simplest ratio, 1:2, which produces the octave, does not actually produce harmony, meaning that this ratio is so large that it can be considered an identity where only the difference between pitch and bass is present. The ratio 2:3 then forms the fifth, 3:4 the fourth, and 4:5 the third. These tonal differences can also be heard on one and the same string, the so-called monochord, if it is divided according to these numerical ratios. In this case, the entire string strikes the fundamental, the supporting points are at rest, and the adjacent parts vibrate in opposite directions. While the major triad is formed by the root, major third, and fifth (1 : 4/5 : 2/3), the minor triad is formed by the root, minor third, and fifth (1 : 5/6 : 2/3). These three tones are conceived in such a way that they form a completely dialectical totality, in that the fifth forms the opposite of the octave, as the relationship of identity, while the third, as the concrete unity of both, is supposed to be the mediation of the opposite. Arithmetically, this resolution of the opposite is supposed to be most completely contained in the multiplication of the two thirds being exactly identical with the opposite. Finally, the melody is the balancing of the harmonic and the dissonant. Now, when the form in sound has escaped from the vibration of matter rushes back to it, the vibrations gradually become slower and shorter until they have returned to their previous state of rest. However, since the quantity of sound is determined by the magnitude of the force applied to the body, it does not always remain at the momentary, formal negation of separation in matter. With greater energy and multiplication of vibrations, the vibration becomes more effective and contests the cohesion of the body, thereby making the ideality of matter permanent in the form of heat.

## Heat

### Temperature

As far as the concept of heat is concerned, first, it must unite cohesion and vibration into a higher unity, since the outward-directed activity of sound, which again proceeds without trace within the body, now simultaneously maintains itself as the inner force of vibration. The superficial trembling of matter in sound now becomes the actual softening of the same, as in vibration. But what in vibration, as softening, was merely compression by external pressure, to which hardness then followed as reaction and outward expansion, where these two states, as internal and external activity, are no longer in alternation in heat, rather the softening of the material is itself the outward expansion. Cohesion does not restore itself from the pressure it endured from vibration, the density actually undergoes a permanent change through heat. However, we can already explain the destructive moment of heat from its expanding activity, which if taken too far has a destructive effect. Also, from the stated concept of heat, it is clear that it is not a substance (caloric) either. 

Although heat always influences the density of bodies by generally making them lighter, it does not always expand them, but sometimes contracts them, e.g. clay, by driving out its moisture and thus reducing its volume, and likewise, heating leather causes it shrink. However, this also depends on the degree of temperature, i.e. the degree of expansion, whether the bodies are only quantitatively changed in their cohesion, or whether, with a certain amount of heating, their state becomes qualitatively different. This is the change in the phase of matter brought about by heat, which is best illustrated by the example of liquid, a model example in this respect. When the temperature in celcius has dropped to zero, the liquid becomes solid, becomes ice. The fact that liquid expands shortly before freezing is an exception to the expansion of heat since in this case the removal of heat causes expansion. This is explained, however, by the fact that ice is specifically less dense than liquid, thus taking up more space. Once the liquid has reached 100 degrees, however, it becomes steam, and here the expanding power of heat is completely revealed. The movements, the ripples of the liquid, become increasingly stronger the closer it approaches the boiling point. 

The negation of heat, i.e. cold, is itself heat, only a lesser degree of it. Like liquid, most other bodies also change their physical state through heat: metals are melted by heat, diamond immediately changes from solid to elastic liquid by evaporating. Gases in particular, because they lack great cohesion anyway, are significantly expanded by heat, even to the point that it becomes a source of power. Absolute cold would not only be absolute rest, but also the reduction of contraction to a point, as in the absolute attraction of astronomy: absolute heat would also be the absolute repulsion and dispersion of matter into infinite space. We can thus continue the moments of continuity and discreteness, through attraction and repulsion, contraction and expansion, softness and hardness, and warmth and cold. Precisely because in warmth, the softness and the expansion of vibration have become one and the same, the moments of heat, like those of gravity, maintain equilibrium. The more the form, through softening, has attained control over its own materiality within, the more it behaves negatively toward the extraneous matter through its expansion. The softening of the body through heat, however, is not a destruction of its inner qualitative form. Even if a heated wax figure loses its shape, this is only the destruction of a purely spatial, not dynamic, form mechanically imposed on the wax from the outside.

### Heat Transfer

The heading of bodies leads us, secondly, to the relationship of heat to the outside. As a relationship of finitude, heat must naturally also have an external cause. However, this is either the original or a derived one. As far as the original cause is concerned, it must be homogeneous with heat. Now, if this, through the dissolution of rigidity, is the ideality of the material, but already as materialized identity, then the first cause of heat can be none other than light, i.e., the pure ideality and identity of matter. While light, immersed in darkness, loses its luminosity through contact with it and absorption into it, it acquires, as an equivalent, the ability to heat, while in itself being cold. Just as heat is an intensification of sound, so is the intensification of heat, which progresses to the extreme of the complete dissolution of material separation, plasma, from which, since it is also the ideality of matter, the immaterial abstract identity, which is light, emerges. Thus, if light is, on the one hand, the generating cause of heat, then, on the other hand, it is simultaneously, in the cycle, its generated effect. However, in this second form, it loses something of its pure identity by accepting slight modifications from the individualized matter of which it is the dissolution. Now, once that ideality of matter, which we call heat, has taken place in a body, it strives to propagate it, to transfer it to other bodies. This is the communication of heat. Just as the vibrations of sound are communicated to other bodies, so too does the expansion of heat naturally have an expanding effect on other bodies, since they are all connected, in contact, and interacting with one another. The warmer body disrupts the cohesion of its cooler neighbor until they have reached equilibrium. This is the second, derived cause of heat; and here, in addition to the inert communication, such as mere contact, a more active mechanical cause of external heat, such as pressure, impact, and friction, can be added, because all of these entail a disruption, thus a dissolution of cohesion. The internal work that arises as a result of external pressure is heat. 

### Heat Capacity

In what has just been said about better or worse conductors of heat this leads us, thirdly, to the fact that every body demonstrates its individuality when heated by others in that it absorbs heat in different ways, this could also be deduced from the aforementioned change of states of matter. There are, in fact, bodies that absorb and release heat more quickly than others. Good conductors of heat include metals, while poor conductors, apart from wood, also include glass, liquid, and gas. The former are continuous, coherent, the latter are brittle, loosely connected. The parallel between sound and heat can be drawn here as well. But even if bodies that come into contact cancel out the inequality of their temperatures relative to one another, even if they are made of completely different materials, they require a different amount of heat to reach the same temperature. For example, liquid must be heated much more than metal to exhibit the same heat level; gas much more than liquid. This is called heat capacity or specific heat. Metal therefore has less heat capacity than liquid because it is heated more by the same amount of heat. Thus, in the case of a change of phase of matter, one can say: ice has less heat capacity than liquid, liquid less than vapor. The excess heat in ice therefore disappears into the liquid, and the excess heat in liquid disappears in the vapor. Conversely, when vapors are transformed into liquid, liquid into ice, the temperature of the contracted phase of matter increases because it has less capacity. 

Heat capacity and density are intimately related to each other because density indicates the degree of compactness of the matter, which is dissolved by heat. However, the relationship between density and specific heat is usually inverse: i.e., the greater the density of a body, the less heat it requires to reach a certain degree of heat. This is easily explained by the fact that the activity of breaking up cohesion is replaced by mass. The one moment is, in a sense, the equivalent of the other, just as mass and distance represent each other in the lever. Therefore, for the same heating, less heat activity is required where a larger mass is present, because this mass multiplies the imparted activity through its material compactness. The smaller mass, on the other hand, can only be brought to the same phase of heat through greater activity because its expansion offers fewer points of contact. But nothing proves the victory of form over matter more than heat capacity, because here the body, even in its dissolution, asserts its individuality.

Heat, as the form that brings all parts of the body into flux, has freed it from the rigidity of matter and determined it independently of gravity. Heat no longer escapes matter, like sound, but has, within matter, made itself master over it. Just as heat combines the moments of vibration, so it also combines those of cohesion into one undifferentiated unity, as the various forms of spatial filling—point, line, and surface are fused into the fluid punctuality of the drop. Finally, that determination of the material which we found to be its first particularity, the specification of gravity, is also brought under the power of heat, since this changes the weight of bodies. Since in this way all moments of the individualizing individuality are held in the unity of form, this has become the principle that gives birth to total form. All qualitative determinations of nature, which we previously saw in their separation, are combined in total individuality as phases of matter.

## Phase of Matter

### Gas (Air)

In negating the individual through the transfer of heat, gas is indeed the self-preserving, universal phase of matter. But since the process of negating is a sublation of individual particularities, it also turns this negation against itself because it is itself a particular. However, in this negation of itself, it must retain the qualitative particularity from which its activity flows, it initially negates itself only mechanically or spatially. It is indifferent to its own spatiality, and this completes the explanation of its changed density. Because the gas transforms the individual into its substance through evaporation, thereby acquiring a greater mass, it does not change its volume, and this contains the third of its properties to be considered, compressibility. If, for example, a gas is admitted into a vessel already filled with gas, there is still room for it without the gas needing to occupy a larger space. Conversely, it can be forced to occupy a larger space with the same mass, thus becoming rarefied, e.g. by pumping the gas out of the vessel. If the gas has been compressed by an external force, it reacts out of its own power against this demand by, following its nature, striving to expand again in order to return to its normal state. But if the compression has arisen through the gas's own consuming activity, only an external stimulus can cause it to expand again. It waits for it to thin out, because here it is compression that flows from its nature. The living activity of gas consists in this alternation of distraction and compression, of lesser and greater density. They are, on the dynamic level, that which in mechanics manifested itself as attraction and repulsion. But while in mechanics these two moments were always in absolute equilibrium, gas is this higher activity, when the momentarily successful attempt has been made to give one moment predominance over the other, even from the loss of this equilibrium, it returns to it.

Since in the compressibility of gas, it is itself both the negating and the negated, it will, as the negation of the negation, be not only the negation of its spatial determinacy, but also of its qualitative particularity. Since this is the determinacy of universality, the gas negates the negative universality, which it what it is itself. In other words, in negating, the gas only negates the side of its universality. In fact, compressibility strives from extension to contraction, as much as possible, toward one point. This negativity, reduced to a point and thus individualized, is plasma. This is the dialectical transition from gas to plasma in the concept. The pneumatic lighter is this remarkable speculative phenomenon, that this dialectical transition also takes place genetically in nature, as we have exceptionally allowed for a narrower cycle. Because the gas is compressed into an unexpanded point by a piston, a spark is created that ignites the tinder. The plasma dormant in the gas is thus the living individualization of the gas. 

### Plasma (Fire) 

As the universal individuality, plasma is firstly the source of light, which is however already tainted with its opposite, with particular individuality, the combustible substance. Plasma is the light that originates in matter, clings to matter, and thus each kind of matter burns differently, eminating a specific frequency of light. Since gas is the possibility of the dissolution of things, their gradual consumption, plasma by contrast is the individual, sudden consumption, so plasma has gas as its condition and precondition. Removed from the gas, under the gas pump, covered with sacks and sand, plasma is extinguished instantly. As the change, the passing away of rigidity, as the transformation of its being into nothingness, plasma is materialized time or becoming.

Secondly, plasma thus does not possess the independence and substantiality that gas had. Plasma can only be understood as a moment of opposition, as a process of dissolution, as materialized negation. Therefore, it requires a basis or ground in order to exist, an other in which it is and on which it is dependent, in that it also disappears with the disappearance of its other. But just as light is the simply ideal but permanent identity of nature, which nevertheless allows all the multiplicity of material externality to exist equally. Plasma, as the individualized light concentrated into one point, is the real identity, which no longer allows this externality into which it immerses itself to exist. In this way, the further we progress in nature, the ideality of light becomes ever more real, because the otherness of nature is increasingly overcome. Just as plasma comes to the individual externally, by igniting something by bringing it close to an already burning plasma, so this mutability of things can also break out from within them, without anything already being burning, through spontaneous combustion, which is merely a greater intensity of self-luminosity. However, bodies can still ignite of their own accord, because finitude, negativity, and mutability are their very nature. Finally, materials can ignite through friction. For since friction, as previously described in mechanics, is the beginning of the return of motion to rest, of inertia to gravity, heat and light can also escape from it.

Thirdly, this absolute negativity of plasma negates itself, just as the negativity of gas also applied to itself. Since this negation is no longer universal and gradual, but individual and spontaneous, the plasma thereby negates itself in its entire materiality and collapses helplessly as soon as it has consumed the last bit of fuel. By contrast, the gas, which was empirically negated only in its spatiality, returned to its original state after its compression. With the final extinction of the plasma, however, the opposition it evoked in its process is also extinguished. In extinguishing itself, the living contrast initially becomes a pacified one, in that the one moment, the burning, transforms into a gaseous vaporous mass as smoke, while the burned substance transforms into the indifferent rigidity of ash. In this way, the living contrast of the plasma process has been transformed into its opposite, the indifferent, disintegration of matter. This indifferent phase of matter that has taken the place of opposition is the liquid.

### Liquid (Water)

From the indifferent difference of liquid, it follows first that it does not possess the compressibility of gas, to completely restore itself after ompression or expansion. Liquids are completely powerless against these forces, they either cannot be compressed at all, or only to an extremely small degree, taking the shape of whatever container it is poured in. When it begins the process of contraction and expansion from within itself, it dissolves everything individual without a trace while also preserving its individuality in its being, easily taking on new properties of taste, smell, and color that it did not originally have. As the indifferent middle between two states of matter, solid and gaseous, liquid possesses the greatest ease of being transformed into these extremes, to be frozen or evaporated. It is only when it has been brought into these states does it have the ability to expand and burst the vessel in which it has been enclosed. However, it is brought into these states by the presence or absence of its other external opposite, plasma, i.e. by adding or removing heat.

While gas is extremely viscous, and clings to itself, the liquid allows itself to be dispersed by everything and to be broken up into an indefinite number of drops without reciprocally reuniting with itself. This relationship to otherness is in the nature of liquid that it separates itself from itself on its own and seeks, through spontaneous contact, to join to anything but itself. Liquids have a true desire to insinuate itself into others, but not, like gas, to corrode them and transform them into their substance. Instead, liquids abandon their own substantiality to assimilate itself to the phase next to it, taking on the qualities of this other substance, i.e. the liquid soaks whatever it touches. If there are exceptions to this, since liquid does not have the affinity for oils, fats, and wax that it demonstrates for most other things, then this is again only a sign of nature's impotence in not being able to consistently maintain the conceptual definiteness it imposes on its forms in all cases. Liquids may also find such flammable liquids antipathetic. But the other thing that liquids are drawn to is the solid toward which it gravitates, the last phase of matter.

### Solid (Earth)

Finally, the solid, as the ground from which the three other moments are supported, as the rigid core to which they adhere, is now the materialized totality of the three dimensions, in that its mass expands calmly in all directions from the center. The three other phases, which transform into each other in ceaseless movement, only seek to eliminate the one-sidedness of their direction. While liquids, seeking the deepest, depends on the surface, and plasma, seeking the highest, depends on the line, and diverge as extremes. However, gas approaches totality, albeit only in an ideal way, since it contains the extremes intermediate forms, and seeks the center in all directions, and embraces it as an enclosing surface. But the solid, as the phase of matter that encompasses all the others within itself, has itself become a truly individual element, in that it is not only the bearer of the others, but because contains the possibility of them and constantly strives to actualize these possibilities within itself. Because the solid forms this totality of determinations that are opposed and contradictory to one another, they cannot remain calmly together in this contradiction. They therefore resolve this contradiction by constantly transforming into one another, the chemical process in which the solid, as ground, becomes the active being by allowing its moments to appear, abandons its one-sidedness of being a mere ground by also being the ground of itself.
