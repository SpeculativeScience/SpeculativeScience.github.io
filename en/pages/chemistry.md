## Mixtures

The first process of chemistry is still abstract, it is not yet a complete inference, but is only a judgment in which two homogeneous bodies combine directly through fusion and form a result. As the copula, the indifference of these already mutually indifferent entities, however, in their own peculiarity, also form a certain difference. But because the difference is superficial, the process has a similarity to electricity, but differs from it in that the result is always already a concrete body, even if it only belongs to one side of the difference. Mixtures, however, have a similarity to magnetism in that their poles, as well as their point of indifference, belong to the same general determination. However, they also differ from it in that the moments are already qualitatively particular corporealities. Since there are three different levels of bodies, according to color, smell, and taste, the object of the mixture, to which its other object still differs, is also threefold: and therefore there are amalgams of metals, of acids or combustible bodies, and salts or neutral bodies. The compounds have compared to the bodies from which they result, only mechanical differences: cohesion, density, hardness. However, the color also changes, but not according to the ratio of the compounds. Likewise, their fusibility changes, proving that we are dealing not with mere mechanical compounds, but with chemical interpenetrations. Copper and lead turn yellow as brass. Gold and silver, amalgamated, lose their density as they fuse with each other. Hydrochloric acid and nitric acid do not dissolve gold individually, only in their combination as aqua regia. Finally, an acid with two bases, or a base with two acids, are called double salts, where in being an amalgam of mutually indifferent elements, a real combination of different elements is realized.

## Chemical Reactions

### Galvanic Corrosion

Galvanic corrosion begins the true cycle of chemical processes because in it the metal, which as the posited product of nuclear fusion, forms the presupposed object of the first. To now speak of the object of the galvanic corrosion, the metal begins the cycle because it is the body of identity which, as an electrical and thermal conductor, is still indifferent to the specific differences of electricity. Despite this indifference, the metal is also, as a particular metal, tensed or reactive against other metals. Thus, two metals are indeed in electrical opposition to each other. However, this tension has a more real consequence here than electricity, namely, a chemical reaction. 

However, this opposition of the metals is not the original form of galvanic corrosion. The Italian physician Galvani discovered an opposition in the spinal nerves and the corresponding muscle of a dead frog when they were brought into contact with copper and iron, in which they then twitched. Volta discovered that it was the opposition of the metals that produced these twitches. However, different liquids combined with a metallic body can also generate an electric current. The original phenomenon of the galvanic process produced by Volta was a series of different metal plates, such as copper and zinc connected by salt water. The excitation of the activity uses water as the neutral, indifferent element, and hydrogen as the differentiating element. Since in such a voltaic chain or galvanic battery the two electrical poles are distributed to the extremes of the column, magnetism and electricity are directly linked here. Thus Oersted found that a voltaic column exerts the same effect on the magnetic needle of rotating the poles west and east. If the medium of the galvanic process is a stronger electrolyte than water, the effectiveness of the galvanic battery becomes all the stronger.

Secondly, as regards the activity of the galvanic process, it is the same as that of electricity, namely in that each side seeks to establish its difference in the other in order to integrate itself with it and to derive from it the definiteness it lacks. The active agent of this process is the air, without which the voltage cannot exist. Therefore, in the rarefied air of the air pump, the electric current decreases considerably. However, since metals are abstractly indifferent to each other, they cannot initially bring their superficial differences into voltage, but require an indifferent medium in which they can first differentiate themselves. This passive medium of the process is water, the element of neutrality, which has overcome its opposite within itself. The metals, by entering into the electrical opposite, are represented it chemically in the separation of water. They are chemical elements, to which they include not only these two, but also nitrogen and carbon, and then all metals, sulfur, phosphorus, etc. However, the galvanic process does not stop at the separation of the physical element, but after this element has interpreted itself into the opposition of active and passive, of combustible and combustible, namely oxygen and hydrogen, it now imparts this opposition to the metals that initiated the entire activity. The metals, too, not only experience the electrical voltage, but, thirdly, a chemical result also occurs in them. Oxygen, as the combustible element, seeks its substance at the metallic pole where it develops. It tears the zinc out of its indifference and burns it in a wet way. From solidity, it becomes brittle or combustible, i.e. it calcifies.

The penetration of oxygen and metal, an oxide, can then have various degrees, from oxide to peroxide. The fact that there are metals that then also form a compound with hydrogen, the hypergenized metals, is an inconsistency of nature, as we have often noted. The simplest phenomenon of the oxidation process is the rusting of iron in water or moist air. But on the one hand, it is also the result of the galvanic process. As oxidation, a compound, it is nevertheless simultaneously associated with a dissolution, namely of the element. Even if we can distinguish chemical processes in their stages according to the contrast between combination and dissolution, each stage is nevertheless a totality, the connection of both activities, in that either, as here, where the elements dissolve, concrete bodies are combined. Conversely, as in deoxidation, elements are combined and concrete bodies are dissolved, or both moments present themselves in concrete bodies, as in salt metathesis.

But what appears as a result in the galvanic reaction is also found in nature in the form of oxides. These immediate oxides are, like most of the semimetals or metalloids, maintained in a regulative state with great difficulty and often exclusively by means of an amalgam with mercury. Even for some minerals it is possible to discover a metallic base in them by removing its bond with oxygen, such as calcium oxide (lime), barium sulfate (barite), silicon dioxide (silica), etc. The alkalis also belong here through galvanism, discovered a metallic base in them. Because the metal has been torn from its inert indifference by oxidation, it is now capable of becoming the moment of a new totality. Thus the oxide can either be the precondition or result of a new process. The noble metals, however, are those which, due to their low chemical reactivity, are more resistant to corrosion and so are more capable of maintaining their regulative state. The base metals are much more volatile and susceptible to this process, and the semi-metals even more so because they cannot retain their regulative form at all, oxidizing near instantaneously just by being exposed to the air. 

### Combustion

The second of the actual chemical reactions, combustion, now proceeds from where the first ended. The oxidized metal has attained the possibility of combustion, and this possibility must now be transformed into reality. However, fire here does not remain an abstract phase of matter, but is materialized fire, the burning body that exerts the effect of combustion on the oxide. The opposition between the combustible and the combusting, which was previously evident in water as the chemical elements of hydrogen and oxygen, now becomes an opposition between actual bodies. While the oxide, as combustibility, forms the passive chemical basis, which already resulted from the previous process, the combusting body, as the active acid, is only produced in combustion. Just as the radical of the oxide was the indifferent element, which was only brought to difference and brittleness by galvanic corrosion, so does combustion begins with the other, immediately opposite radical, which directly belongs to this brittleness and combustibility, that is, sulfur, phosphorus, etc. As the medium of air is decomposed by fire into the different, combustible oxygen and the abstract indifferent, combusted element, nitrogen, the oxygen combines with the radicals, which, precisely through this combustion, become acids, in order to then, in turn, burn the bases. Water also belongs to this as the mediator, since the acid, diluted by water and exposed to air, increases its activity. But combustion then also affects the oxides. The opposition between the oxide and the acid is forced into a contradiction by fire. The oxides become caustic and the acids corrosive. Neither can maintain itself in this violent state if one does not allow itself to be saturated by the other, but rather integrates its difference with its surroundings until it is neutralized. 

### Neutralization

Since the passive combustible and the active combustible have become concrete bodies that cannot endure the contradiction in which they find themselves, they abolish their opposition and collapse into the neutral product, the salt. Their opposite is inherently an identity, for the oxide has the moment of activity in oxygen and the acid in its radical that of receptivity. They therefore no longer require a third element that is the necessary condition for their combination, they independently combine themselves with each other in the product. The elements also no longer initiate the activity, but are simply purposed as the means for the formation of the salt crystal. In particular, water serves the salts in their formation because it is related to it. Water is the abstract salt because it self-ionizes, containing both moments of acid and base within itself. It functions as an acid when it combines with a base, and it functions as a base when it combines with an acid. And as a medium, water also plays an active role in salt formation when it freezes and crystallizes as ice.

The salts themselves, however, tend sometimes to one side, sometimes to the other, forming basic, acidic, and neutral salts. As the salts themselves lie on one side or the other, they have fallen from their totality into exclusive, one-sided bodies. They have become a result that cannot inspire itself as a true totality from within itself, but rather are the precondition for new processes, which represent new ratios of salts, compounds, and separations of them, until finally the radicals themselves become products. The principle of all these processes is salt metathesis.

## Salt Metathesis

### Ion Exchange

In ion exchange, neutrality is in conflict with itself as the two salts are dissolved in water. If the acid of one has a greater chemical affinity to the base of the other than to its own and vice versa, then the acids exchange bases with each other. Thus, nothing comes out of the process other than what was already there, namely two salts. But if a certain quantity of an acid saturates the entire series of alkalis, each according to a certain quantity, then every other acid in its certain quantity saturates all alkalis in the same ratio. In whatever ratio two bases are in, they are also required for the saturation of another acid of the same ratio. The acids have the same saturation capacity for all bases, or stated differently, the quantity of hydroxide required to saturate a given acid must always contain the same amount of hydrogen ions. The two neutral salts dissolved in water thus form two new neutral salts of a different chemical species. The relationship in which different bodies combine with one and the same amount of a given substance is the same for their combinations with other bodies.

### Oxides and Acids

The salt of the preceding process now comes into contact with a different body. To free an oxide, one must bring into contact with the salt in which it is bound another, free oxide, to which the acid of the salt has a greater affinity than to the first. The acid thereby releases the latter in order to enter into the new compound with the second oxide. Conversely, if one wishes to free an acid, one must cause another acid to release the oxide to which it is bound. Here, the process of salt metathesis is initiated between salt and acid, as before between salt and oxide.

### Autocatalysis

In order to finally recreate the radicals, the original prerequisites of chemical processes, from the oxides and acids, and thus make them the ultimate results of the chemical processes, the greater affinities between acids or oxides on the one hand and radicals on the other must be exploited. For if one relates an oxide to a metal, an acid to a combustible substance, the metal can in turn have a greater affinity to the oxygen of the oxide, and the combustible substance to that of the acid: so that the oxygens seize the free radicals and transform them into oxides and acids, while they abandon their previous radicals, chemical separation also produces a new chemical compound. The cycle of chemical processes is thereby closed since the prerequisite from which the first ring of the chain arose has now also become the result of the last ring, which in turn intervenes in the first. But because the beginning, middle, and end of the process still fall apart as a series of independent realities, and each stage of it requires new external conditions. Its imperfection is therefore that the interlocking of the rings, which is necessary in the Idea itself, is not yet a connection of the actual forms, these still form a multiplicity of individuals that disappear and reappear.

That it is now one and the same individual that passes through these stages as a result of the process and also ignites them through itself, through its own power, is the concept of life. In the organism, purpose is the activity that maintains itself in its product, the form that does not perish in its representations, but rather reduces them all to moments of its existence. The concept thus ceases to be the merely inner in-itself of forms, it is the present idea of ​​nature born from its reality. Nature has thus attained its concept, is no longer the otherness of the idea: or at least, in this otherness, already the beginning of a return from it. In life, all differences are now contained in the unity and universality of form, and reciprocally generate and annihilate one another within it. The one individual is its own presupposition and result since it alone persists in the change of its particularities.
