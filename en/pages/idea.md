# Idea

## Life

```
U ⧁ U ( P ( I ) ) 

Soul ⧁ Body 
```

The Idea of ​​Life, as the Idea only having just revealed itself from objectivity, is still connected to the object itself. Life stands partially in objectivity and partially in the Idea, which is the reason why it is the Idea in the form of individuality. The overarching subjectivity, which presents itself in life as the purpose that realizes itself in reality, is fully expressed in the object. Life only exists as living individuals, and this is the conceptual limitation of life in general. In the individual living being, although encompassing the universal concept within itself, is nevertheless not adequate to it and is thereby engaged in a struggle with it.

This process of life is threefold: the living being firstly forms itself within itself, secondly assimilates the object external to it, and thirdly reproduces itself as a being-for-itself in the other object. These three processes are interconnection of the moments of the concept of life as individuality, particularity, and universality, each of which forms itself into a complete system.

### Formation 

```
U ⧁ U ( P ( I ) ) 

Soul ⧁ Body ( Organs ( Health ) ) 
```

Life is the objective unity of the subjective and the objective, i.e. a unity of objects that repels itself from other objects. Yet since it is not an individual object that simply opposes other objects mechanically or chemically, but instead, as the totality of the concept, harbors inner purposiveness within itself and is the very activity of the purpose in its execution. At the same time, the immanent concept of purpose is perpetually realized and inherent to it. The living individual is therefore the process by which the object that executes its purpose, i.e. the process of living.

In the process of formation, the individual being receives within itself the general concept, which already exists in the totality of its particular moments, as the multiple objects comprising it. Formation is therefore subject to the first figure (I-P-U), in that the individual living being (I) lives up to its concept (U) when each of its particular moments (P) are in accordance with this universal. As inner purposiveness, life does not have external objects as its means, rather the specific moments of its objectivity serve as its means. As moments of the object's purpose, the particular members are ends in themselves in that each one makes the others into its means and vice versa. In the organism, each organ maintains all the others by maintaining itself. This mutual self-maintanence of all its organs, by which the process of living is realized, is the health of the organism. The major premise, P is U, states that every particularity is a universal end-in-itself. The minor premise, I is P, makes every individual member a particular means. And the conclusion, I is U, asserts that the individual living being, as living up to its universal concept, is healthy. If any member makes itself the universal purpose in the major premise without also offering itself as a means in the minor premise, thus amounting to the negation of the minor premise, then the organism becomes unhealthy or sick. The unhealthy organ, which does not want to be used a means, but instead wants to make itself the sole end at the expense of the other members, is not the true purpose but a falsification of purpose in that it pretends to be the universal when it is not. It can be clearly seen how illness in general is a judgment of particularity: it is a violation of the whole, a contravention of the purpose. In one moment, the illness withdraws from the purpose and thereby draws all the others into suffering with it, and so is on the verge of passing into the negation of death.

### Assimilation 

```
I ⧁ U ( P ( I ) )

Life ⧁ Metabolism ( Nourishment ( Object ) ) 
```

In the process of assimilation, however, the opposite relationship occurs to that of formation. The individual living entity, which through the inner formative process conforms to its universal concept, has become the universal in the conclusion I is U. Now, U takes the place of I, I is pushed into the middle and P in the last position, which is the inference of the second figure (U-I-P). The organism relates, as the overarching universality, to the particularity of external objects by means of individuality, which as the middle, is both an external object and a means. The process of external teleology is reestablished within the purposive activity of the organism, but only as a means to sustain its life. The middle term is, on the one hand, the activity that takes possession of an external object, and on the other, the activity of metabolizing the object as nourishment, thereby completing the external process of assimilating the inorganic by the organic.

While in the formative process the individual living being was still external to its universal concept, the assimilation process conforms to its concept while at the same time subjected objects alien to it as a part of itself. We now have the relationship of an organic object to something conforming to a concept that is equally organic, thereby dissolving the mechanistic relationship of the individual to the individual. Instead, since the conclusion of the second figure reads U is P, the universal concept that has become objective in the individual is also the mediator that relates two organic objects, which develop into each other through individuality and particularity in the process of reproduction. The genus has now disjointed itself into its species and therefore objectified itself, whereas previously only the individual was objective.

### Genus-Process

```
U ( P ⇄ ( I ) )

Genus ( Species ⇄ ( Embryo ) )
```

In the genus-process, the inference of the third figure (P-U-I) is formed, in that the genus now carries out its universal purpose through its two species in that the active one (P) posits the purpose of the genus and the passive one (I) that receives its purpose from it. The extremes opposed to the genus, as individuals, are therefore opposed to each other in that the one expresses the genus in the particular and the other expresses individuality as such. Since both remain individual objects, the genus presents itself only negatively in them, asserting itself through the negation of their opposition. The genus, as the universal, now completely triumphs over the objectivity of the individuals. Living individuals are born, develop, and eventually die, but the genus persists as the circle of life continues. Life is now no longer merely the Idea in the form of individual objectivity, but has raised itself to the subjectivity of the species, which by extension means that the species has itself become objectified. Each aspect of the Idea, subjectivity and objectivity, has thereby become the whole Idea: it posits itself as a totality through the sublation and preservation of the other as a moment within it. The Idea is thus divided into a subjective and objective side, and is posited in each under a mediated form.

Since nature in general stands from the standpoint of objectivity, it does not transcend the individuality of the living, and death is only the negation of these individualities in an immediate way. But even if the species presents itself positively in nature, it nevertheless always does so only in different particulars so that the species process, as the endless progression of the evolving generations, is simply mediated by new particulars. Though since the result of the genus-process is the objectification of the universal concept in the individual and the elevation of the individual into subjective universality, the subjective and objective Ideas initially stand opposite one another as the mediated Idea. The universal objectivity of the genus determines the subjective universality of the individual, for the genus has only elevated the individual into this universality through its negation. 

## Ideas

### Theoretical Idea

```
U ( P ⧁ ( I ) )

Cognition ( Thinking ⧁ ( Thought ) )
```

The theoretical idea resulted from the genus-process of life, a process which, by allowing the individual to pass away into the universal through death, thereby elevated it to the level of truth. For we designate as is that universal which remains immutable amidst the flux of all individualities. To the extent that the individual now participates in this immutability of the universal, it corresponds to its own concept, and therein lies the preservation of the individual within the universal, the negation of its immediate existence notwithstanding. The theoretical idea is this judgment of the individual and the universal, a judgment in which the individual subject attains the status of truth precisely by assimilating the universal object, as its predicate, into itself. However, since both sides already constitute the entire Idea in this context—meaning that the individual subject is a universal, and the universal predicate is an individual—they are already reciprocally related through the mediating concept of *particularity*, which encompasses both universality and individuality within itself. Therefore, Truth is not only a judgment, but is already an *inference*. On the one hand, Truth appears as an inference of the *first figure* (I-P-U): for the individual subject comes to know the universal concept of the world by gradually assimilating and unifying the entire content-rich series of particularities of the universe, drawn from individual objects. On the other hand, Truth may also be viewed in accordance with the *fourth figure* (U-P-I): for in this perspective, the subject, now enriched with the entire content of the world (U), unfolds itself into the particularities and individualities of the objective world.

Therefore, Truth resides neither within the subject nor within the objective, but as the *copula* connecting the two, and for this reason, it is also defined as the correspondence of subject and object. However, since the extremes are initially still external to one another, we can regard Truth as a definition of the Absolute only to the extent that externality has already been resolved. The resolution of this external relationship begins, however, in the three movements of the theoretical Idea. Firstly, in the minor premise (I–P), the individual object ascends to the universal in the analytic method. Secondly, in the major premise (U–P), the universal defines its particulars in the synthetic method. Thirdly, in the conclusion (U–I), the universal realizes within the individual, the latter developing out of the former in the genetic method, i.e. the problem. Even this third movement remains within the sphere of the theoretical Idea, i.e. within the form of universality, which is the characteristic feature of Truth.

#### Analytic Method

```
U ( P ⧁ ( I → U ) )
```

Since the theoretical idea reveals itself as a complete inference, which at each of its stages presents only one of its judgments, the analytical method is more precisely the first premise, I is P, because it brings about the immediate contact and coloring of individual things through the universal concept. Individual objects always, through their dissolution or death, eternally return to their generic universality rather than their particular kind. The universality of thought, which at first seemed to belong only to the concept of the world, has proven to be inherent to the objects of cognition. it is the realm of genera, which, through the dissolution of individualities as such, emerges in them as the truth. For instance, in the animal organism, sight and hearing are theoretical senses because they most purely transform the infinite multiplicity of individual objects within sensory cognition itself into the form of universality, yet by transforming them into universals, they still allow them to persist in their objectivity. This is the point where the analytical method presents itself a mode of cognition. 

Knowledge appears to the ego as thought, the abstract universality whose generalized framework it now seeks to fit individual things. They are the truth that it seeks to appropriate by utilizing the activity of observation. The truth of individual objects, however, proves itself rather to be their untruth, and only becomes truth once the cognition captures them in thought. Truth, as the mean of the extremes, consists here in allowing the individuals to participate in ever higher universals. The higher universals are comprehended as axioms, principles, laws, etc. until the the highest universality, the identity of all identities and differences, is comprehended. Analysis has thereby analyzed itself and has become its opposite in synthesis. For if its result is the dissolution of all particulars that are summarized the highest universality, then analysis itself led to this summary. There is no analysis without synthesis, and it is a one-sided conception of the empirical sciences to try to keep these two methods separate. To analyze the particularity of a concept from all individual objects means to synthesize them within it. 

#### Synthetic Method

```
U ( P ⧁ ( U → P ) )
```

Whereas analysis ended with the highest universal, synthesis begins with it. The most general concept that synthesis takes as its standpoint is highly abstract because the differentiated content originally in it was omitted in analysis. Synthesis must now rediscover the content that was in the universal and connect it back to itself. Therefore, it forms the major premise of cognition, U is P. In the object of analysis, the content is given, while in the concept of synthesis, the content it is hidden. From the perspective of reason, analysis discovers the unknown within the known, while synthesis reveals the known within the unknown. In fact, in the study of any subject matter, the object is the first to be known and the absolute universal the last. But the advantage that synthesis has over analysis is always that, since it begins with the universal, it does not depend any presupposed object that is simply given. By beginning with the independence of thought, it strives to condense the apparent indeterminacy and lack of content of thought into ever more complete content. Synthesis is therefore a priori knowledge, whereas analysis, as a posteriori knowledge, must rely on experience.

If analysis is grounded in inferences of experience (i.e. induction and analogy), then synthesis is grounded in inferences of necessity, derived a priori from the universal concept. However, progress within the synthesis can only consist in the fact that, in the second premise (U is P), the subject initially only determines itself in its universality, positing its essential difference in its moment of definition. From this, the universal is partitioned down into its particular kinds in its moment of division. This process continues until it ends in its final moment of demonstration, where the universal descends to the individual through particularity. 

##### Definition

Even if synthesis in definition begins with the universal, this universal is nevertheless not an abstraction, precisely because it is the result of the entire movement of analysis. In this way, the universal is just as much a particular, since it includes the determinate difference within itself, and as by distinguishing itself from others, it is also a particular. The definition is therefore already the totality of the concept, and therefore we can also grasp it as a definition of reason. Yet, of course the definition is only the positing of immediate, still quite general distinctions. As a categorical judgment, the necessity of the relation between subject and predicate is necessary only immediate because the specific distinctions that bring about mediation have not yet been posited. 

The definition or logical explanation is defined as the complete development of the content of a concept, which is achieved by specifying its essential characteristics. But if we are dealing with the concept of any object in general, its definition is indeed also the unfolding of its content, but in such a way that its universality still entirely encompasses the totality of genera or particulars as its characteristics, and the object is still conveyed in its simplicity. Even in the definition of the concept of any object, as always a categorical judgment, the necessity of the relationship is not yet posited because the distinctions have not yet been developed. The same is true when we are dealing with a determinate concept. As determinate, it is distinguished from others, and the difference it has from other concepts is its characteristic. The essential or essential characteristics of the concept are, however, that which forms the substance of this concept. Thus, the definition of an individual concept (definitum) requires, first, its next higher generality (genus proximum), and the characteristic that distinguishes it from other particulars of the same genus (differentia specifica).

If the characteristic of a particular concept is supposed to be the essential one, like the predicate in a categorical judgment, but the higher or lower generic concepts are discovered through analysis, then it is contingent whether it is essential or inessential. And therein also lies the immediacy of necessity, which has already been explained from the categorical judgment. Just as the contingency can lie in a characteristic, it can also lie in the subject. Indeed, because of the contingency of the two parts of the judgment, the characteristic often falls to the point of no longer being the objectively existing differentia specifica, but instead becomes a subjective marker with which we distinguish one concept from another. If, on the other hand, animal species are distinguished from one another by their skeletal structure, then such characteristics are not something merely subjective, rather, it is through these characteristics that animals define themselves objectively from one another.

To formulate a correct definition, firstly the definition should not be a mere explanation of a name, which would reduce it to an identical judgment. It should be an actual explanation because it must specify and explain its content. For this reason, the definition cannot be a negative judgment, nor contain a contradiction in terms. Then the definitum should not be contained in the definition, because otherwise it would be circular. The identity must be the content-rich identity of accident and substance, so the hallmark of a correct definition is that it can be reversed. This reversal is only possible, however, if the definition is neither too narrow nor too broad. The definition is too broad if a generic term is specified that is too general, e.g. A square is a right-angled quadrilateral does not distinguish squares from rectangles. Likewise, the definition is too narrow if a distinction that is too specific is added, e.g. A parallelogram is an equiangular quadrilateral excludes scalene parallelograms. Both definitions therefore cannot be reversed. Incidentally, mathematics otherwise offers the best definitions, it is the model of the synthetic method. Since the concept of definition is not static, but unfolds further distinctions from within itself, the definition becomes division.

##### Division

If the definition represents a categorical judgment, then the division is based on the disjunctive. The universal, be it the absolutely universal or a determinate universality, resolves itself into particularity by its nature. While in definition, the predicate P of the major premise U is P, has only the meaning of the determinateness of the universal concept, in the division it becomes the totality of its determinations. The concept disjoins itself into the realm of genera, each genus into the system of its species. So if definition denotes the intensional content of the concept, division denotes its extensional content. The essential requirement of every division is completeness, though this is not always possible to achieve. For example, in evolutionary history, the species and their genus are discovered by chance through the induction of analysis, it is also accidental whether all species of a genus have been identified, experience can always discover new species. If these species share the generic characteristic with the already known species, but differ from them by a specific characteristic, the classification becomes incomplete. Therefore, whether the classification is complete or not is a matter of chance. 

Since the definition is contingent, not only because an inessential characteristic is specified, but also because the definitum can have other essential characteristics than those specified. The division is nothing other than the characteristic of the defined concept that divides its content. Therefore, there can be several divisions alongside one another so long as as each essential characteristic of the concept can offer a specific ground or reason for the division. If, on the other hand, species and genus are further divided, further subdivisions and superdivisions also arise.

While in the empirically established divisions the species present themselves as merely accidental differences of the genus and are therefore of any number, the truly objective division based on the objectivity of dialectics must have necessary opposites as its members. The division is thus a dichotomy, or rather, since the opposites then achieve unity in a third kind, a trichotomy, which in nature often becomes a tetrachotomy. For the system of philosophy, logic is the first moment of the division, which has its opposite in nature, while both are linked in spirit as the third member of the division. The same logic also applies to the objective divisions of specific concepts. The particularity into which the division interprets the general concept is not, however, its ultimate determination. As particularity becomes ever more particular, it becomes a determinate determinacy or individuality. This richest reality, which the concept has attained in the individual species, is now the theorem.

##### Theorem

Since in the theorem, reality corresponds to the concept, albeit only in a very specific way, the theorem represents a conceptual judgment in which the particular has become appropriate to its universality. While the definition contains only one general determination, and division indicates the relationship of several particular determinations to one another, the theorem expresses the relationship of one individual determination within itself, whose moments have themselves become the whole. Thus, the theorem is the true synthesis, in that the developed moments of reality are summarized in the simplicity of the concept. Now, if the theorem presents itself as an optimal category for the concept because it fully expresses the idea as the identity of the subjective concept and objective reality, the definition has already achieved this, only that it remains with the general, which merely includes the other moments of the concept within itself, whereas the theorem separates them in their individuality. But when it comes to a specific concept, the difference between the definition and the theorem becomes even more clear. It can be doubtful whether such a concept should be conceived as a general one or as an individual one, and thus be set forth as a definition or as a theorem. It can be a definition in one science, a theorem in another, or it can appear as an individual theorem in a more general science, and in a narrower one it can form a general definition placed as an axiom. The progress of theorems, however, consists in their expressing the complete content-determination of the individual ever more purely, which is the case for the axiom. For example, in the theorems about triangles, the Pythagorean theorem does this most completely. The unity of the concept of reality, initially only presupposed in the theorem, is then mediated within itself and finally proven. This yields the three moments of the theorem: the premise, the construction, and the proof.

###### Premise

The unity of its moments presupposed but still unproven by the theorem is the premise. That in a triangle two sides and the angle enclosed by them completely determine the triangle is, as a merely stated assertion, an assertoric judgment. Every premise is an assertion, but precisely by being asserted does it already contains within itself its concept and reality. The angle enclosed by two sides is the completely expressed subjective concept of the triangle and the three other pieces added to it form its self-evident reality. Yet the premise still contains this unity of concept and reality itself only in the form of universality, i.e. in the form of a still undeveloped reality.

###### Construction

That the moments of the concept appear for themselves as distinct sides of reality is the transition of the theorem from universality to particularity. This is what the synthetic method calls construction, where the concept now establishes itself in the object. That the square of the hypotenuse in the Pythagorean theorem is equal to the squares of the two sides is to be made clear by dividing the square of the hypotenuse into two rectangles, each of which is equal to the square of one of the sides. The further lines drawn by the construction to prove this equality through the triangles, as the halves, do not yet have the clarity of the subjective concept. One does not yet know why they are drawn in this way, precisely because they are only the objective particularization of the concept, which has not yet been summarized into subjective unity or insight. Construction is therefore only hypothetical, or a problematic judgment. In the objective form of the construction, the same thing takes place as in the subjective form. The moments of mediation indeed confront the immediacy of the premise, but they are not yet recombined.

###### Proof

The recombination of what was demonstrated in construction only occurs in the proof, the apodictic judgment. It reveals the unity of the concept in the dissected members of reality as in, for instance, in the Pythagorean theorem that squares and rectangles are equal because their triangles are.  The formalism of such a proof consists in the fact that, if the construction has been put forward as the ground for the proof, it now turns out to be a simple consequence of the relationship already objectively present in the concept. Reality itself is thus only a consequence of the concept, not the other way around. But the proof is not yet the creator of this relationship as in the genetic method, rather proof is only the subjective insight into the correctness of the relationship, and so from this perspective, the proof is also entirely formal. In fact, the subjective concept, which clarifies itself out of the blind construction of objectivity, is also to be grasped as the presupposition that generated the reality it now recognizes. By synthetically summarizing the moments of construction, the concept simultaneously analyzes them from its inner simplicity. The interconnection of both paths, the analytical and the synthetic, is now the hypothesis or genetic method.

#### Genetic Method

```
U ( P ⧁ ( U → P → I → U ) )
```

The hypothesis presupposes the essential moments of the total concept, but only as something empirically given from which the rest of reality is derived. From the object, as the inessential multiplicity of reality, the essential simplicity of the concept is analytically separated in order to discover from it, through a second analysis, the remaining pieces of the puzzle. This double analysis is also a double synthesis, for the simple totality of the concept is the universal, which is synthetically derived from the individual given objects, and the moments of the concept and reality are likewise synthetically linked to one another as the essential and the inessential. Hence, hypotheses are generally formed through analysis, whether by induction or analogy. In a problem, we begin with the subjectively universal as something objectively present, in order to allow the whole of reality to arise from it through the genesis of the relationship. However, propositions can very easily be transformed into problems if one gives one side of the premise, e.g. two sides and the angle enclosed by them of a triangle, in order to construct the entire triangle from it. The genetic method is still different from the dialectical method because the former begins with the simplest and most immediate fact of experience, whereas the latter begins with the simplest and most immediate concept. Both methods, however, are already very close to each other, differing only in finding their results in a different order. Since in the hypothesis of the problem, both sides are already presupposed, it is not simply a matter of having insight into their identity, but deducing its reality from its concept. Thus, the construction is no longer blind, as in the case of a theorem, for which it seeks, as it were, an external means to an end alien to itself. An internal teleology is posited to bring the purpose to fruition in an object appropriate to it. Therefore, the subjectivity of construction also vanishes, and in its place comes that of the answer to the problem to which the concept itself leads us.

The proof of the hypothesis seems to be superfluous to the extent that the insight into the necessity of the relationship already appears in solution. The proof therefore solely and exclusively points to this reality produced by the solution is the only correct one. This proof can only be carried out by demonstrating that every reality other than that arising from the concept does not correspond to this concept. In other words, every other reality is a negative judgment of the concept. Such a proof is called a proof by contradiction, which is actually only the simple demonstration of the principle of identity, that what corresponds to the concept corresponds to it, and what contradicts it contradicts it: that what does not correspond to the concept cannot be what corresponds to the concept. For example, if one were to choose a point other than the one where the perpendiculars intersect as the center of a circle, it would follow that the isosceles triangles constructed on the two lines would not have equal sides, and the radii would not be equally distant from the center, all of which is absurd. Thus a reductio ad absurdum is undertaken: the contradictory opposite cannot possibly be identical with its opposite, and the law of the excluded middle applies here completely. From this example, however, it also follows that the standpoint of cognition has been completely reversed. The objective idea is no longer the rule according to which the subjective idea should be determined and fulfilled because the reality that it expresses are inappropriate to the concept. The subjective idea is now the rule and the complete standard, which posits this one reality as that which corresponds to the concept and negates those others as that which contradicts it. 

### Practical Idea

Whereas in the theoretical idea, objectivity is intended to fill the empty subjectivity with itself, in the practical idea, subjectivity—having already been filled by substantial objectivity—now, as absolute self-determined universality, takes precedence over mere objectivity. The opposition between the two sides is thus not yet resolved, but has merely assumed a different form. The content-rich concept—as the higher element—now transcends the object, permeates it, and supersedes its externality. Thus, the practical senses—in contrast to the theoretical ones—are those that dissolve matter by asserting the validity of subjectivity within it, whereas the theoretical senses allow it to persist as the rule. Within Spirit, practical philosophy likewise constitutes the standpoint of making the objects true by transforming them. However, since theory constitutes the generalization of the object, it appears, according to Aristotle (*Metaphysics*, Ch. 7), to stand higher than the practical idea, latter becoming finite to the extent that it concern itself with the particularity of the object. Yet, precisely by imbuing this very particularity with subjective universality, according to Kant (*Critique of Practical Reason*, pp. 215–219, 4th ed.), practice in turn stands higher than theory, for it alone constitutes the true realization of the concept:

>Grey, dear friend, is all theory,

>And green is life's golden tree.

However, since action is the activity of the universal concept that strives to realize itself through the succession of individual objects, the practical idea falls under the inference of the second figure (U–I–P). The concept that constitutes the principle (U), connects itself with objectivity (P) through a double middle term: its own self-particularizing activity and an individual object toward which this activity is directed (I). The moments of this process are necessarily such that, standing opposed to the particularized realization of the Good, there is an inadequacy—manifested as Evil—which the postulate seeks to resolve within the universality of the norm.

#### Good

The first premise of the inference of the practical Idea—whether it takes the form (U-I-P) or the form (P-I-U)—serves as the major premise of this figure that constitutes the judgment ((I = U) or (U = I)). The subjective concept that possesses objective truth within itself as its rule, and seeks to take the place of the external objectivity that is to be superseded, is the Good. This contains the universal concept within the particularity of the subject (I = U) just as much as it seeks to particularize this universal concept (U = I) through its own activity. Since objectivity is in-itself this very correspondence, given that the subjective concept has derived its truth from it, the Good constitutes the inner teleology tasked with realizing the purpose that is immanent to the object. 

However, the Good is not exclusively a moral concept—as Rosenkranz asserts in order to exclude it from Logic. The Good is the universal metaphysical category whereby the objectivity corresponding to the concept appears only as a *particular* objectivity, whereas in some other object, this objectivity has yet to be realized. Whether a human action or a natural object is good depends upon its objective constitution. Yet, since this constitution may correspond to its determination as often as it contradicts it, the Good constitutes a *problematic judgment*. If we were to make the Good the definition of the Absolute, it would still suffer from the deficiency of being realized in some objects, but not others.

#### Evil

We now designate as Evil the objects that do not yet correspond to the Good—objects in which their purpose is not yet realized. This constitutes a problematic judgment that leads to the negative form of the apodictic judgment. The Good, conceived as the contradiction inherent in being a purpose realized within a specific individual object, while simultaneously encountering a limit in another, non-purposive object, now passes over into activity within the second premise of the practical inference. In this way, the practical Idea is no longer, as it was in the first premise, the merely theoretical and immediate relation of activity to reality, or of reality to activity. The Good no longer exists as a bare presupposition, but reaches out beyond the sphere of objectivity. Thus, activity constitutes both the individualization of the Good that which seeks to universalize itself within the object (I = P), and conversely, the activity now impelled by universality, which proceeds to individualize itself (P = I). Viewed from the perspective of the object, however, the minor premise implies that, insofar as the object—as a particular entity—is grasped by the individualizing activity (P = I), it is simultaneously grasped by the universal principle in the very act of particularizing itself (I = P). The object that is selected by the activity has thus become a purposive means for it, by virtue of which the Good reaches out toward an objectivity that is as of yet devoid of conceptual form, in order to likewise overcome it.

When reduced to the level of ordinary representation, this yields the proposition that theologians often employ in theodicy to demonstrate the necessity of evil in the world, i.e. that God Himself makes use of evil in order to bring forth the Good. In imitation of the Divinity, the Jesuits have also used this argument to their own advantage when they assert that the end justifies the means. The aforementioned isolation of the Good finds further expression in the proposition of the Theodicy that, since the world was necessarily a finite one, it could not be spared the limitation of Evil. The true Theodicy, however, lies in the recognition that, since the Good is essentially the activity of the self-realizing Concept, the latter requires a resistance that it must overcome in order to achieve victory. This opposing element is precisely what constitutes Evil. The finitude of the standpoint of the Good *in general* lies, however, in the fact that—due to its isolated realization as a *final cause*—it still requires an external means (a *proximate cause*) in order to assert itself in reality in a universal manner. To this extent, the Good reverts to the standpoint of *external teleology*, with the sole difference that the end is no longer an arbitrary, finite one—such as building a house or fitting a bottle-stopper—but is the *substantial* end: the universal Idea itself.

Evil as an external object opposing the Good is to be rendered commensurate with the Concept within the second premise now finds its application in the spheres of both Nature and Spirit. *Pain* constitutes *natural* Evil, that which stands in opposition to the *Pleasant* and the *Agreeable* (the natural Good), whereas immorality constitutes the correlative to the morality. When theologians speak of a justification of God, specifically regarding His permission of evil in this world, they proceed in precisely the same manner as we have outlined with respect to physical suffering. Just as the pain serves as the necessary counter-force within unconscious nature to bring physical well-being into existence, so too does morality, within the conscious will of man, require a worthy adversary in order to achieve victory. While Socrates sought to explain pleasure solely as the consequence of a preceding pain, and Robinet could even assert—with regard to nature—that pleasure and pain invariably maintain a state of equilibrium, Evil will gradually yield ever more to the Good in the moral order of the world.

Since the Good, in order to realize itself, requires an appropriate means, the question arises whether the Good is already contained within this means or not. As an appropriate means, it must indeed partake in the very nature of the Good. However, as an external object, it must simultaneously offer resistance to the Good, such that the Good is therein partly already objectified, and partly not yet. Since the already existing Good strives to realize itself further, the future Good is of a higher order than the present one, otherwise one would not transcend the latter to attain the former. However, the Good that is not yet realized is precisely that Evil which the Good employs as a means to attain a higher mode of existence. Thus, within Evil itself, the higher Good is already implicitly contained. The end that has not yet been realized within Evil is therefore *better* in contrast to that already present within the Good, which thereby appears as the *worse*. Hence, the French have a very apt saying for this: *Le plus grand ennemi du bien, c'est le mieux* (The greatest enemy of the good is the better). By virtue of the finitude and relativity of the Good, its realization falls apart into an endless progression, the resolution of which constitutes the third moment of the Practical Idea.

#### Postulate

The Good, to be sure, posits the demand that—because it exists in the particular—it must also be objectified in all the particulars of the world as a whole. Yet the Good remains a mere postulate, since all particulars are never fully given. Thus, no matter in how many individual objects it may already have realized itself, an infinite multitude of other particulars still confronts it—particulars which it is compelled to recognize as Evil, such that the practical Idea finds itself drawing water into the tub of the Danaids, making no headway despite its ceaseless activity. In this manner, the individual—much like the protagonist in Schlegel’s *Lucinde*—might be inclined to surrender himself to inaction in a state of "divine laziness," reasoning that an infinity of goods has already been attained *in and for itself* without any intervention on his part, and that his own actions can never exhaust the infinity of goods yet to be realized. Caught between these two infinities, the practical Idea stands bewildered and irresolute.

However, the contradiction inherent in this endless progression must spur us to undertake its resolution. The error lies solely in the assumption that the universal end of the Good, merely by manifesting itself in a particular object, thereby seems to have assumed the very nature of particularity itself. Yet, if we recognize the universal—as it resides within the Concept of the world—as a principle that retains its universality undiminished even within the particular, then it is precisely at this culminating point that the universal itself attains its own true reality. Whereas in the two premises of the inference (U-I-P), the Universal was immediately particularized (U = I), and as this particular entity, appropriated objectivity as a specific means to its end (I = P), and thus in the conclusion (U = P), the Good has become objectified in an absolute sense, regardless of what specific objects serve as the medium for its manifestation. But if in every *individual* realization of the Good also realizes the Good *in general*, then the activity of the Good perceives in its realized object neither an objective limitation nor a mere means, but as the full truth of the good end that has been absolutely realized. The practical Idea—to the extent that it has now truly objectified itself within its object—has once again become a theoretical Idea, just as earlier, conversely, the theoretical Idea—by having absorbed the True from the external world into itself—was compelled to transition into practical activity in order to liberate the True from that very limitation of subjectivity into which it had fallen.

### The Ideal

To the extent that the unity of theory and practice exists—even here—only within individual objects, therein lies the deficiency of this standpoint as well. Although this unity is already a very elevated standpoint, it is not yet adequate to serve as the highest definition of the Absolute. If we relate this identity of the theoretical and practical idea specifically to the general concept of the world *as such*, then we must conceive of its subjective contemplation, in a completely unrestricted manner, simultaneously as an objective activity. This is in the sense in which we had previously characterized the Absolute as the absolute unity of the inner and the outer, and as we shall soon come to know it as the Absolute Idea. In this respect, the biblical pronouncement—"God said, 'Let there be light,' and there was light"—is simply an exemplification of the general principle formulated by Philo: "His word is His deed". However, wherever only *particular* objects that correspond to the concept are contrasted with those that remain inadequate to it, those objects themselves remain, in part, alienated from reality. Such a privileged object—which, as a particularization of the subjective idea of ​​the Good and the True, stands (so to speak) with only "one foot" in reality—we now designate as the *Ideal*. According to the third figure (P-U-I) of a disjunctive inference, the archetype constitutes one extreme (P) that is mediated by the external object (I) through the universality of the concept (U).

The Ideal in Nature is the archetype of every particular kind—an archetype which, having been implanted (more or less adequately, depending on contingent conditions) into an individual natural form, radiates from it and towards us with ever-recognizable features. By contrast, the Ideal in Spirit belongs primarily to art. Even if the Ideal has not yet been realized by the artist, it remains—even if only within their mind—a truly objective form. In the finished work of art, the beholder then recognizes it as an existing reality. The artist represents the active manifestation of the practical idea—the endeavor to bring the Ideal into the world, and the beholder represents the theoretical idea—the mind that perceives it as a realized purpose lying before it. Yet, both figures constitute the whole: the artist must previously have possessed the theoretical intuition of this work corresponding to the Ideal, and the beholder cannot see the Ideal within the work of art without simultaneously having practically produced it within his own inner self. The work of art itself is the True, since it has been brought forth as an object from a subjective Idea, and the Good, since it has become a static object as an objective Idea.

The unity of theory and practice may also be discerned in the formative processes of both animal life and the organism of the state. To the extent that the form has yet to be created, the formative process constitutes the practical realization of the Ideal, but to the extent that the form is also already ever-present, the archetype is theoretically comprehended within it. However, this distinction between Nature and Spirit lies in the fact that, in the natural organism, the form is merely theoretically preserved, whereas in the life of the state, it is also practically and continuously further developed. In both processes, however, the archetype is only present to a greater or lesser degree. Conversely, where the practical realization of the subjective Ideal coincides absolutely with the perfected objectivity of the form in theoretical comprehension opens up to us the realm of the Beautiful.

#### The Beautiful

If we isolate the specific object in which the True appears as the Good and the Good as the True—the already fully realized Ideal—and consider it in-and-for-itself: this yields precisely the concept of the Beautiful *as such*, quite apart from any application to Nature or Spirit. Just as the unity of theory and practice in general, so too does the Beautiful fall under the third figure (P-U-I). However, because the Ideal—in the concluding proposition of the disjunctive inference—had posited itself as one extreme within the object (as the other extreme), thereby elevating that object to the status of beauty. This Ideal now shifts to the middle term in the specific syllogism of the Beautiful (as the first figure under the form of the third figure: I-P-U). its purpose here is to unite—as the particular archetype (P)—the extremes of the general Idea of ​​the Beautiful (U) and the individual beautiful form (I). The external object—which is inadequate to the concept by itself—is, however, once it has been seized by the dominion of the Beautiful (now isolated for its own sake), consequently cast out as a term of the inference.

Indeed, The Beautiful is already the Absolute, but only in its individual manifestation. To put it another way, an individual form has now been elevated to the status of the Absolute. Thus, while we may well be able to define the Absolute *as* the Beautiful, it is nevertheless not the Idea *in and for itself*, but the Absolute *as it appears within objectivity*, for the Beautiful derives its origin from its very act of *appearing*. In this lies the inherent limitation—even of the Idea of ​​the Beautiful itself. However, it is inadmissible to incorporate into Logic one specific Idea—Truth, as Rosenkranz does—or even two—the True and the Good, as Hegel does—while omitting the third: the Beautiful, which has always been juxtaposed with the first two. Rosenkranz’s question (*Science of the Logical Idea*, Vol. I, p. X): “Why—if the True and the Good are posited as moments of the logical Idea—should not the Beautiful be included as well?” is a very good point, so the fact that he answers it in the negative is all the more curious, given that he otherwise readily aligns himself with the trajectory of Hegel’s *Propädeutik*. But even here, Hegel himself makes mention of the Beautiful within the sphere of Logic—although only incidentally, as a *potentiated* form of the Idea of ​​Life: “Life—represented both as physical and as spiritual, and liberated from the conditions and limitations of contingent existence—is the Beautiful” (Vol. XVIII, p. 120). 

Two observations can be appended to these words of Hegel. First, Schiller, in his poem *Das Ideal und das Leben* (The Ideal and Life), also defined the Beautiful in precisely the same manner:

>It has cast out every witness

>Of human neediness

However, we must counter Rosenkranz and Hegel with the argument that the Beautiful represents the Idea—as the unity of the finite and the infinite—while itself remaining within the realm of the finite, for it is embodied within the finite material of an individual object.

The second observation that the Beautiful—much like the Good and the True—constitutes an Idea is precisely because—as Hegel himself notes—it finds its application in Nature no less than in Spirit. We may call a natural object beautiful by virtue of the regularity, symmetry, and harmony of its parts. But this category applies to natural beauty just as much as it does to artistic beauty. We may call a landscape beautiful on the account of the forms of its objects, yet even more so when our mind is induced into an pleasant mood—much like when standing before the landscapes of Claude Lorrain. A wine is beautiful not only if it is genuine (and thus *true*), nor merely if it is palatable (and thus *good*), but if, through its sparkle, its color, its bouquet, and so forth, it superadds the quality of being *beautiful in appearance* to these other two attributes. What underlies the Beautiful in this context is always the concept of *inner* purposiveness, not merely formal purposiveness (as in cases where the content of the purpose remains arbitrary and finite), but a purposiveness that is entirely *actual*, in that as its content is the Idea itself. This becomes particularly evident in the products of fine art—for instance, when we behold a depiction of Christ. Conversely, that which is contrary to purpose will present itself to us as *ugliness*.

#### The Ugly

Since the Beautiful is the objectification of the Idea, the remainder of objectivity has not extended to the activity of the Idea constitutes the Ugly. However, since the Idea is always already immanent to objectivity in general, no object is absolutely ugly, nor its inner purposiveness completely obscured, precisely because it is impossible for an object to completely fail to correspond to its concept. Therefore, the distinction between the Beautiful and the Ugly is relative, what is ugly is simply *less* beautiful, one whose inner purposiveness exists to a lesser, more imperfect degree. Thus, while the Beautiful is that objectivity in which the purpose reveals itself in perfect clarity, the Ugly is a more remote means toward this purpose—a means, however, that stands poised to tip over into purposelessness. To the extent that it resists its purpose, it constitutes the Ugly forming the second figure (U-I-P) within the third figure, whereby the Ideal (U) is mediated with the Beautiful (P) precisely through the Ugly (I). On the one hand, the Ugly, precisely through its contrast with the Beautiful, serves only to highlight the radiance of the Beautiful all the more brilliantly. On the other hand, the Ugly is no mere a foil for the Beautiful, but is the very means by which the Ideal is realized, specifically by virtue of the struggle into which it enters with the Beautiful.

As the *negatively* Beautiful, the Ugly not only stands in direct opposition to Beauty, just as Evil stands opposed to the Good, but is itself drawn into the very sphere of the Beautiful. The Ugly manifests Nature either as a loss of inner purposiveness, such as traits of the organism that are useless or have fallen into disuse, or where similar structural irregularities have taken the place of regularity. The Ugly also reveals itself in the Spirit, even more profoundly there than in Nature, though it must not be confused with Evil. Just as physical pain is a tearing of the fibers of the flesh, so is spiritual ugliness a fragmentation of the soul, an inner dissonance, whatever nature it may be. To even depict ugliness within a work of art, and to do so with a sense of beauty, is the artist's great task.

#### The Sublime

The Ugly, having been brought into contact with Beauty, is permeated by it and transformed into a moment of Beauty. The Beautiful casts its radiance upon its foil. The restoration of the Beautiful out of its struggle with the Ugly is the Sublime, in which, as the true third figure (P-U-I), now in the concluding movement, and through the power of the Idea (U), the Ugly has become beautiful (I = P). Thus, if we wish to return once more to the scenes of martyrdom in Christian paintings, the glory of the martyr who endures suffering for the sake of faith is sublime. Or if we have before us a fearsome scene from nature, such as a roaring storm upon a raging sea, we may call the rainbow that arches overhead the sublime. However, as Kant observes, the impression of the Sublime vanishes if we ourselves are in danger, for in that case, the calm, contemplation of beauty is absent (*Critique of Judgment*, § 28). The undulating contours of the mountains bordering Lake Como, gently uplifted by volcanic forces, are beautiful, whereas the jagged, rugged lines of the Finsteraarhorn and the Furca, clearly bearing upon their very forms the traces of meteorological devastation, are sublime. The downfall of the tragic hero—after he has affirmed his virtue amidst the conflict of ethical forces—is sublime, for the victory of absolute ethicality is achieved through the vanquishing of the Ugly within the confines of the moral conflict. The hero’s tragic flaw, as Aristotle observes—is one that commands respect, because the Ugly itself is simply a lesser form of the Beautiful. As much as ugliness has entered the sphere of the beautiful, and the negative has become a moment of the positive, the objective Idea thereby coincides with the subjective Idea in its entire breadth and scope, the Ideas having demonstrated their unity as the Absolute Idea.

## The Absolute Idea

The Absolute Idea is the highest stage of the Idea—that in which all opposites resolve themselves into a thoroughgoing harmony. Not only have the True, the Good, and the Beautiful now developed together into a single Idea—as the most complete fusion of theory and practice—but the Absolute Idea, as the unity of subjectivity and objectivity, also comprehends itself at every stage of its own development. As the unity of form and content, however, it serves simultaneously as a doctrine of principles and a doctrine of method.

Hegel describes this highest standpoint of the Idea (Vol. V, p. 349) most aptly as follows: “The richest is the most concrete and the most subjective; and that which withdraws into the simplest depth is the most powerful and the most all-encompassing. The highest, most acute pinnacle is pure personality—which, solely by virtue of the absolute dialectic that constitutes its very nature, encompasses and sustains all things within itself just as fully as it constitutes itself as the freest entity: as that simplicity which is the primordial immediacy and universality.” However, one must by no means understand this to mean a transcendent Subject enthroned above and beyond the objective world, rather it only signifies the conscious individuality of the actual person for themselves in thought is its highest pinnacle, and is considered here, within the context of Logic, solely in terms of its pure and eternal content: i.e. the Eternal Personality.

As much as the Absolute Idea constitutes the absolute interconnection of the subjective Idea and the objective Idea—of Method and Principle—we must firstly consider the self-movement of this Absolute Idea within the Absolute Method, comprehended as the universal subjectivity of the Concept. Secondly, the product of this self-movement takes place within the systems of the world, understood as the Judgment of the Method in the particularization and objectification of the Concept. Thirdly, the system and method are elevated to the Encyclopedia—the Inference of the Absolute Idea—the supreme consummation of the Subject-Object in the absolute individuality of the Concept. Yet, in all three of these spheres, the Absolute Method simultaneously presents itself as the Absolute Principle.

### World-Dialectic

The Absolute Idea is neither the rigid objectivity of the already existing Truth, nor the fluid subjectivity of the restless striving for The Good that never realizes its final goal. Instead, the Absolute Idea is the eternal activity of the Idea to bring itself forth, the dynamic form that constitutes the substantive content itself. The rhythm of the objective world is the absolute method, the immanent movement of the subject matter itself that may be termed "World-Dialectic," to borrow an expression from Schelling. From this standpoint, we can now rightfully assert that The Absolute is the Method, it is nothing distinct from its self-movement, and is the very process of the world itself. Put simply, the method is the entire series of principles that results from the progression of the method itself. Does not Scripture also say of the Deity: "I am the Way and the Life"? Dialectic is neither analytic, nor the synthetic, nor genetic, but it is the higher unity of all three. As we analytically develop one distinction of the Concept from another, precisely because it is already implicitly contained therein, we link them synthetically, and through the synthesis of what has been analytically developed, a third element that closes and unites the other two. However, this genesis is not the genesis of an empirical reality from a given concept, nor the genesis of facts from one another—as Trendelenburg conceives his method—but signifies the genesis of the absolute Concept from itself, to the extent that the moments developed from it ultimately return back into it. Although, in the realms of Nature and Spirit, this generation of categories from categories also transforms into the generation of facts from facts. Thus, the Method—as the very movement of the subject matter itself—comprises within itself the following three moments:

#### Thought

The beginning of the Method is the Concept in its simplicity, wherein all oppositions are still contained within it. The world in general, as much as an individual object, is initially apprehended in this undeveloped state. We may term this first moment *Thought* and the Method therefore begins with the immediate Thought. At every stage of Logic, we have encountered this *enveloped* identity, e.g. as Being, as Essence, or as the Universal. In its purest form, it is the Principle of Identity itself. This beginning is at once the result of the highest analysis, arising through the elimination and sublimation of all distinctions into a pure universality, as it is also the result of the highest synthesis, wherein the entire richness of this content has been enveloped and is present only implicitly or *in itself*.

#### Thinking

Secondly, the progression of the method consists in the fact that what has been synthesized is once again analyzed out of the apparent simplicity of the Concept. The opposition that was implicitly contained within the unity now unfolds itself explicitly. This constitutes the second moment of the method, *Thinking* or negation of the Concept. To this sphere belongs the finite, the hostile, and the discordant, in other words, the False, the Evil, and the Ugly. Precisely because the moments of truth are held apart from one another, there also arises error, which is itself an aspect of truth conceived in isolation. As a result, the immediate itself is already the False, the Negative, for it is merely the other form of one-sidedness. Even if Thinking represents the negation of this initial negation, it does not thereby constitute Affirmation; for it has not yet negated itself in the very act of negating its opposite. Likewise, Evil represents the sundering of the moments of the Good, manifesting, for instance, as the conflict between norms and desires, and so forth.

Thinking constitutes the entire Concept just as Thought does, but whereas Thought presents the Concept in its universality and simplicity, Thinking re-presents the particularization, division, and manifold diversity of the Concept that manifests as the mutual externality and independence of its moments. Thus, the World Dialectic which in the Thought lies dormant is transformed within the Thinking into a pulsating heartbeat of life. To the extent that Thinking—as the division of Thought into opposites—encompasses both moments within itself as negations of each other, it constitutes a duality within itself. When this duality is added to the first and third moments of the method, it often transforms the method's triplicity into a quadruplicity. This is particularly common to Nature, as it is the objective externalization of the Idea, though occasionally in Spirit as well. This fourfold structure can even expand into a fivefold one when the middle term differentiates itself into three moments of unity, opposition, and synthesis.

#### Closure

Thirdly, the culmination of the method is *Closure*, the conclusion that reunites the moments of opposition that had been torn apart by Thinking. Closure is the total concept that links universality and particularity together within individuality of the concept. Thus, only Closure is the truly positive—as the negation of the negation—as much as Thought and Thinking only stand in a negative relation to one another as the negativity of both sides are positively united. Whereas Thought had become a negative only through Thinking itself—having initially presented itself as an unreflective, undeveloped unity—Closure now returns to it, transforming the immediate unity into a mediated unity. For Closure allows the determinations that had dissolved into Thought through abstraction to clash and negate one another as fully independent opposites, elevating them to a developed interconnection that preserves their distinctions. This return to the beginning constitutes the true circular proof—a proof that proves itself—in that the unity validates the distinctions and the distinctions validates the unity. Within this circular movement, the World Dialectic reveals itself to be its own cause, a cause that is simultaneously its own effect. This final cause is the purpose that remains precisely what it is in the very act of bringing itself forth, as that which descends into the simplest depth and ascends to an all-encompassing whole. 

In that the end has thus returned to the beginning, it has also once again appropriated the nature of the beginning. Closure constitutes a further synthesis from which higher, self-negating distinctions are developed, until we have arrived at the ultimate negation and the highest closure. Just as the Absolute Method is by no means the mere empirical genesis of facts, as if we merely sought to demonstrate what experience presents to us, so too is the Absolute Method even less a mere construction based upon a constantly recurring schema of categories applied in a monotonous fashion across the various stages of experience, as Schelling once proposed. Instead, precisely because the Idea serves as the starting point of its activity and its own driving force, the World-Dialectic practically unfolds into a World-System.

### World-System

The unities and opposites that mutually generate one another within dialectic are no empty play of concepts, they are *the world itself*. Both Nature and Spirit is the real movement of this ebb and flow, this surging back and forth of thought. In the World-Dialectic itself, this movement of thought appears as an immediate thought because the Concept still retains all its moments, which unfold with necessity, within the simplicity of the world of thought. Likewise, within the World-System, everything has a mediated character because the Concept arrives at its primordial division within the Judgment. The three moments of the World-Dialectic therefore present themselves here—within the general form of mediation—once again as immediate, mediated, and closed. This stage in the development of logical science has already been masterfully treated by Rosenkranz (*Das System der Wissenschaft*, §§ 264–283; *Wissenschaft der logischen Idee*, Part II, pp. 378–430), so nothing remains to be done but explain the main features of his exposition to endow them with their dialectical coherence and scientific significance.

Here lies the point where pure Reason—or Dialectic—as it pours itself out into reality and creates the *external juxtaposition* of thought—must take account of the genetic method. As the term implies, the genetic method traces the *genesis* of facts from facts, which can never attain ultimate exactitude due to its inherent incompleteness. Dialectic thus remains, even here, the regulative principle and source of exactitude amidst this surging rhythm of the objects of experience over which the genetic method presides. But in its third moment—as the World-Inference—the Absolute Idea will overcome even this final inadequacy by leading the World-System back into the World-Dialectic. For since the Absolute Idea ultimately evolves into the total individuality of the Concept, encompassing both universality and particularity within itself, it transforms the more empirically given and contingently occurring elements of the World-System itself into necessary moments of the Idea *in and for itself*. Within the World-Inference, it reintegrates the a posteriori genesis of experience into the a priori self-generation of thought. 

#### Chaos and Local Systems

To the extent that the World-Dialectic transforms every moment of the World-System into a reality that undergoes differentiation, beings not only separate themselves from others, but are also internally divided. However, since this duality still appears in an *immediate* form, the genesis of phenomena in the world begins with a wild, disordered mélange of all the forms that are destined to evolve from it. We designate this immediate unity as *Chaos*—the first category of the World-System. Just as in the poetic myths of the various peoples, the world sprang forth from chaos, so too is every form within the world chaotic in its inception. Matter constitutes this undeveloped oneness of all determinations in general, while in the organism, it exists as the embryo, and for the Spirit, it exists as feeling. The primordial state of humanity binds together all the spiritual distinctions of human reason within the instinctive unity of familial love and tribal fellowship. In the wild, natural existence of nations, these distinctions are even more chaotically jumbled together. Thus, amidst the separations inherent in actuality, Chaos mimics the simplicity of the *Concept*—although always doing so in an negative manner. 

The very negativity of Chaos differentiates into *local systems*, localities separated in space or time that arose from the dense unity of Chaos with varying degrees of contingency (depending on external circumstances), yet which continue to hold their internal oppositions together within their own dense unity, just as Chaos did with all oppositions in general. In this way, the local system replicates the general chaos within their own particularity, and to borrow an expression from Rosenkranz, they are separated from one another as if by a "hiatus" or are only externally (mechanically) related to one another. Just as the particular local systems exist within Chaos (which is itself a local system, the very first one) so too are the individual, contingent existences that exist within every local system—which in their processes limit and contend with one another—bound together into a whole that gives the system a specific character. The formation of local systems is Chaos in the process of particularizing itself, yet both Chaos and local systems imitate the Idea. For while Chaos, as a unifying entity, is the manifestation of the universal Idea, local systems are the totality of this Idea, but specifically as the same Idea in the process of particularizing itself. At this juncture, it falls to the genetic method to demonstrate how the individual local systems gradually evolved out of chaotic beginnings, such as how the initial homogeneity of the Earth’s formation—the distinct characters of the various continents gradually came to the fore, or how the diverse lineages and nationalities of the present evolved from the primordial unity of the human race, in short, to explain how even more particular and contingent local systems came into existence.

Attempts have even been made to apply the genetic method to the very pinnacle of local systems—the history of philosophy—by seeking to derive the origins of philosophical systems from national, psychological, geographical, and other perspectives, rather than from the logical-dialectical development of the Concept itself. Because these perspectives are specific to the domain they originated from, they are matters of a strictly secondary nature. However, the different stages of the development of Nature arises solely through a dialectical-conceptual process, rather than through a genetic-empirical one. The two processes therefore coexist simultaneously in space, even if, within narrower cycles, temporal succession is not entirely precluded.

#### Oscillation and Compensation

If in the immediacy of the World-System—where Chaos and local systems appear as simple unity and differentiating multiplicity—these elements merely constitute the peaceful coexistence of the moments of the opposition, then secondly, the true opposition within the world-system consists in the struggle of these particularities and contingencies against one another. Thereby, they present distortions of the Idea that only acquire adequate expression only to the extent that these very elements cast themselves from one extreme to the other in order to find the true mean. Firstly, these distortions constitute, as the oppositional element *within* the oppositions of the World-System itself, the oscillations occurring within the local systems. Examples of oscillations within, cited by Rosenkranz, ranges from storms, earthquakes, floods, droughts, fires, intrusive species, epidemics, madness, revolutions, and so forth. With regard to the last example, we can more specifically designate—in the case of France, for example—the pervasive corruption of the absolute monarchy and the monstrous excesses of the Convention as the oscillations of French society in its struggle to attain political freedom. This oscillation assumes the form of a periodicity in the manifestation of the moments of the Idea, to the extent that the victory of one moment demands and prepares the victory of the opposing moment as the content of the next period. In Nature, for instance, mollusks and insects can be regarded as the conceptual oscillations of animal life that found their equilibrium in the vertebrates.

This equilibration constitutes, within the opposition of the World-System, the second opposition that stands in opposition to the oscillations. For since the Idea is immanent to all phenomena, it has the power to render full justice to itself amidst the tangle of finitude and contingency, achieving this through empirical adjustments and the negation of whatever opposes it. The destiny of the individual existence, engaged in the struggle for survival against the other entities with which it is interconnected, is not to promote its own particularity, but to advance the whole Concept. Through the cumulative averaging of positive and negative formations, and through the successive interplay of these opposing states acting as light and shadow, reality attains the completeness of all the moments that constitute the harmony and totality of the Idea. Thus, Azaïs spoke long ago of a "Law of Compensations", and Rosenkranz aptly describes this same principle as an "uninterruptedly active dialectic." But as Eugène Sue observes, the "indolence of Providence" often allows these compensations to result from the oscillations only after considerable intervals. Hence, the short-lived present generation must frequently content itself with deferring to the judgment of future history. In the realm of nature, the oscillations of the Earth's magnetic field compensate one another only over relatively long periods. Within the sphere of organic nature, Rosenkranz cites a further example of such compensation: that epidemics are followed by a "surge of reproductive vitality."

The actual universe—as it arises from the periodic alternation of extremes as the unity of all local systems and their metamorphoses, encompassing the coexistence of progress, regression, and stasis—leads the philosopher who surveys the totality of compensations toward optimism. By contrast, the *philosophaster*—such as a Schopenhauer or Hartmann—who fixates solely on the extremes of oscillation and closes his eyes to the compensations, are driven toward a pessimistic outlook. But just as in the *immediacy* of the world-system, Chaos and the local systems appeared as the static opposition of unity and difference, so too, in the *opposition* proper, do oscillation and compensation in their progressive motion and reciprocal interaction reveal themselves as the *caustic* moments of disorder and order within reality itself. This superseded contrast would indeed be capable of converting pessimism into optimism— establishing a more veracious *theodicy* than any achieved hitherto by theologians or even by Leibniz—as much as it now becomes clearly evident how the evils of oscillations serve as purposeful means leading toward the good of compensations.

#### Concentration and Uniqueness

Finally, the closure of the World-System presents itself at the perfected apex of the universal Concept as Individuality. However, since this configuration still falls within the realm of a reality characterized by divergence, it cannot be devoid of an oppositional form. For the completion of the World-System consists, on the one hand, in the fact that every local system collects itself into a concentration that restores the diffused universality of the Concept, and on the other hand, in the fact that this very concentration bestows upon a specific individual form the value of uniqueness, wherein this concentration finds its completion, inasmuch as no other form can equal it. Concentration and Uniqueness thus constitute the opposition within this reconciliation. Although uniqueness, through its own activity, brings about the settled result of concentration, because it initially stands as an isolated individuality, it must engage in a struggle with other individuals in order to guide them toward concentration. Victory must necessarily fall to the favored individual, the one endowed with the universality of the Concept, because the others already embody this totality of the Concept *in themselves*, and are thus inevitably permeated by it.

This active, concentrating Uniqueness constitutes, finally, the highest and most sharply defined apex of pure Personality—"encompassing and holding everything within itself"—precisely because it is the true universality itself. Thus, as individuals that are identical to the Concept, Christ represents this uniqueness of the *genus* in religion, Homer’s *Iliad* in the epic, Aristotle in Greek philosophy, Raphael in Romantic painting, and so on. Hence, Emerson also termed them "representative men," and Schelling once called his childhood friend Hegel "the living and complete personality of cognition." Rosenkranz characterizes a capital city as the central subject of a vast regional system. In Nature, gold among the metals, the rose among the flowers, man among the animals, and the Earth among the planets—among other examples—occupy this position. It in no way diminishes the perfected reality of the Idea that its adequate expression is a mere individual, for this individual is simultaneously endowed with universal subjectivity. Under the weight of these concluding categories of his logic, Rosenkranz appears to have wavered even in his initial theistic transcendence—his positing of a hypostatized personality of the Absolute—having finally attained the insight that "the Idea is no longer relegated to the Beyond, but is rather beheld as present." What Strauss terms the "cult of genius" amounts to the same thing, and certainly even in this notion, the essential polarity of the World-System is highlighted even more distinctly, as much as the genius nevertheless belongs to the sphere of finitude, even if that finitude is irrelevant to this specific context. 

However, the individualizing concentration into a unique form presupposes chaos and local systems, as well as oscillations and compensations, because this form constitutes the unifying center of the extremes. Thus, even if the concentrated singularity merely appears to be a judgment in which this particular individuality is the subject and the universal center is the predicate, every individual system is a microcosm of the Idea, precisely because the center between the extremes has attained a real existence. The unique individuality unifies all other individualities of the same system with the substance of this whole, as in a inference of analogy. Not only has every concentrated local system itself—as a unique inference—become one of the moments of the World-System corresponding to the Idea, but these systems are themselves concentrated into the total World-System as the true objectification of the Concept.

With this, however, the fluidity of dialectic has withdrawn the indeterminate multifaceted objectivity back into the unified subjectivity of the Concept. The motley jumble of the World-System has been elevated into the eternal trinity—as universality, particularity, and individuality. Thus, the entire system unfolds as a universal inference that expresses the totality of the Idea in the three moments of the World-Inference.

### World-Inference (Encyclopedia)

In the World-Inference, the highest principle is attained in its highest form, and within it, the Absolute Idea has achieved its perfect expression. For it has now become a circle of circles, each of which encompasses the entire Idea within itself. Thus, the Absolute Idea now appears neither in the immediate form of undeveloped universality existing *in itself*—as the Method—nor in the mediated form of fragmented particularization—as in the World-System, but these moments of universality and particularity—united into absolute individuality—constitute the closed harmony of the Absolute Idea, as the reality that is adequate to its Concept. However, since each of these moments is itself the totality, each presents itself as an inference, whereby the World-Inference of the Absolute Idea comes to be constituted as an inference of inferences. In recapitulating the entire content of the Logic, the World-Inference traverses the stages of Being, Essence, and the Concept in such a way that—under the general form of immediate Being in the first figure—it is an inference of Inherence, under the second figure—as Essence particularizing itself—it is an inference of Reflection, and in the third figure—as the absolute individuality of the Concept—it is an inference of Necessity. 

The World-Inference is thus the architectonic of pure reason—as stated by Kant (*Critique of Pure Reason*, p. 634; 1818 edition). As much as there are the three moments of the Method, as much as they embody the three Systems within the universe, here constitute three distinct sciences. Of these sciences, each one perpetually mirrors this same totality in its own subdivisions and branches, so that we now clearly recognize how Truth encompasses the entirety of Being. Firstly, the Idea of Logic, which we have hitherto examined, is the Absolute Idea in the immediacy of its Being, under the form of universality (U). Secondly, the Idea of Nature is the Absolute Idea in the mediation of its Essence, under the form of particularity (P). Finally, the Absolute Idea returns to itself within Idea of Spirit—the most acute apex of personality (I)—through the closure and completeness of its Concept. The World-Inference is, therefore, itself the inference of the logical Idea in the first figure, that of Nature in the second figure, and that of Spirit in the third figure, wherein the *terminus major* in each respective figure expresses the specific character of the corresponding science.

#### Logic

In the World-Inference of the first figure (I-P-U), ​​Nature acts as the mediating element between Logic and ​Spirit because Nature presents itself to us as that which immediately exists. For this reason, the extremes—Spirit and Logic—stand in equally immediate opposition to it, thus making the inference a qualitative inference of Being. Logic appears in the quality of the universal thought, existing *in-itself* and still unrealized (U). Spirit is the individual Concept—existing *in-and-for-itself*—developed to the highest pinnacle of reality (I). Nature proves itself to be the middle term (P) precisely because it connects the logical thought with its own reality, which is in the process of differentiating itself into particulars. By virtue of this reality, Nature is fitted to elevate the unrealized thought of Logic to its complete reality within Spirit. For Spirit is born out of Nature like an embryo, only to then lead Nature itself toward its own completion by transforming the unconscious thought of Nature into the perfected reality of conscious thought.

While it may appear in this process that Nature—just as it provides the thought that is thought by Spirit—likewise gives reality to Logic, this does not imply that Logic, for that reason, thinks itself. It is an error of theism to hypostatize Logic into a creative, self-conscious person. Logic is simply the universal thought within which Nature and Spirit differentiate and individuate themselves. Hence, this inference is, in turn, merely an inference of universality. It is *only* Spirit that thinks itself, it realizes itself as it thinks the Logic, just as Nature is the realization of the system of logical categories.

The major premise of this inference (P = U) posits that Logic inheres in Nature: the quality of the conceived reality inheres in the quality of the conceived thought, for Nature is precisely the objectification of thought. Hence, Nature appears to be the *prius* and the bearer of Logic. But in the minor premise (I = P), because Nature serves as the birthplace from which Spirit is born, it is simultaneously that which inheres in Spirit as the highest reality: the quality of actual thought inheres in the quality of actual thinking. From this we perceive all the more clearly how Nature constitutes the real middle term that links the extremes of thinking reality and universal thought together. But since, in the conclusion (I = U), Spirit has become the Universal and encompasses within itself both the abstract thought of Logic and the concrete reality of Nature—it is the true bearer of both terms, between which it now positions itself as the mediator of the extremes.

#### Nature

From the conclusion of the first figure (I = U), the second figure (U-I-P) constitutes the Inference of Nature because Nature forms the *terminus major* within it. However, Spirit mediates Nature with Logic in a far more authentic manner than Nature was capable of mediating its own extremes because it is the *thought that thinks itself as being*, whereas Logic is the *thought of being*, and Nature is the *being of thought*. If in the first figure, the being of thought that is Nature forms the *medius terminus* between the thought of being and the thinking thought, then in the second figure, the thinking thought serves as the middle term between the thought of being and the being of thought. As the thought that thinks itself, Spirit encompasses the qualities of thought and being within its own essence as equally thought and being. As the absolute individuality of the thought that thinks itself, Spirit is the totality of the essence which reflects itself into the extremes of the abstract universality of thought and the concrete particularity of reality, treating them as the two sides of its own manifestation.

The major premise of the second figure (I = U) states that Spirit thinks of Logic, and the minor premise (I = P), states that Spirit thinks of Nature. Spirit subsumes both Logic and Nature under its own essence, so the conclusion (U = P) therefore bears the significance of a particular judgment—one in which the Logic presents itself as the active thought within Nature in such a way that *some* logical content is Nature. For this reason, this second figure constitutes the Inference of Nature, just as the first figure was the Inference of Logic. For Nature, as the Idea particularizing itself, attains its Concept by fulfilling its definition as the partial realization of thought. 

However, we cannot (as Rosenkranz does) accept the opposition that Logic is Being positing itself as Thinking, whereas Nature is Thinking positing itself as Being. (Das System der Wissenschaft, § 284; Wissenschaft der logischen Idee, Part II, p. 431.) For by doing so, Rosenkranz remains stuck within an opposition that even he was unable to overcome within the Idea. Instead, we must state that just as the Logic is Thought as Being, so too is Nature Being as Thought. In the conclusion (U = P) of the Inference of Nature, Thought (U) explicitly posits itself as all Being. For while thought is immediately present in Nature (P), it has, through Nature, become a reality for Spirit (I). Therefore, the Logic becomes, at this juncture, the *medius terminus* at this juncture, the middle term unites Nature with Spirit, and in both of them grasps itself as the moving power of dialectic.

#### Spirit

This third figure (P-U-I) brought about by the conclusion of the second figure is now the Inference of Spirit. It no longer possesses an immediate premise, as much as its major premise (P = U) is the conclusion of the second figure, and its minor premise (I = U) is the conclusion of the first figure (§ 110). However, since it is precisely through the conclusion of Nature that we know the logical Idea to be the essence of Nature—just as, through the conclusion of the logical Idea, we know that it simultaneously constitutes the essence of Spirit—the logical Idea functions in this third figure as the *medius terminus*. It disjoins itself as the universal genus into Nature and Spirit—that is, into particularity and individuality—yet thereby relates the members of this disjunction (Nature and Spirit) back to one another in the conclusion B-E, because they themselves have been elevated to the universality of the genus. This syllogism is, therefore, a conceptual syllogism of necessity—which is precisely why it falls under the third figure. For just as Spirit inheres in the logical Idea, and the latter in Nature, so too is Nature subsumed under the logical Idea, and the latter under Spirit.

The conclusion ((P = I) or (I = P)) either states that Nature is Spirit, or Spirit is Nature. Spirit is creator of Nature, and Nature the creation of Spirit, though certainly not miraculously manner, as in the miraculous doctrine of creation found in theology. Instead, because Spirit is thought that knows itself as thought—the thought of the Logic—it therefore knows Nature as well because it is all being that proceeds from and returns to thought. Spirit, however—being itself the highest pinnacle of reality—is not, as the Logic is, merely the *abstract* foundation of reality and merely inheres in the concrete forms thereof. Nor is Spirit like Nature, in the second figure, serving merely as the *appearance* of the Essence, bringing Logic and Spirit to objectivity. Spirit is the firm, unshakeable basis of all reality and no less the most comprehensive universality. For this very reason, however, Spirit unites within itself the highest opposites: the abstract universality of Logic and the concrete particularity of Nature. Just as it subsumes both within itself as the *terminus major*, so too does it simultaneously constitute itself as their eternal bearer and creator. With absolute necessity, it unfolds itself *into* them the constituent aspects of its own reality.

As much as Nature had constituted itself as the *terminus major* in the second figure, there still remained the last trace of an oppositional illusion—that Nature is the essential bearer of Logic and Spirit. In truth, however, Nature is the purely *empirical*, genetic presupposition of Spirit, just as Logic is the purely *ideal*, dialectical presupposition of Philosophy. Spirit, however, is the absolute *prius* in the Logic, and no less than in Nature. The Logic—serving as the mediating center (U)—is the living fluid that permeates all the forms of the world, yet the disjunction of Logic into Nature and Spirit is not a *temporal* act of creation by Nature or Spirit. Instead, Spirit recognizes, solely within the eternal creation of thought, the logical categories as the powers inherent to the subject matter itself, moving dialectically through the constituent members of the universe.

---

We have hereby concluded the exposition of Logic, as the Idea of ​​Science, and now effect the transition into the Philosophy of Nature. However, the fact that this transition constitutes a distinct science is something that we cannot determine in advance, since we can only infer it retrospectively—specifically from the fact that, in the course of our dialectical development, we have arrived at a point where we must now acknowledge that we are no longer operating within the sphere of the pure logical Idea. Whether this is indeed the case will be revealed only by the exposition of the "Second Philosophy" that now follows the exposition that established the Logic as the "First Philosophy". The specific nature of this transition, however, lies in the fact that, having evolved into the Absolute Idea, the Logic simultaneously posits itself as the totality of all reality, as the disjunction of the syllogism of the third figure demonstrated to us. All determinations and oppositions have resolved themselves back into the simplicity of their most primordial immediacy that we termed *Being*. But this *Being* now simultaneously harbors the entire richness and content of reality within its simple bosom. Such a *Being* we designate as *Space*. Within it, all of the separate moments of reality exists simultaneously as an absolutely simple *co-existence*.

Space initially presents itself to us as the first thought of Nature, thereby constituting the transition from Logic to the Philosophy of Nature. If Hegel conceives of this transition as the Idea *resolving* to release itself in the guise of *otherness* out of itself, we need not for that reason imagine—as Schelling mockingly suggested—that the Idea has endured a tedious delay merely to arrive, at long last, from the "thorns" of dialectic to the "roses" of reality. Even less, as the same philosopher maintains, should we despair at attaining to reality by means of pure reason, or cast ourselves into the arms of divine revelation in order to recognize the truth. As we have already demonstrated, pure reason is the whole of reality itself. As a result, the Idea does not, strictly speaking, ever truly *release* reality from itself as something *other* than itself for any significant duration, and still less does it *abandon* itself when it releases itself into otherness. After all, one can only release from oneself what one already encompasses within oneself, so Schelling’s vulgar and offensive jests regarding this expression constitutes no refutation. The otherness of the Logic is itself one of its own thoughts—the thought of its own bound state or opposition—within which it still remains the Idea. Although, to be sure, Spirit eventually arrives as the Redeemer to liberate the thought that held captive to Nature—like a new Hercules freeing a new Prometheus from his bonds.

Thus, in turning to Nature and Spirit—as the *applied* Logic—we neither execute a *salto mortale* (deadly leap) nor commit a *metabasis eis allo genos* (transition into another genus), but we remain firmly within the sphere of thought. Henceforth, we shall be dealing with philosophical thoughts, save only that these are no longer the categories of *Logic*, but the more concrete categories of Nature and Spirit, which will dialectically unfold and result from one another before our very eyes.
