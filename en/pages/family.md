# Family

> This webpage is a direct translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Natural Law, Vol. I* (1866) 

## Marriage

### Solemnization of Marriage

As regards the conditions of marriage, the original condition is the autonomous decision of two legal persons to enter into this relationship. However, since it is a matter of contingency which specific persons are brought together by love, it is the arbitrary nature of their mutual agreement that constitutes the solemnization of the marriage. Marriage thus originates as a contract, which may indeed be concluded in writing in the form of a formal marriage vow. Since, however, the free choice of the lovers constituted the other essential element, the persons independent of one another cannot be so closely related as to already constitute a natural unity of persons. For the unity in question is intended to be not a natural one, but a unity mediated through freedom itself. Since marriage, according to this view, is a ethical relationship, one that is merely preceded by a contract, the marriage vow cannot compel the marriage itself. Instead, should one party withdraw, it can at most entail financial compensation, just as stipulations aimed at preventing financial loss in the event of a potential dissolution of the marriage may indeed be established within it.

However, if the marriage is not yet regarded as concluded solely by virtue of the marriage vows, then secondly, wherein does its actual conclusion consist? Even sexual union as such does not yet constitute the consummation of the marriage, however one might choose to label such a relationship as "natural". In truth, however, the merely natural union of the sexes is termed *concubinage*. Kant effectively reduces marriage to this very level when he defines it (in *The Metaphysical Elements of Justice* § 24) as a "union of two persons of different sexes for the lifelong mutual possession of their sexual attributes." The best proof that the laws themselves do not regard even consummated sexual intercourse as a fully valid conclusion of marriage lies in the fact that even a pregnant bride cannot sue for the actual solemnization of the marriage based merely on a promise to marry, but instead acquires only the rights of a divorced wife should the groom refuse to honor his vow. However, if the marriage is to take place, then as a symbol of the moral unity of the persons contained therein, a formal conclusion of the union before the two assembled families, representing the pre-existing ethical relationships from which a third relationship is now to emerge, is required. The marriage is entered into the records of a family council, not into those of the municipality or the church, and it is this act alone that renders it fully valid and legally recognized. Marriage is a civil institution, this form alone is obligatory, whereas the religious solemnization by a member of the clergy is left entirely to the discretion of the individuals involved. Although marriage possesses a religious aspect, in its essence it remains a purely civil institution.

The fact that marriage is not a mere contract is demonstrated by its ethical purpose. Property has been termed a *real* right, and the contract a *personal* right, although both, albeit in different ways, partake in both characteristics. Marriage, like all familial relationships, is intended to be a *real and personal* right. This does not mean, however, that a person may be treated like an object, a notion which appears to be implied in the aforementioned Kantian definition of marriage. Instead, whereas the object of contracts concerns only isolated performances by persons, in marriage it is, strictly speaking, an aggregate of performances. Indeed, the entire substance of a person becomes the object of another's freedom, and yet this occurs without thereby infringing upon the freedom of the former, for that person receives in return the entire substance of the second person as the object of their own freedom. From this follows the principle of monogamy and the timelessness of marriage. For if one party were to be married to multiple partners, the latter would forfeit their freedom due to the unequal assignment. Conversely, limiting marriage to a specific duration would entail an imperfect union of persons. Thus, in marriage, as the Romans defined it, established the *mutuum adiutorium* (mutual assistance) and the *consortium totius vitae* (partnership for life). While marriage is not precluded by the cessation of its natural purpose of love, it is nonetheless generally to be assumed that it is entered into *liberorum procreandorum causa* (for the sake of procreating children). The timelessness of marriage is, however, admittedly only its *purpose*, a moral imperative. Clearly if the ethical unity of spirits has vanished, it would be immoral to keep them shackled together.

### Rights and Duties of Spouses

From the ethical purpose of marriage, the rights and duties of the spouses also appear. The common will of spouses constitutes a single personality directed outward toward third parties, for it is precisely within this will that the incomplete persons become a single, unified ethical personality. This in no way precludes a shared process of deliberation and decision-making, since, depending on the specific matter at hand, the preponderance of insight may lie now with one spouse more than the other. Particularly with regard to property, the distinct rights and duties of the spouses appear most clearly in the context of its acquisition and preservation. For in this context, the shared means of subsistence, constituting the couple’s assets, has taken the place of individual ownership. Since spouses constitute a single legal person, the most ethical arrangement is a community of property. However, if in view of the possibility that this marital unity might dissolve, then pursuant to such a contract, each spouse may indeed reserve their own assets and retains the right of free disposal over them even during the marriage. In such a case, one spouse cannot act as the other's external representative in any matters pertaining to the assets that are reserved by them. In this regard, however—speaking from the standpoint of philosophy—we must here call upon positive law to grant the wife that equality of rights which she currently still lacks. Nevertheless, even where separate ownership between spouses has been expressly stipulated, the use thereof will invariably be shared. For each spouse will, after all, be required to contribute from their own assets toward the household expenses, and generally speaking, love restores the marital community of property that the marriage contract had abolished.

### Dissolution of Marriage

The possibility of the dissolution of marriage lies in the fact that the family is a relationship of natural ethicality, yet the aspect of naturalness inherently bears within itself the capacity for change. This mutability concerns, on the one hand, the sentiment of love, which precisely because it rests upon natural sympathy, it is possible for it to fade away. On the other hand, love extends even to the entire natural existence of one of the spouses, which may be terminated by death. In the former case, the Catholic Church has adhered consistently to the consequence flowing from the concept of indissolubility, permitting no divorce. The *inconsistency* of the Protestant Church, however, constitutes a *higher* consistency. For to refuse to sever the bond when the sentiment has vanished is, in truth, no different from servitude. Even the current practice of Protestant orthodoxy, which recognizes only adultery and malicious desertion as grounds for divorce, while refusing to remarry a spouse divorced on other grounds, demonstrates the necessity of civil marriage. This necessity is demanded even more imperatively, moreover, by the insistence of the Catholic clergy upon the Catholic upbringing of children in mixed marriages. The commission of a criminal act, such as an act of physical abuse, truly demonstrates that the ethical unity of the two persons has already been dissolved. And the final ground, that there exists insurmountable aversion, is by no means the worst. Even the Catholic Church cannot entirely evade this higher logic, and it therefore permits, at the very least, separation *a mensa et thoro*. But in doing so, it commits a further inconsistency: failing to legally dissolve a marriage that has already been dissolved *de facto*, and thereby prohibiting either party from remarrying during the lifetime of the other, a policy that has nothing but immorality, adultery, and concubinage in its wake.

However, if death dissolves the marriage, the question arises as to where the shared assets should go. We shall answer this question by assuming, for the moment, a childless marriage, without also formulating the answer in such a way that it would become inapplicable were children present. If community of property between spouses exists, then answer is simple: the survivor receives one half as their own property, because during the lifetime of the deceased, they already held it as their joint property. The question becomes more difficult only when a separation of property existed between the spouses, and thus the transfer of ownership to the survivor must be explained by recourse to the concept of inheritance. However, the rationality of the right of inheritance is a highly contested matter, so this institution therefore stands in urgent need of justification. To this end, one proceeds from the concept of the acquisition of property, as if the right of inheritance was only a mode of acquiring property that we had simply overlooked among those we enumerated earlier. Roman jurists even term the right of inheritance a *universal* mode of acquisition, in as much as one acquires through inheritance not only a single item of property, but an entire bundle of rights. Yet the right of inheritance can never constitute an acquisition of property, neither an original nor a derivative one, and therefore has no place whatsoever within private law. For where are the agreement and the transferential will that are requisite for a derivative mode of acquisition? The deceased cannot effect a transfer *mortis causa*, for they are literally no longer a person, and as long as they *are* a free person, they have no desire to effect such a transfer. Thus, regarding inheritance, the only remaining option would be to posit an *original* mode of acquisition, as if the relatives typically gathered around the deceased were to acquire the now ownerless estate through *occupation*, a principle which the laws would then have established as the rule in all cases. The flaw in such explanations, however, lies in treating the family assets as the *individual* property belonging to a head of the family. Instead, they constitute the *common* property of the family members, assets which, upon the death of one of the co-owners, become subject to actual partition among them. In other words, to substantiate the rationality of the law of inheritance, we must invariably fall back upon the rationality of the *community of property*, even in instances where positive law, or the arbitrary will of the spouses, has formally excluded it.

## Parentage

In as much as the unity of persons, which exists in itself and appears within marriage as the ethical disposition of spouses, is realized in their child as the objective existence of another personality, it follows that that the moral will of the child is intended to be nothing other than the manifestation of the unified will of the parents. This constitutes the concept of parental authority over children, an authority which we must now examine in terms of its origin, in terms of its existing rights and duties, and in terms of its dissolution.

### Origin of Parentage

Whereas in marriage, naturally distinct individuals constitute the immediate precondition of the parental relationship, the result of this relationship is the fusion of the differences of spouses within the personality of the child, and in whom these differences cease to possess a separate existence. Therefore, parental authority first comes into being through procreation.

However, even if the child represents the enduring neutrality of the parents' opposing natures, they nevertheless take after one of the two natures. In its spiritual aspect, since every child inherits as much of the father's characteristics as of the mother's, every human being is a unity of the two sexes, transforming the physical traits, temperament, and character of both parents into a new personality. Hence, within the relationship of parental authority, even at the very moment of its inception, the element of arbitrariness that played role in the contract of marriage is now excluded. The unity of the persons involved is no longer arbitrary, but is brought forth through a necessary activity of nature that has itself been given an ethical significance. The actualization of the parents' spiritual unity, which had originally existed only as a possibility, the child initially possesses its freedom only within the freedom of their parents. Having been designated by nature itself as the actual representative of their will, the child constitutes the object of their activity. 

But just as marriage can confine itself to the purely spiritual aspect, within the *consortium totius vitæ* (partnership of the whole of life), so too can parentage arise without any natural unity of blood (without procreation), through free will and purely spiritual love. For in any ethical relationship, the spiritual aspect is to be regarded as the primary element. This is the nature of adoption. In such cases, naturally, the separate property of the adopted child are merged into the common property of the family.

While in adoption, the exclusively spiritual aspect may very well compensate for the absence of a natural connection between parents and children, conversely, the natural fact of procreation by no means automatically entails the ethical relationship. Through illegitimate procreation, the natural father or mother acquires no rights of parental authority over the child whatsoever, for they do not have any spiritual connection person with them. The spiritual consecration of the relationship between parent and child is established solely through marriage. However, since the mother originally exists in a natural unity of person with the child through the very act of nature itself, illegitimate procreation can still bring about a moral unity of persons between the mother and child. But it is immoral to attempt to create a highly ambiguous relationship between father and child through paternity suits and demands for maintenance payments and upbringing costs. Napoleonic legislation put an end to those judicial proceedings, which invariably gave rise to scandal, through the principle: *La recherche de la paternité est interdite* (The search for paternity is prohibited). But this does not preclude the reservation of the free will of the father and mother to add the spiritual dimension to the natural one, such that the father combines natural procreation with adoption through the legitimation of illegitimate children, whether indirectly through subsequent marriage to the mother (*per subsequens matrimonium*) or through a formal proceeding before a council of both families. The *levis notæ macula* (stigma of slight disgrace) attached to illegitimate children is a relic of the crude mentality of the Middle Ages, an era that fancied itself truly religious precisely when it treated the natural aspect, the very element requiring integration, with nothing but contempt.

### Rights and Duties of Parents and Children

If parentage has arisen in one of the three ways indicated, the second question that arises is: what constitutes the content of this ethical relationship during its existence? To this end, we must first elucidate the nature and status of the parties involved in this relationship, then specify the influence that this difference exerts upon family property, and finally, we must examine their reciprocal conduct that maintains the relationship itself.

#### Parents and Children

Within the family in general, the natural individual initially appears as one-sided, deficient, and imperfect, a being that is, in the context of the ethical relationship and through mutual assistance, supposed to mature into a complete ethical person. This is also the case in the relationship between parents and children. However, their natural difference must evolve into a spiritual one, and ultimately, their one-sided positions must be balanced out precisely by learning from each other.

In the relationship of parent and child, one party is the active, generating element, and the other is the passive, generated element. From this natural distinction, both parties combine these two determinations only from different perspectives, in as much as the parents are themselves children of their own parents, and the children will become parents by having their own children. From this arises a progression that unfolds within the infinite succession of generations that mutually generate and presuppose one another. The family thereby represents the unity of the human race as a species. The unequal position of each individual link in this chain, in their relation to one another, constitutes the natural source of their differing rights and duties. The children, as the potential existence of free personality, grows in strength precisely as the strength of the parents begins to fade.

The spiritual difference between parent and child lies in the fact that, precisely because of the helplessness of children who have yet to mature into free personalities, parents exercise authority over their children in order to bring their free will to fruition. For children are, both naturally and spiritually, dependents (*alieni juris*) and not masters of themselves (*sui juris*). The development of spirit consists precisely in the task of acquiring freedom, something that is neither immediate nor natural. To this end, precisely because of their helplessness, children require the support of the parents' already developed strength of will. To the extent that parents assist in the maturation of their children, knowing full well that with this maturation the requirement of their support begins to wane, lies the selflessness of parental action, whereas the children, in their act of receiving support, manifest a certain egoism, grow in strength due to the sacrifices their parents' have made for them. Nevertheless, parents act in this selfless manner because they seek to bring their own unity of will to objective existence within the child, to perpetuate their spirit through their children. For this reason, parents love their children as their own rejuvenated selves, upon whom they wish to bestow the benefit of all their life's experiences.

Although the relationship between parents and children is, on the one hand, of a higher nature than the relationship between spouses, this relationship is also, on the other hand, of a lower nature than the marital bond, into which both parties enter with perfectly equal rights. A third relationship arises out of the two one-sided relationships of the marital bond and parentage such that the highest form of family relationship is thereby attained. In this bond, the parent and child exchange their natural and spiritual attributes with one another, such that each party ultimately arrives at the very point from which the other originally set out. The more strength that children acquire through the self-sacrificing care of their parents, the more that the strength of the parents begins to wane. In their old age, they become just as helpless and dependent as their children were in the beginning, and the moral duty inherent in the family bond, one that transcends natural necessity through a shared unity of action, is now passed on to the children. The child now cares for and protect their parents, thereby giving back to them in their old age precisely what they themselves received from them in their youth.

#### Family Property

If the parental will finds objective expression in the person of the child itself, it finds an even greater expression in the child's property. Just as the property of spouses is held in common, this must also be the case with the property of the children, as long as they have not yet attained consciousness of their own personality, and with it, the legal capacity to dispose of property. Admittedly, gifts can be made to the children by strangers or relatives on the express condition that the parents have no power of disposal over them. These are stipulations that come into full effect only after the family has dissolved, for it is only then that the children receive, as a prior allotment, the assets reserved to them, and having been released from parentage, are thereby free to manage and dispose of them independently. However, so long as the child is still a dependent, the family retains the right of usufruct and the right of administration.

However, precisely because the purpose of the family is to raise children into free persons, this unity of property must necessarily dissolve as the children become increasingly independent. Just as a husband may bestow a morning gift upon his wife, so may a father provide his daughter with a dowry, or give his son the means to establish himself in a profession and to embark upon the endeavor of personal independence. By virtue of the child subsequently acquiring assets through their own independent efforts, they become a legal person independent of their parents, and is thereby an owner of property in their own right. 

#### Childhood Education

The means by which children become autonomous, explicitly articulated as a duty of the parents, is the education of children. At the same time, this is one of most significant of rights that follows from parentage. Since the task of education is to elevate children from a potential personality to an actual personality, the body itself must first be nurtured to this end, secondly, the natural drives must be disciplined, and thirdly, the individual must be elevated to freedom through the actual cultivation of the spirit itself. Physical, moral and intellectual education are thus the three necessary components of every true theory of childhood education.

##### Physical Education (Nurture)

The mother's primary task following the birth of a child is, as Aristotle states, its *nourishment* (θρέψαι), whereas its *formation* (παιδεῦσαι), its physical upbringing, is the primary task of the father. The nurturing the child by the mother constitutes the prerequisite for all subsequent physical health, just as physical health, in turn, serves as the prerequisite for all sound intellectual development: *mens sana in corpore sano* (a sound mind in a sound body).

As a further aspect of physical education, Aristotle places particular emphasis on *movement*. Indeed, the question arises as to whether Plato’s counsel to let children bounce and dance in their arms might not also possess a philosophical dimension. Nourishment, such as the intake of milk, amounts only to an augmentation of *substance* or bodily fluids. But true health also entails *digestion* and *assimilation*, that is, physical education itself possesses an aspect wherein the natural or material element must be *negated* in order to be properly processed. That this process is best facilitated through physical movement becomes self-evident in light of natural philosophy, which defines movement as the negation (at least in a spatial sense) of the material body. After all, nothing aids digestion more effectively than a brisk walk after a meal. Movement as such, however, is not the only means of exercising and strengthening the body. Through the art of gymnastics (the *gymnastikē* of the ancients), the body also becomes agile and dexterous, enabling the individual, in all circumstances, to recognize their mastery over nature and to conduct themselves as a free person in relation to it. Swimming, jumping, running, wrestling, fencing, and all other sports fall under this category. When Rousseau protests against the swaddling of infants and the binding of their hands and feet, he is entirely justified in his objection to this restriction of movement. But if one were to adopt a diametrically opposite approach, leaving the baby entirely unswaddled, surely a happy medium ought to be struck. For precisely at that stage when the baby is not yet capable of autonomous movement, this primary source of physical instruction must not be withdrawn too abruptly.

Just as the negative aspect of physical education comes to the fore in movement, so too does the spiritual aspect. Even the acquisition of physical skills through movement, which is the primary task of gymnastics, also has a spiritual dimension. With the agility of the body, the agility of the spirit develops as well. If the purpose of education is to raise children to become free spirits, then spiritual activities must be stimulated early on and during the very course of their physical education. Physical movements must serve as means to gradually prepare children for the spiritual pursuits that are destined to constitute the substance of their lives once they have reached adulthood, i.e. play. The child freely expresses themselves in play, imitating the activities of adults as they unfold upon the stage of civic life. For the child, imagination compensates for whatever is lacking in reality. In play, children envision themselves as grown-ups. But education is not complete with play alone. An education that confines itself to play, one that seeks to impart everything to children solely in a lighthearted, comical manner, does not achieve the essential negative aspect inherent in education: the necessity of freeing children from the naturalness and immediacy of their desires.

##### Moral Education (Discipline)

The child originates in a state of selfishness, seeking to satisfy their natural drives at the expense of everyone else, and by whatever means necessary. This selfishness has its positive aspect, for in the realm of morality, our drives are purified rather than suppressed. In physical education, this selfishness is taken into account, and the individual’s physical self is developed. But in equal measure, the individual must also acknowledge that there are other selves, and that they must sacrifice their own particular, selfish drives to the extent that they conflict with reason. However, because the child is unconscious of their own reason, it initially appears to them as something external until they have become conscious of it. The pleasure to which they surrender themselves must be met by a corresponding displeasure. The enjoyment they have indulged in that is followed by pain serves as a sign that such pleasure should not to have been indulged because it is inhibited and curbed by this very pain. This constitutes the concept of *chastisement*. Here, punishment does not carry the significance of retributive justice (as in criminal law), but of moral improvement and deterrence, though justice is a equally necessary element here as is in the former context. The archetypal model of chastisement, as envisioned by Rousseau, can be termed *objective chastisement*, whenever pain follows the child’s selfish act as a matter of natural necessity, e.g. a child upsets their stomach and makes themselves sick because they have eaten too much.

In his *Emile*, Rousseau quite consistently insists that chastisement can never appear to children as an arbitrary act on the part of their parents, for children would all too easily deduce from this the principle that arbitrariness and injustice are the forces opposing their own actions, and that their own arbitrariness is just as legitimate as that of their parents' discipline. One logical consequence of this approach is the complete abandonment of corporal punishment. Carrying out this punishment, such as in the form of a blow or a slap, is physically harmful, damaging their delicate muscles, bones, and brain. Since it is the necessity of reason, rather than arbitrariness, that must be pitted against the child's capricious impulses, it follows that even in instances where nature does not punish immediately, the parents' disciplinary action must be presented as an *indirect* extension of nature's own immediate punishment, so that the parents' *unmediated* exercise of authority remains concealed. In other words, Rousseau calls for a constant conspiracy involving parents, neighbors, and others. Whenever a child does something wrong, they must be exposed to its negative consequences until they realize that they would have been better off if had they heeded their parents' advice.

Rousseau’s doctrine is, in principle, entirely correct. It is the inherent logic of the situation, not the arbitrary will of the parents, that must become apparent to the child. They have to recognize that they depend upon necessity, and they are bound it as to a higher power. Necessity cannot confront them as a blind force, but only as free reason, reason that is destined to become the child’s own—and as the true nature of things. That said, from the fact that it would likely prove difficult to conceal the discipline of the parents at every turn through a continuous conspiracy, it wouldn't even be desirable for the parents to do so. For once the child has recognized their parents’ counsel as sound, they begin to place their trust in them. Reason gradually becomes embodied in the will of the parents, and in that will, the child recognizes not arbitrariness, but necessity.

To the extent that rationality reveals itself to children as a will distinct from their own, one that is not yet *theirs*, they come to the realization that their own self-interest is not the highest good. They sense that they are not yet what they ought to be. They learn obedience as a result of discipline, precisely so that, through this very obedience, they will not need it anymore. Their obedience is an enlightened obedience, not a blind one. And here as well, educational theory has once again raised the question of which approach is best. Admittedly, if one chooses to base one’s upbringing on the attempt to guide children through didactic reasoning, one must accommodate oneself to the level of their intellect. One must present them with a limited perspective, one by which they are to be persuaded to act or to refrain from acting. However, an opposing and equally limited perspective readily presents itself, arguments beget counter-arguments. The child’s intellect, within its own limited scope, is remarkably acute, grasping counter-arguments with perfect accuracy, and often leaving us in astonishment. The child counters the parental argument with one of its own, and the educator finds themselves at the end of their wits, unless he chooses to resolve the matter by decree. Such a decree, however, comes too late, for the children perceive the standpoint offered to them as a "final" one as being, merely the *last* in a sequence, something they are done with and have already moved on from. Through this method of upbringing, their obstinacy, conceit, and spirit of contradiction have been awakened, and in the rejection of their own arguments, they see nothing but parental arbitrariness. 

Children easily perceive the childishness of the arguments presented to them, they know full well that they do not represent anything actually permanent or absolute. Such an approach to child education has forfeited all trust in the eyes of the children themselves, with some even come to despise such educators. With this, however, obedience naturally vanishes, and the result is the utmost confusion and disorder. Therefore, when I speak of *enlightened* obedience, I refer to one that is validated by experience, one in which children, whether through natural circumstance or deliberate design, have learned to recognize that their parents' counsel was sound. In other words, the parents' rational will must prove its worth not only with words, but also with deeds. In their parents' will, the child comes to love their own better selves, becoming one with their parents as free spirits. Taking the place of blind obedience, a joyful obedience joins itself to this enlightened form.

From this obedience, however, follows a reverence for one’s parents, striving to emulate them and become grown-up and rational as they are. One must not stoop down to the level of the child without simultaneously lifting them up to one’s own level, and these two aspects must not be separated. The act of stooping down serves as a means of gaining the foothold by which one draws them upward. If this reverence, or the very possibility of it, is lacking (for instance, if the parents are unworthy), then domestic upbringing becomes impossible. Instead of serving as a guide toward virtue, the home becomes a nursery for vice.

##### Intellectual Education (Instruction)

If upbringing is intended to foster sociality, specifically by negating selfishness, thereby preparing the child to enter the spiritual community of humanity, there finally exists a drive that requires neither transformation nor refinement to develop into spiritual freedom. Instead, in its very naturalness, it already encompasses the entire fullness of spiritual existence. This drive is the drive for knowledge, which is satisfied through instruction. The natural curiosity of children elevates itself to a noble thirst for knowledge as it directs itself (and is guided by instructors) toward every object of natural or spiritual existence. This constitutes the positive aspect of education, which despite being spiritual in nature, nevertheless remains in harmony with the nature. Through teaching and instruction, the individual is meant to internalize the entirety of the natural and spiritual world. However, notwithstanding this act of reception and passive absorption, the individual is destined to rise to the level of free, active thought. Here, the natural drive itself furnishes the individual with spiritual content.

In public education, the limitations of domestic education are manifest, limits that come into play partly because of the contingency that parents may very well be morally incapable of instructing their children, and partly due to the necessity that scientific instruction in all subjects vastly exceeds the capabilities of any one parent. Yet the boundary of the family, specifically that of parentage, is not merely established in this external manner. Instead, the parental will sets its own limits, in as much as it also, through the very process of upbringing, works to render itself superfluous. Thus, we must now consider the dissolution of parentage brought about by the completion of upbringing itself.

### Dissolution of Parentage

The basis for the dissolution of legal parentage may lie partly in external circumstances and partly in internal contingencies. Thus, three modes by which legal parentage ceases is emancipation, death, and the attainment of majority.

Should the parents wish to dissolve the relationship through emancipation, either another family must be granted guardianship of the child, in which case the negative act of emancipation is counterbalanced by a positive act of adoption, or the child must have already attained a degree of independence sufficient to enable them to support themselves. An emancipation of this latter type may not take place prior to the child’s twentieth year, precisely because it is presumed that, by that time, the primary objectives of the child’s upbringing have already been achieved. Thus, only after the fulfillment of this ethical duty is free scope granted to individual discretion. Furthermore, emancipation does not sever the ethical bond between parents and children, rather, even as free individuals, they remain ethically bound to one another, a fact that will become evident, for instance, in the consequences pertaining to inheritance. 

Opposed to this dissolution of paternal authority, brought about by internal contingency of free will, stands the dissolution resulting from an external contingency of nature. The dissolution of parentage through death entails, once again, that each child receives their specific share of the common estate for actual use. Previously, by contrast, for as long as the unity of the family subsisted, the child possessed a share of the common estate only as an ideal portion of the bare ownership. For the right of inheritance is not a transfer, nor a form of derivative ownership, but rather, in the very act of transition, at the same time an original acquisition. This is because the heirs were, by virtue of the original unity of persons, already the original owners. As Plutarch expresses it so aptly: they were *in ownership* (ἐν κτήσει) and now enter into *use* (ἐν χρήσει), a state for which the Roman jurist Paulus employs the term *administratio*.

In considering this mode of original acquisition of property brought about by the external contingency of nature, if we conceive of internal arbitrariness (i.e. the exercise of free will) as being entirely excluded, then we arrive at the law of intestate succession for children. Here, it is not difficult to determine, in accordance with the inherent rationality of the matter, what the children ought to receive. If one of the spouses has already passed away, the children divide the entire inheritance among themselves in equal shares, in as much as no one child represents the deceased testator any more or any less than another. However, if one spouse still survives, to whom one half of the estate falls, then the children divide the remaining half between themselves in the very same manner as they would have divided the whole estate in the previous instance.

The arbitrary will of the testator, standing in complete opposition to the ethical relations of intestate succession, constitutes the law of testamentary succession (*hereditas secundum tabulas*). In a testament, the arbitrary will of one property owner appears to transfer assets to another property owner. Thus, we seem to have fallen entirely back into the sphere of legality, placing ourselves outside the ethical sphere of the family. Indeed, the testament has been subsumed into purely legal relations in such a way that it was construed as a benevolent contract, specifically as a gift, by being classified alongside the *donatio mortis causa*, the counterpart of which being the *donatio inter vivos*. However, the difficulty we previously raised in the context of marital law, regarding inheritance law in general simply as transfer of property, reappears with even greater intensity in relation to the testament. Moreover, the rational justification based on the unity of persons, which served as the foundation for intestate succession, does not appear to be applicable in the case of the testamentary heir. We refer to children as heirs by nature, whereas the Romans called them *heredes sui* or heirs of themselves. Assuming that the testament is not intended to be a mere documentary confirmation of the right of intestate succession (in which case it would be superfluous), the simple question can be posed: Is it rational for a property owner who has no natural heirs to relinquish their assets to an *extraneus* (a stranger) by means of a testament? If it is rational, then the basis for this rationality must originate from the same principle in which it was found in the case of intestate succession. In other words, just as adoption creates an artificial filiation in the absence of a natural one, so too is the testament such an adoption effective upon death: an *adoptio mortis causa*, not a *donatio mortis causa*. Therefore, the testament is the declaration that a purely spiritual circle of friends has stepped in for the absence of the natural family, and thus becomes entitled to share in all of the family's property rights. Consequently, it is the "family of the spirit" that is intended to inherit through the testament, thereby bringing to realization the ethical principle that all things are held in common among friends. The share of the inheritance designated by the testator should, in this light, be understood as a measure of the degree of friendship, the unity of persons that existed between the heir and the testator.

Finally, the ethical dissolution of parentage is the attainment of legal majority by the children, a state which, by virtue of their intellectual and physical development, renders them capable of leaving their original family to create one of their own. The very basis of parentage originates from the inequality between children and parents, an inequality necessitating that the children rely upon the superior will of the parents for their guidance, in as much as they were not yet capable of guiding themselves. Now as the parents themselves have, partly through the fulfillment of their duty of upbringing, eliminated this inequality, and Nature, for its part, allows children to mature of their own accord. Therein lies precisely the ethical dissolution of parentage as unity of the natural and the intellectual grounds for its cessation, whereas in the act of emancipation, only the intellectual ground is present, and in the event of death, only the natural ground for cessation came into play. However, the exact age at which legal majority commences is a matter of positive legal determination, one that falls outside the province of philosophy, depending instead upon geographical latitude, the physical constitution of the people in question, and other such considerations. The Romans, for instance, whose laws generally tended to favor the retention of parentage, did not recognize this threshold until the completion of the twenty-fifth year. Generally speaking, however, the Romans did not conceive of legal majority as constituting an ethical dissolution of the family unit at all, but merely as the termination of guardianship. As long as the father or grandfather was still alive, even a fully grown son, even if he was a Consul, remained subject to parentage. While the Romans thus merely oscillated between the two extreme poles of alienation and servitude, German law arrived at the ethical resolution of parentage without the familial bond thereby being severed. In Rome, by contrast, the daughter’s marriage did not even dissolve parentage unless she lived with her husband in a strict form of marriage (*in manu mariti*), in which case, rather than remaining the father’s slave, she became her husband's. Since, in this manner, the independence thus gained by individuals does not yet constitute the complete dissolution of the family, but rather the family persists even amidst its dissolution, we must now, thirdly, examine the familial relationships that pertain to the family in this state of dissolution, i.e. extended kinship.

## Kinship

With the members of the family becoming independent, the dissolution of the family is already pronounced, just as marriage itself, by virtue of the independence of the legal persons entering into it, was only the family *in the making*. Parental authority constitutes the family that has attained full actuality and enduring existence. However, since the independent persons did not begin their existence as free legal subjects, but arose from the ethical unity of the family, they represent the family *in the process of dissolution*, a process that is itself a form of family relationship. Specifically, this dissolution is, in the first instance, a process *in the making*, occurring within the actual persistence of the family bond itself, i.e. the relationship of siblings. Secondly, we encounter the family *during* its dissolution, a state in which the original unity is reduced to a mere possibility, i.e. the relationship of relatives. Finally, there is the family that has been actually *dissolved*, a state where even the bond of blood kinship has ceased to apply, yet which remains a genuine family relationship among independent persons, i.e. the relationship of neighbors.

### Siblings

If we consider the sibling relationship in relation to the parental one, the question arises whether, upon the death of a brother or sister, the surviving siblings must share the estate with the ascending line of relatives or whether they succeed to the inheritance exclusively. Naturally, were one to measure the right of inheritance strictly by proximity of kinship, parents stand in the first degree of kinship, whereas siblings stand only in the second, so according to this, the former would take precedence over the latter. But in positive law, we observe that the siblings inherit *per capita* alongside one another, which is a more reasonable arrangement. For parents and siblings are not only related to one another, they partake in a unity of personhood and blood, wherein no distinction of degree exists. Just as the testator stands alongside their children as a single, co-equal legal person, so too is the property of a childless testator regarded as if it still constituted their common property. In this scenario, the surviving spouse would likewise receive one half of the estate. The testator, together with their parents or the grandparents and their siblings, constitutes a single collective personhood, every member of which possesses an equal, ideal share in the common spirit of the family. It is entirely in order that full siblings take precedence over half-siblings, for they have necessarily belonged to said family unit. However, when half-siblings do succeed to an inheritance, it is entirely immaterial whether the deceased’s estate was derived from the parent shared by them or from the parent not shared by them. The inheritance pertaining to the shared parent has already been settled, so the matter at hand solely concerns the deceased’s independent estate, which may just as well have acquired through their own efforts or received from a third party. Conversely, half-siblings must accept the necessity of sharing the inheritance with an ascendant whom they do not share with the deceased, for by virtue of their own blood relationship with the deceased, and the deceased’s blood relationship with his ascendant, an artificial unity of persons must be presumed to exist between that ascendant and the half-siblings, which may as well have actually existed in the manner previously described.

### Relatives

Whereas in the sibling relationship, the unity of persons within the family bond has certainly existed as a reality, in the relationship of relatives, by contrast, the commonality of family life and of parental authority is only a *possibility*, a presupposition that chronologically precedes the individual members, and is only actual on the condition that their shared grandparents are still alive. We can conceive of this situation as a reality in cases where the parents have predeceased the grandparents, and so their children find themselves under the parentage of their shared grandparent(s). 

In as much as the relationship of relatives has its basis in the sibling relationship, representing the further development of the latter through the progression of successive generations *ad infinitum*, so too must the associated right of inheritance be traced back to the very same source. Here, the testament, or the actual, spiritual family, invariably prevails over the potential, natural family. Furthermore, in the law of intestate succession, collateral relatives always inherit *per stirpes* (by stocks) or by lineage, never *per capita* (by heads). The basis of the inheritance is no longer the equal entitlement of individual persons within the unity of the family, but their common descent from siblings. Thus, it is not individuals, but rather the individual *stems* that appear as co-equal heirs. The stem of a deceased sibling holds equal rights with that of a surviving one, just as in direct succession to a grandparent's estate, the grandchildren of a single child collectively receives only one share. For here as well, they do not inherit as individuals, but as a *stem*, a representative of the deceased that takes their place and constitutes them as a single legal person in relation to the testator. Where the right of inheritance is cut off by law due to excessively remote degrees of kinship, Trendelenburg in his *Natural Law* advances the sound idea that the inheritance should not be appropriated by the public treasury, but should devolve to the local community to be utilized for the endowment of charitable purposes.

### Mentorship

The outermost form of family relationship, in which the natural blood relation derived from a common lineage ceases to exist, is between the mentor and apprentice. This relationship announces itself as the very boundary of an ethical relationship by the fact that its members not only enter into it by means of a contract, as is the case with marriage, but that the relationship simultaneously remains a contract throughout its entire duration. However, there are certain ethical duties attached to this contract, even if they cease to exist the moment the contractual agreement is no longer in force. Thus, this relationship constitutes a remarkable blend of an ethical relationship and a contract. Moreover, it also combines the defining characteristics of the first two family relationships, marriage and parentage, in an even more interconnected manner than the sibling relationship itself. Alongside the complete personal freedom of both individuals involved, there is simultaneously a relationship of dependency, similar to that found in parentage. However, whereas this dependency is, in the case of parentage, a condition established by nature, here it is a dependency that is voluntarily, and therefore morally, willed by the apprentice themselves. A free legal person voluntarily places themselves under the parentage of their mentor, yet they do so not in such a way that the reciprocal return is deferred until a later time, but rather in a manner where the exchange always exists simultaneously and reciprocally. The neediness of the individual, which is intended to be compensated for by these moral services—is no longer a natural need, but stems from an inequality of intellectual cultivation, whereby the mentor, by virtue of their superior education, delegates certain duties to the apprentice, while the apprentice, by undertaking these duties, is at the same time destined to share in the higher education and prosperity of the mentor. 

To the extent that the ethical relationship is preserved within the mentorship, notwithstanding the dissolution of the family, we have thereby moved beyond the family without abandoning ethicality. Instead, ethicality has assumed a higher form by having transcended its natural origins. It is free persons who are involved here, persons who do not, as in marriage, re-enter a state of ethicality by lineage, but rather, ethicality remains purely spiritual, mediated solely through the free will of the individuals involved. To put it another way, in the mentorship, free legal persons facing one another persons who not only pursue moral ends of mutual assistance in their social dealings with one another, but also, in their personal lives, pursue moral ends by mutually assisting the members of their own family. This same duality of ethicality, directed both inward and outward, also arises from the two preceding relationships, which itself belongs to the dissolving family structure. As brothers and sisters found new families, a process that is repeated among their descendants and more distant kin, the common lineage expands into a larger community that comprises numerous families. Externally, the different families initially confront one another primarily as strictly defined legal entities, and yet, because they are driven by the internal ethical imperative to preserve their own families, the same families simultaneously form an ethical bond with other families. Through the reciprocity and exchange of services, they collectively ensure the preservation of their families by satisfying their manifold needs, constituting a free association of families formed for the specific purpose of satisfying the self-interested ends of each individual family. Thus, we have effectively transitioned from private law to public law, elevating the human being out of their isolation in natural law into the social being of political science.
