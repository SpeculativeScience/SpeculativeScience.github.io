# Christology: The Eternal Incarnation of God

## The Incarnation of God

With the personality of God existing only in the realm of Ideas, He is not yet truly posited as infinite Spirit. To be this, He must necessarily envision Himself in finite form—in order to emerge from this finitude into conscious unity with Himself:

>Lonely was the great Master of Worlds,

>Felt a lack; therefore He created spirits—

>Blessed mirrors of His blessedness.

To be merely the Infinite—that would constitute precisely the lack that the Infinite would have felt; for in that case, it would not also be the Totality. Only when God is both Himself and His Other is this lack resolved, and He becomes conscious of His own infinitude:

>Though the Supreme Being found no Equal,

>From the chalice of the entire realm of being

>Infinitude foams forth for Him.

If one now conceives of this self-multiplication and personification of God—which is held merely in the realm of ideas—in a temporal manner once again (such that it is imagined to have chronologically preceded its temporal realization—even though the very essence of the eternal Idea is, rather, to bear no relation to any specific moment in time, but to exist at all times), then this corresponds to the dogmatic conception of the generation of the Son *prior* to the creation of the sensible world—that is, within a spiritual realm populated by angels of light, among whom, however, one—Lucifer (according to some, even the Firstborn, whose place Christ subsequently assumed)—fell away, dragging other spirits down with him, and thereby introducing the principle of the Negative and the Finite into manifestation within the infinite realm of God Himself.

Since this first realization of God—having proceeded within the realm of the Idea—can by no means be understood in a temporal sense, it bears, philosophically speaking, no other significance than this: that the divine thought—precisely because it primordially bears within itself the principle of self-realizing, and thus self-negating, activity—passes over, in and of itself, into determinate differentiation within the realm of Ideas. For that "Other" to which the divine activity relates itself in a negating manner is not an "Other" encountered from without, but rather constitutes the very self-determination of the divine essence. As a result, this self-realizing activity can appear only as the self-determination of divine universality that giving rise to this specific sphere of particularity. This realm of particularity—conceived as the *Logos* or the Word (i.e. the Son of God)—is essentially the active thought of God Himself that dispatches the generative forces of nature as its messengers (*angeloi*) to bring the universe into creation. Now, as much as this eternal Idea has existed at all times, it has likewise been active at all times. Thus, the divine thought has perpetually realized itself as the universe, and Creation itself is an eternal process—one that neither ever ceases nor ever began.

For this very reason, Nature—even in its lowliest forms—bears within itself the illusory semblance of eternity. In its eternal stars, it endures just as the divine Idea itself endures—though, at the same time, it bears the semblance of that Idea's ceaseless activity in no other manner than through mere change of place. Hence, among all peoples, the worship of the stars has historically constituted the primordial form of religious cult, and in this context, the Sun has been regarded as the first personification of God within the realm of temporal manifestation. But the enduring forms of nature represent only a very imperfect mode of manifestation of the divine Idea—precisely because it is merely the first, and their imperfection lies in the fact that this revelation—like those natures themselves—is an unconscious one.

However, the absolute self-identity of the divine Idea cannot be realized merely in the unconscious state—existing *in itself*—of the celestial spheres, wherein any conscious process of becoming-other is still precluded. Rather, in order for the infinite Spirit to attain the absolute unity of its essence through the actual, substantive idealization of all distinctions, it must first advance to a true distinction of itself from the *Other*—that medium in which it is destined to realize itself. This falling-away of the Spirit from itself is precisely *consciousness* itself, understood as the emergent separation of a conscious subject from an unconscious object—that is to say, a self-consciousness that remains formally identical to itself, distinguishing itself from the flux of its own states. Yet the consciousness of this separation constitutes merely a transitional stage—albeit one indispensable to the realization of the divine Idea; for since this realization must be brought forth through the Spirit’s own activity, it can only emerge from a separation that has subsequently been transcended. Furthermore, inasmuch as the succession of time belongs intrinsically to self-consciousness—conceived as the identity of consciousness amidst the flux of its states—the infinite Spirit does not, strictly speaking, *institute* time only at the moment consciousness arises (for time already exists as an infinitely enduring succession and continuity), rather the Spirit institutes—as one of its own internal distinctions—a specific mode of being characterized precisely by the consciousness of time.

This being is, in fact, man; and in this sense, Kant is entirely correct that time is a form of our intuition. For man perceives the succession of many "nows" in such a way that he simultaneously recognizes himself within the altered sequence—the "before" and "after"—of his own states. We cannot yet assert this of the animal, for it enjoys only the moment of the present and does not know that it lived in the past instant—just as, conversely, it is unable to harbor hopes for the future. Man is thus spirit only to the extent that, amidst the flux of his states, he remains the idealizing unity of many "nows". However, man is at the same time a finite spirit, for he must acknowledge the unbroken chain of "nows" as something that constantly slips away from him, seeing as he is incapable of holding fast to any single link of that chain for longer than a mere instant. Now, since the genesis of the Absolute Spirit—in which the formal unity of self-consciousness and the shifting content of temporal moments have become perfectly interpenetrated—necessarily entails the conscious awareness of the separation between these two sides, the Absolute Spirit can only overcome their mutual externality *within* this very succession. Thus, in order to exist not merely as an Idea-in-itself, but to be truly real, God descends into the consciousness of the finite spirit, and He annuls time not from a standpoint *outside* of it, but *within* it—through the eternal presence of absolute self-consciousness within man.

This constitutes the necessity behind the genesis of the human race within time—a process whose sole purpose, however, is to dissolve this illusion of the temporal, in order to realize the Eternal right in its very midst. Thus, even if God attains self-consciousness only within man, this does not mean that man is positing agent of this personality of God, for man is himself posited by the divine Idea. However, this personification of God—if it is to be a genuine one—must necessarily fall within the realm of time, for without its temporal manifestation, its very Idea would not be eternal—since only when eternity appears within time does it cease to be bounded by it, and only when the Concept has attained to a corresponding reality does the Form become eternal.

However, on the other hand, that time itself encompasses eternity within it means that no single, isolated moment of time may serve to represent the eternal Idea. Instead, the real personification of God—His Incarnation—must manifest itself as a totality in its temporal appearance. Thus, this manifestation is initially unconscious and external to the living individual—even though, from the very dawn of history, the human race has indeed borne within itself the unity of divine and human nature—yet in such a way that the Incarnation of God either resided solely in the human species in general, or else—as a multiplicity of divine persons—confronted specific human individuals, whether in actual life or in art: this constitutes the pre-Christian era. Secondly, the Divine attains consciousness of its own personality within a single human being, Christ knows Himself—as this specific individual—to be identical with God. The third stage is that the unity of human and divine nature ceases to be merely unconscious, or to exist solely within a one consciousness, and it instead becomes a matter of universal consciousness, as every individual—as a member of the community—raises himself in spirit to unity with God, and Christ rises again within him.

Now, while it is true that the unity of the finite and infinite Spirit—or of human and divine nature—must be elevated from an unconscious state of *being-in-itself* to the level of consciousness (since it is destined to become a goal achieved through the free development of the Spirit), nevertheless—viewed from the perspective of the divine Spirit—no actual change takes place in this process. For the infinite Spirit has, in and for itself, already rendered itself finite by realizing itself within creation; And indeed, with the temporal emergence of the human race, the entire fullness of the Divine was immediately present within it, and it was made in the image thereof. The entire change lies solely within human consciousness.

This state of the immediate unity of God with man is presented in our religious teachings as the paradisiacal state. The tradition of such a golden age lies at the foundation of all peoples, and it is nothing other than the idea of ​​the unity of the divine and human natures within finite consciousness itself—an idea which, here no less than previously in the eternal realm of spirit, must not be erroneously construed as a state chronologically preceding the present. To this idea, one may ascribe only a priority of dignity. The human race—although bearing within itself, from the very beginning, its inherent destiny—does not, however, commence in its most perfected form (the form commensurate with the Idea), but rather in the lowest mode of its existence, and this is precisely because it is destined to rise to the heights of this Idea—and thereby fulfill its destiny—solely through the exercise of its own freedom.

The primordial state of the human race is, however, neither a state of already consummated apostasy, nor one of perfected divinity. Man was initially a purely natural being—and therefore sensual and self-centered, just as we may still observe in every child today—so that he might subsequently ascend to spiritual freedom by casting off this sensuality. Yet this sensuality, as such, does not constitute sin—any more than the animal, which is confined to such sensuality, can be said to sin. Instead, sin consists in this: that the I—having detached itself from the universe as a spiritual consciousness—nevertheless makes sensuality the sole content of its activity. It is through this very act of self-activity that man first truly becomes man. Thus, we need not—as do some natural philosophers and earlier mystics—view the initial, merely animalistic state of the human race—that unconscious innocence of sensuality—as the perfect understanding of nature, which was supposedly obscured only by the Fall. We may recognize only the final state of the human race—that brought about by redemption—as such; for it alone is a spiritual state, brought forth through the self-activity of man. Bader, therefore, rightly terms the Fall a "fortunate transgression", for only through the loss of Paradise can it be regained as a free act of the spirit.

Since free knowledge can by no means be that with which man begins, he does not, in his primordial state, possess the consciousness of the unity of the divine and human natures. But even if the individual still lacks this consciousness, the unity itself—as a divine act—is not bound by time. As that which is chronologically primary has not yet entered into the distinct consciousness of individuals, it constitutes precisely that unconscious *in-itself* which is present within the entire human species as such. Thus, even if the initial state of the human race in general is one of unconsciousness, we nevertheless observe within it—everywhere—more or less obscure intimations of that unity breaking forth. Since every activity of the spirit immediately requires an individual for its realization, it is indeed in individuals that this unity primarily achieves its breakthrough—yet always only in such a way that the divine essence finds its expression solely within the species in general. The godlike heroes of antiquity—in the founding of religions, the establishment of states, and so forth—certainly attribute everything to divine inspiration, yet without their individuality actually acquiring divinity, rather serving merely as blind, will-less, and indifferent instruments of that higher power.

The entire pre-Christian era thus presents to us this gradual awakening to consciousness of the divine personality within the species. In all Eastern despotisms, individuals vanish—without legitimate standing—into the divine substance. Often the dissolution of individual consciousness and of the *I* into universal Reason is even explicitly proclaimed as the highest beatitude. The Divine has, to be sure—whether in the person of an emperor, a priestly caste, or the like—assumed a personal form through incarnations. However, it is not *this* individual who is divine *as* an individual. For this individual always changes, with the death of the Emperor or the Lama, God has sought out a different vessel for His personality—just as, among the Brahmins, He is fragmented into an indeterminate multitude of personalities. These individuals, in whom the Divine manifests itself with particular intensity, are therefore—much like those heroes—mere representatives of the species, and through them—acting, as it were, as prophets—the divine will is proclaimed to the entire people, without residing *exclusively* within them, precisely because it is diffused throughout the All.

In Greece, finally, the realm of nature and spirit—as the forces that animate and sustain the world—coalesced into a pantheon of divine figures. Whereas in the East, God was merely the universal personality of spiritual substance—an abstract power that served only to shatter individualities, and whose own manifestation as a personality appeared merely as an accidental guise—in Greece, the Divine differentiates itself into a distinct multiplicity of enduring personalities. The Sun, Moon, Earth, and Sea; mountains, rivers, and trees; the State, marriage, art, and science—in short, the highest blossoms of the natural and spiritual universe—have, as ideas, transformed into so many gods; and the individual is divine insofar as he partakes in such a substantial idea. Thus, in Greece as well, the Divine is essentially nothing other than substance—save that this substance has now acquired a determinate content, and the various facets of this content have found their embodiment as personalities: whether in the emotional life of human beings, in the creative imagination of the poet, or in the sculpted marble.

As much as the divine substance in Greece thus strives toward individuality, the deeds of the Divine are likewise individual deeds, and virtue of the underlying unity of divine and human nature—all human institutions are simultaneously divine institutions. As a result, the entire unfolding of Greek life and history must be viewed as a continuous incarnation of God. The state, marriage, agriculture, and all the arts and sciences were, indeed, introduced by the specific deity presiding over them. Thus, for the Greeks, Justice is personified as Themis, Science as Minerva, etc.

At the close of Greek history, we observe the impulse to break free from this fragmentation of the divine essence. Thus, in Alexander, the yearning of the Greek world to conceive of the Divine as a single, conscious human person is revealed—manifested in his claiming to be the son of Jupiter Ammon, and in his desire, through this means, to penetrate to the actual individuality of the divine personality.

In Greek philosophy—particularly in the Alexandrian school—the thinking subject finally grasped itself as the substratum of objective divine thought, and thus as the sole creator of an intellectual world born of Reason. This already touches upon the Christian doctrine that God is a true Subject. Consequently, Clement of Alexandria affirmed the findings of Greek speculation as a genuine preparation for Christian knowledge. Justin Martyr speaks more fully on this matter: “Every race of men partakes of the Divine Reason; and those who lived in accordance with it are Christians, even if they were held to be atheists—such as Socrates, Heraclitus, and those like them. For each of them, beholding in part that which is akin to the generative Divine Reason, gave beautiful expression to it. The Divine Reason—though difficult to explain—has become ingrained in the opinions of men. Thus, the seed of Reason is an inspiration. In Christ, however, this Reason exists in and for itself.”

Thus, when the fullness of time had come—the moment when the unity of the divine and human natures was no longer to remain merely in itself, but was to emerge into manifestation as the consciousness of the individual—the universal personality fused so intimately with a single, actual, living person that God was no longer—as in the East—an abstract, universal personality, nor—as in Greece—a merely imagined, particular personality, rather, He was a single human being, born into the light of the world—one who, as Gabler puts it, could claim of himself that he was God. Reason, or the Word, became flesh, and this God-made-flesh is Christ. He is the center of world history, and just as the entirety of paganism had striven to elevate the Divine only as far as this apex of the actual, living person, so now—proceeding from Christ as the very summit of this pyramid—a second, inverted pyramid begins to rise, one that does not take substance as its base, but establishes the intrinsically infinite subjectivity of the God-man as its foundation, only to broaden out once again from that point toward substantiality—though now as a substantiality that is fully realized in consciousness.

Thus, through the very course of our investigations, we have naturally arrived at one of the most widely debated questions of our time—a question within the sphere of modern philosophy itself (ranging from Göschel to Strauss) regarding which such a diversity—indeed, a total opposition—of views has emerged that these positions have been likened to the "Left" and "Right" wings of the political spectrum. While Rosenkranz has been positioned as a center point between these extremes, the question still remains: is this middle ground truly the correct one—the one possessed of genuine substance? Leaving aside, then, the individuals involved—a course I feel all the more compelled to take given that I, too, have personally become entangled in this dispute, and a personal interpretation has been falsely ascribed to my words—we must now provide the true reconciliation of those divergent tendencies within a speculative formulation of Christology, which appears to us to be the only possible one. And should I find it impossible to avoid mentioning specific names, I wish to state at the outset that I intend them to be understood solely as representatives of particular schools of thought.

The entire difficulty in the doctrine of Christ lies in an objection that might be raised against the divine nature of Christ—an objection whose rigorous examination alone can dispel the obscurity surrounding the matter. For if God’s essence is the thought that remains eternally identical to itself—the realm of the creative ideas of the natural and spiritual universe, which are always active in the very same manner—then this does not preclude changes occurring within created beings. For example, the gravitational forces that constantly drives the planets around the sun remains invariably the same, even though at every moment the Earth occupies a different position relative to the sun. The guiding idea of world history remains absolutely true to itself, no matter what diverse national individualities may appear upon its stage. But then what is the meaning of the claim that, 1,840 years ago, God—acting as the Holy Spirit—begot a Son with the Virgin Mary, and that this Son, as God, walked the earth for thirty-three years until he returned once more to Heaven? Here, evidently, a change would have taken place in the relationship of the three divine Persons to one another and to the world, and thus God—despite being the immutable principle of all things—would himself have been swept up into the process of change. Although in religious conceptions God the Father is not restricted to this brief span of time—but the eternal generation of the Son is affirmed—it cannot be denied that this Son—through his conception, for instance, and his birth from the womb of a Virgin, through his brief sojourn on earth—must have undergone substantial changes, and that the entire divine nature—which is present in the Son in its absolute fullness—could not have remained untouched by these changes.

Here, however, Church history teaches us—as an orthodox interpretation—that it was only the human element within the Son that suffered; and that, for this very reason, a Christian sect was condemned as heretical: because it held that God the Father had also endured the state of humiliation—which is why they were known as Patripassians. Now, the fact that God the Father—who lived and realized Himself in Christ—did not suffer can, for us, bear only this significance: that the eternal Idea of ​​the Universe, notwithstanding its changing manifestations, remains in its substance always and immutably the same. Then, to the extent that as Christ must be presented in religion as both true man and true God, it was not His inner substance—the Divine Word within Him, which was with God from the beginning and was God Himself—that underwent change, rather, only the mode of His appearance assumed a different form—that is to say, only the human element within Christ underwent change, and this constitutes the key to resolving the difficulty we raised earlier.

Since the change that occurred in time did not affect the eternal Idea of ​​God itself, this Idea remains ever-present in His creatures—and most notably in the human race—in a personal manner, and thus, the unity of the divine and human natures remains, in and of itself, unchanged. Every work of God is God Himself in an individual guise, everywhere, therefore—as Scotus Eriugena observes—we encounter a theophany. Yet, within the human race—conceived as the free unfolding of the Spirit—the consciousness of this unity between the divine and human natures must needs awaken. This consciousness—of that which exists eternally within the Idea and which, in its essence, constitutes the very substance of the human spirit—must, as a conscious awareness, enter into the realm of time. For in order to be a free production of the spirit, it must presuppose a state of unconsciousness, out of the very negation of which it emerges. For human consciousness, therefore, the distinction arises that God is initially unconsciously one with man, and that this unity subsequently attains to consciousness. Yet, for consciousness to arrive at an awareness of this unity, that unity must also have been sublated; for consciousness—here, man—distinguishes itself, as such, from its object—God—with whom it is meant to be one. Thus, for the consciousness of reconciliation with God to emerge, the consciousness of the Fall must have preceded it. For this reason, the Roman world—as this world of misery—served as the fertile ground upon which the Jewish doctrine of the Fall could bear fruit, and that fruit was the doctrine of reconciliation. When the utmost fragmentation became palpable within the human race—when the heart of the world was broken—it was then that the belief also became universal that the unity of the divine and human natures had been realized.

Just as the falling away of the finite from the infinite is an eternal idea—one that did not occur merely once, at a single given point in time—so, too, is it with reconciliation. Just as God eternally makes Himself finite, so does He eternally reconcile Himself with the finite; and this process constitutes the eternal life of God—meaning that the Incarnation, too, is eternal, even though it appears within time. Yet, even if God’s falling away from Himself and His return to Himself are eternal acts, the consciousness of both must nonetheless fall within the realm of time. Thus, just as a time had to arrive when a natural form of the earth—perceiving itself as an ego—consciously detached itself from God, thereby for the first time positing itself as a finite spirit, so, too, did the human race have to arrive—at a specific point in time—at the consciousness of this return of the finite spirit to God. As a result, nothing was altered by the appearance of Christ other than the consciousness of the finite spirit.

Once the human race had transitioned from an unconscious unity with God to a consciousness of its estrangement from God, the need for a faith in the absolute unity of the divine and human natures was bound to gradually awaken. Indeed, the deification of the Caesars—much like that of Alexander—had its source in this very need. The human being, then—in whom this faith emerged into consciousness not as a mere incidental accompaniment (perhaps as a political objective—as with Alexander—to fuse the East with the West), nor as an afterthought (as with the Roman Emperors, once they had died), but that which had claimed the entire soul of this man—his entire conduct and activity, his every thought and aspiration—that is Christ. In him, the consciousness of the universal personality of God grasped itself—as this specific individual person—for the very first time. It is through this consciousness that Christ distinguishes himself from all other human beings. In him the unity of human and divine nature was first revealed to consciousness, and therein lies the divinity of Christ. For all things are divine, and the substance of God is inherent in all beings. In the strictest sense, however, only that person may be termed divine who has attained to the consciousness of this very nature of theirs—a nature which exists in and for itself, for a person is what they are only through their consciousness as Spirit.

Yet, even if we assert that this altered consciousness belongs to the human side, this does not, after all, exclude the divinity of this consciousness. For since God, as a process, is not merely one side but the totality of the moments in motion, this altered consciousness itself—as a specific moment—belongs to the totality of the divine essence as it unfolds itself into its distinctions. If the elevation from *being-in-itself* to *consciousness* constitutes the course of God’s glorification, then God’s becoming-conscious within the human race—viewed as a single living individual—is a moment in the life of God; yet it is not the whole, nor the *Idea* of the divine life, but merely one side of its manifestation—a side whose passing leaves the *Idea* (as the totality of the movement) itself unchanged. This specific side of God—singled out as a distinct person and isolated within the realm of religious representation—is precisely what, in Christian dogma, we designate as Christ: the Son of God, or the God-Man. He is, however, merely a point of transition: the finite element that is superseded by divine consciousness. Were we to fixate the finite—qua finite—as an independent person, one that did not ultimately return to the bosom of the Godhead as a merely transient moment of the divine life, then such a being would constitute Evil. It would be the *Idea* of the absolutely Negative or the Void—in short, that which religious representation personifies as the Devil.

These premises were necessary to shed full light upon the person of Christ. For if we designate that unconscious *in-itself* of the unity of the divine and human natures as the *Concept*, and the consciousness of this unity as the *Reality*, then we may indeed affirm that in Christ, the Reality was adequate to its Concept—precisely because the consciousness of this divine substance (which is present in every human being) completely permeated Christ’s humanity. The content of his spirit was none other than the thought of this unity of the divine and human natures; and his entire life and death had no other aim than to impart this consciousness—which had emerged within him in such a beatific manner—to all of humanity, thereby reconciling humanity with the Divine.

Thus, regarding the conception of Christ through the Holy Spirit, philosophy—as Rosenkranz aptly observes—conceives of nothing other than this: that the sole principle of Christ’s life was the Divine Spirit, which determined his will, and, through his will, his very nature. "However, were he substantially different from other human beings, then the unity of the divine and human natures would, least of all, have been realized in him. Nor, likewise, could he have merely possessed the divine nature alongside the human one; for two substances within a single entity are utterly contradictory: and it is precisely the nature of the divine substance to constitute the innermost essence of man. Fundamentally, God alone is the substantial element in all things, and apart from—and alongside—God, no substantiality whatsoever exists. The distinction among human beings, therefore, lies solely in the extent to which they have brought this substantiality of God within themselves to consciousness and to active expression. In Christ, this merely quantitative difference among individuals became—in this specific regard—a qualitative one, as much as he, first and foremost, possessed this consciousness. Thus according to Strauss, one cannot—within the sphere of religion—advance beyond him because he attained its ultimate goal: that a human being should, in his immediate consciousness, know himself to be one with God.

Since the conscious realization of the divine substance within the human race necessarily had to begin with the consciousness of the divinity of that specific individual who first recognized this unity—and since this very consciousness was demanded by the prevailing belief of that era—Christ, precisely because he was the first to possess this consciousness, is that specific individual who must be presented as having been born into the light of the world as God. Christ’s consciousness is emphatically not merely that the divine substance realizes itself within the human race in a general sense—and thus, as Strauss would have it, that the realization of the divine idea within the universal concept of humanity constitutes the truly real entity. That the universal genus of man, along with its particular ideas, constitutes the Divine—this was something even the Greeks already knew. The distinctive feature of Christian consciousness lies precisely in the embodiment of the Divine within a specific, concrete individuality—such that Christ, within this very sensory individuality of his, knew himself to be God. 

We may, therefore, readily concede to Rosenkranz that, apart from Christ, no other human being has ever appeared in whom "the reality of the Idea was consummated as an individual manifestation." For this consciousness—that is to say, precisely the reality of the divine Idea—can enter into a human being for the first time in one individual alone; and all others can partake of this perfected realization only insofar as they follow in his footsteps. Yet this exclusivity, of course, pertains solely to the perfected reality of the religious Idea of ​​Christianity. Rosenkranz would surely not wish to view the reality of the political, aesthetic, or philosophical Idea—and so forth—as having been consummated in Christ as an individual manifestation. And were one to elevate the realized holiness in Christ—as an *absolute* absoluteness—above religious, aesthetic, or military perfection (which are merely *relative* absolutenesses), then such moral perfection is surely, in fact, *more* relative than religious perfection; and—given that the former can indeed be ascribed to Socrates—it is for this very reason that Christ stands higher than Socrates. This is quite apart from the fact that reducing Christ merely to the status of a perfectly moral human being would constitute a rationalism of the worst kind—whereas we, for our part, have certainly no intention of rejecting true rationalism.

To the extent that Rosenkranz, in the preface to his latest work, *Critical Elucidations of the Hegelian System* (p. XX), reiterates and comments upon that earlier statement of his—asserting that "the essence of the Idea entails the manifestation of its absolute nature as *this* specific human being"—it must be countered that, even when Rosenkranz speaks of the Idea of ​​"thisness," there remains no valid objection to his conception. "Thisness" is a necessary constituent, indispensable for the divine Idea to attain its full realization in its absolute nature. It is solely within the individual that God exists as a person. However, if the term "individual" is to be understood not merely as the individual *in general*, but specifically as *this particular individual*—in this instance, Christ—then this specific human being (Christ) would be required to walk the earth eternally as a living individual—much like the "Wandering Jew" of tradition. Alternatively, Rosenkranz would be compelled to posit a "beyond"—a heavenly realm—wherein Christ existed (and continues to exist) as a sensuous, concrete individual, both prior to and subsequent to the period of his earthly humiliation. For, given that God’s absolutely realized reality within the individual cannot be confined to the span of Christ’s earthly life—since such reality cannot be restricted to a single dimension of time—no other logical possibility remains.

Yet, in the very *disappearance* of Christ’s sensuous, concrete individuality lies the deeper truth: that such individuality does not constitute the authentic mode of manifestation for the unity of the divine and human natures. For, in the very act of his disappearance, this specific individual is transformed into a universal one. However, since the supreme vocation of the Christian is to internalize Christ—to take him into oneself and to *become* Christ, it remains true that it is invariably *that same specific individual* in whom God attains realization; and we are bound to affirm—as the ultimate, perfected consummation of humanity’s ascent to divinity—that all individuals collectively constitute *one* universal individual.

In this way, God has appeared eternally as *this specific individual*—with the sole distinction (when viewed from the human perspective) that the finite individual in whom God appeared was, prior to Christ, an *unconscious* manifestation of God, whereas since Christ, it has been a *conscious* one. However, just as God appeared prior to Christ only as realized within humanity as a whole—and subsequently, in Christ, as realized within this *single* individual—so too does the third point emerge: that through Christ, God may come to consciousness within *every* individual as the universal essence of the entire human species. And this third standpoint flows immediately from the second. For Christ’s consciousness does not consist in the exclusivity of his own individual person as a divine entity. Rather—just as, in a biblical passage we cited earlier, he views all human beings as one with himself in terms of substance, and views himself as being of the same substance as God—so too is his consubstantiality with God posited within *every* individual; and to bring this consubstantiality to consciousness was the task of Christ and his religion—a task for the sealing of which he willingly suffered a sacrificial death.

Thus, Christ was called the Son of God not because he differed in substance from other human beings, but because the *knowledge of God*—which was present in him first—is itself the very *being of God*; this is something that we, in our own time, can all now *replicate in thought* and *possess* for ourselves—whereas those who came before him merely *possessed* it but did not *think* it, and thus recognized as divine only their own substance, but not the individual self as such.

We have hereby reached the third point in the manifestation of the divine personality—namely, the Incarnation in the Christian era, understood as a universal Incarnation within the realm of the *finite* itself, a manifestation conditioned by the emergence of divine consciousness in Christ. Were Christ to be regarded as this exclusive manifestation of God within a single, finite individual—outside of whom (according to Rosenkranz) no other human being bears this divine presence within himself—an interpretation against which Strauss rightly rebels, then Christ would represent merely the opposite extreme to the one-sidedness of the pre-Christian era, wherein God appears solely as the *universal* principle of humanity. Here, then, Strauss has ample grounds to demonstrate the inherent inadequacy of the individual *finite* entity as such: "Even in Christ, the God-manhood could not be actualized in the full plenitude of its content at every single moment; rather, in order to behold its complete reality within him, we must intellectually synthesize the various moments of his life into a unified whole." Strauss thereby answers Gabler’s objection—namely, that if God were not fully realized in a single, individual human being who had entered the light of the world, the existence of the divine Idea would be relegated solely to the realm of human thought. For even this specific individual, after all, we must view *intellectually*—that is, we must elevate him to the level of the *universal* and grasp his underlying Idea—if he is to correspond to divine Reason; such that here, too—as Strauss adds—if that aforementioned canon is to hold true, the reality of the Idea would, in the final analysis, remain merely a *thought* reality.

However, the assertion that the sensuous "This" must be elevated into thought in order to be adequate to the divine Idea does not imply—as Rosenkranz reproaches Strauss—that the latter intends to validate the subjectivity of the Substance solely within the infinite multiplicity of subjects, or within the genus of humanity. "The point to be criticized in Strauss's exposition—to put it in a single word—does indeed seem to lie in the fact that he did not emphasize with sufficient precision the necessity of the "This" as an enduring moment in the incarnation of God, even though this necessity is by no means entirely obscured in his work. It is simply that the individual is not merely—as he claims—the active apex that brings the substantial content into reality; rather, we fully concur with Gabler that the sensuous "This" of a human being born into this light constitutes an essential element of God's perfect reality. In that case, however—as I noted in my *History of the Latest Systems of Philosophy*—this mode of reality must always have been present to God. Surely, Gabler would not wish to locate it in Heaven or on some other planet. Consequently, God must even now appear here on Earth as a sensuous "This"—albeit always as a "This" that negates and transcends itself." This is what must be understood by the eternal incarnation of God.

This is now the point from which a reconciliation of the conflicting views can be undertaken—namely, by relating the eternal incarnation of God within the human race back to the temporal incarnation within this specific, sensuous individual. It is, to be sure, the distinguishing characteristic of the Christian standpoint to prioritize the incarnation in a single individual over the incarnation in the species as a whole, for therein lies precisely the progress of Christianity relative to Paganism. However, as the individual thereby becomes adequate to universal divine Reason, he is elevated above the limitations of his particular manifestation, and only as a process of the individual rising into universality can he serve as a representation of the divine Idea in the act of realizing itself. Thus, in Christ, this eternal cycle of the divine Idea is presented—solely to the believing consciousness—as a singular, historical event, without this implying, however, that it constituted a merely transient act within God Himself. Therefore, when Rosenkranz states in the passage cited above (p. XXI): “In Christ, the unity of man with God is established not merely *by* man—as is the case with us—but equally *by* God,” this opposition proves untenable. For in that case, firstly, other human beings would be ranked higher than Christ, inasmuch as they would have brought about a unity through their own efforts—a unity that Christ could attain only with the aid of God. Furthermore—given the unity of the divine and human natures—human action must, in this context, invariably reveal itself as a divine action as well. And is not the grace of God—in the sense of the word we recently discussed—precisely this divine action which, in every individual human being, is the very force that first brings this unity into being? Would it not, indeed, be preferable to designate the unity with God—of which man is unconscious—as a divine activity, whereas, conversely, in Christ—where the consciousness of this unity first emerged—it can be articulated as something posited by the human spirit?

However, in order for the appearance of Christ to be adequate to the eternal incarnation of God, it could not—precisely in its immediate, individual form—be worshipped as the Highest itself. Even if the divinity of Christ—as this specific, actual person—is the result of the faith of the world (which absolutely demanded such a figure that was a unity of the divine and human natures), this faith—since it belongs solely to the finite, shifting consciousness of man—must not be regarded as something fixed, permanent, or ultimate, rather it must be conceived as a fluid moment within the life of the divine process. This is Kant’s demand: that ecclesiastical faith be transformed into a religious faith of reason—a transformation in which he discerns the approach of the Kingdom of God. Admittedly, however, in order for this specific form to be grasped at all as a mode of the divine’s appearance, it had first to be recognized within a particular individual. This individual—as previously stated—had to be Christ, for he was the very first to possess, within his own specific individuality, the consciousness of the divine essence.

Thus, that which constitutes eternal history—the finitization of the infinite thought, and its return to itself through the negation of the finite and its transfiguration within the infinite spirit—is represented by faith (and necessarily had to be so represented) as a temporal event that transpired in the person of this specific individual. The Son of God was born as a man, was humbled, suffered, died, rose again, and now he sits at the right hand of God to judge the living and the dead. It is the task of criticism and exegesis to determine what, in these narratives, constitutes historical fact. Here we grant Strauss complete freedom to excise as much as sound criticism requires. We do not cling to any of these facts as if they were articles of faith, for in individual historical facts, the Idea can never be adequately expressed. What alone is true is the general belief in these facts—a belief that has indeed endured only by virtue of its elevation into the universality of thought. Christ himself, during his lifetime, did not—like Eastern despots—allow himself to be worshipped as God, nor was he—like a Greek statue—adored by his contemporaries. It is in the death of Christ that this inversion of finite consciousness was accomplished. "Did you not know," he therefore asks the disciples on the road to Emmaus, "that the Son of Man had to suffer such things?"

True Incarnation is neither merely the indwelling of God within the human race—existing solely *in itself* from the very dawn of world history—nor is it merely that which once came to consciousness *in Christ* as the Divinity of that specific individual, rather it is the universal movement of God as the Triune One—a movement that is recapitulated in the consciousness of individual persons as they cast off their natural individuality and are reborn in the Spirit, constituting themselves as representatives of the universal divine substance. The Lord's Supper is the supreme symbol of this universal Incarnation. In the partaking of it, God—as the universally present Essence which indwells all things *in and for themselves*—is apprehended by the believing consciousness precisely as such: as the One that is present to the congregation in the immediate moment. Thereby, God begins to possess an existence external to the believing consciousness—an existence, however, that is overcome through the believer's spiritual partaking of the Supper, for God is buried within the heart of the believer, rises again therein. As the Spirit of the congregation transforms each individual into the very temple in which He reigns enthroned, all believers constitute *one* believer, within whom the pulse of divine consciousness beats spiritually. Just as the human race formerly beheld the Incarnation in Christ as an external, sensuous form, so now is the individual believer, *in himself*, a manifestation of God—but only in that each individual has been transformed into the Universal in becoming consciousness of the universal Idea, having thus cast off his own finitude, naturalness and sin in the very same manner that Christ cast it off through natural death.

Here, one may say just as well that God became man in the individual instance as that He did so only in the general community, and the alternative—whether the realized reality of the divine Idea is to be found only in a single, actual human being or only in the general species—is therefore one-sided. For if Strauss were to view the perfect reality of the Idea as realized solely within the species of humanity, it would have to be countered that the species, in turn, is only real within the individual. Reconciling Strauss and Rosenkranz with one another, and positioning ourselves midway between them, we must say: If God has accomplished the perfected realization of His Idea within the consciousness of a single human being, then the human race as such has accomplished it, and it will then repeat itself in individuals all the more readily, given that the "first-fruits" has left them an exemplar. However, just as there were aspects even in Christ—such as all those activities and shifting states belonging solely to the sphere of human need within Him—in respect of which His actions were not divine, so too will this be the case, to a far greater degree, with every other individual. We may therefore assert with equal validity that no individual—at any time or in any circumstance of his life, or across the entire empirical breadth of his existence—is perfectly commensurate with the divine Idea. For in every individual, as Aristotle observes, absolute Reason remains bound to a corporeal substratum. After all, did Christ constitute an exception to this universal lot of mortality? The individual is always an indifferent, inadequate medium—one in which, to be sure, the divine Idea alone manifests itself, yet does not for that reason cease to be absolute simply because it shines forth into this realm of finitude. On the contrary, only thereby does she attain absoluteness in that she knows how to smelt the pure King of Gold from the dross of the earthly.

The absolute existence of the Divine within the individual is, therefore, not limited to that single person alone, rather, the divine Idea manifests itself in every human being—though either a more negative or a more positive manner. Is not even the sting of conscience within the sinner’s breast a realization of the divine Idea within him—and indeed, a perfect one? For God can only realize Himself perfectly, and as much as everything else about the sinner is but a vanishing shadow, the Divine is contained within him—specifically in the manner in which it is capable of presenting itself within this particular substrate. But in an equally positive manner—just as in Christ—there exist in many other individuals deeds and thoughts that may be regarded as an adequate manifestation of the divine Idea, and these alone are the elements that truly count in the incarnation of God within these individuals, everything else is fleeting dew—a mere incidental accompaniment, an inessential element belonging to the existence of the finite, and one that perishes along with it. Thus, in these individuals, God has become Man in an adequate manner—specifically in those aspects through which they give expression to the divine Idea.

Now, the more the individual succeeds in shattering this external shell—which still serves to obscure the inner, shared core common to all individuals—and allows this inner essence to shine through, the more will the "universal personality" (as Bader terms it) be awakened within that individual; and, consequently, the more will God break forth into that individual’s life. Bader rightly declares this realization of the divine Idea within all individuals to be the supreme goal of the human race; for then, this fully realized reality need no longer be—symbolically and through faith—tied to a single individual; rather, it will exist in and for itself, in its purest form—and thus, God will finally be recognized as the Holy Spirit. "Just as National Unity," Bader beautifully observes, "must still manifest itself in a single individual—the ruler—much as, in the time of Christ, universal Man had to appear in a single human form alongside all other individual human beings; so does this necessity persist until the universal One has penetrated into the very center of all individual forms and has assimilated them all from within." Once this has come to pass, time is indeed abolished, and the eternal Incarnation becomes a reality within every single manifestation. There then dawns that moment which Scripture promises as the time when God shall be "all in all," and when a new earth and a new heaven shall serve as the dwelling place of the human race, now made partakers of eternal blessedness. It is within this conscious unity of the individual with the universal divine Personality that man's eternal life is established—a subject to which we must now turn our attention.

## The New Christianity

> Note: This section is a direct translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's The Epiphany of the Eternal Personality of the Spirit Vol II: The Historical Christ and the New Christianity

Teleophanes. By beginning—my friends—with an expression of my heartfelt gratitude to you for having, for the most part, responded to my recent invitation, and by warmly welcoming those friends I have recently gained, I simultaneously accede to a wish that has reached my ears in the interim: namely, that I should present my views to the company in a coherent, connected manner before the discussion regarding them is opened. Foregoing, for the moment, the sweet pleasure of dialogue, I shall therefore—acting here as your host—serve up to you, for the time being, a lengthy discourse, which I now submit to your critical judgment.

I can find no better way to preface the entire purport of this discourse than by sharing with you the exhortation of Eusebios—an exhortation he conveyed to me by letter, since a higher calling has deprived us of his personal presence: namely, that we should now celebrate together an Epiphany *par excellence*. Indeed, the idea of ​​the eternal personality of the spirit has, until recently, been developed by me only in terms of its *concept*—as the thought of the personality of the Absolute in its eternal incarnation. That which my friends—in their discussions with me—referred to variously as the "image of ideal totality," the "primordial process of the Trinity," or even "all-encompassing subjectivity," I did not believe I was at liberty to conceive—as they did—as a merely *imagined* abstraction; rather, I felt compelled to portray it as the eternal process of *realization* of the absolute personality within the human race. This process does, admittedly, still occupy the standpoint of the "relation of substantiality"—insofar as, within this framework, the personality of the Absolute need not yet have emerged into the conscious awareness of the *individual* human self; that is to say, it has not yet been recognized as a *real* subject that is self-aware. Admittedly, in the prehistoric primordial state of humanity—and later in certain Eastern seers and Greek sages—the Divine Logos itself makes its appearance even prior to Christianity, as Justin Martyr observes. Thus, even in those instances, the unity of Concept and Reality—a unity that can never be absent from the Absolute at any time—has been posited from all eternity; yet the specific mode of its appearance has not yet been fully developed in and for itself.

This, however, constitutes no deficiency on the part of the Absolute Concept, which manifests itself in this particular form. On the contrary, I argued that wherever the Absolute still wholly permeates the divinely inspired priest or the innocent man of nature, the individual contributes least of his own individuality; in such cases, the Absolute dwells entirely unveiled within the purely formal aspect of this manifestation. In this, I recognized the theological concept of inspiration—much in the same way that the earliest historians remained entirely at one with their subject matter. Yet the fact that such a manifestation of the Absolute is merely formal constitutes precisely the deficiency that still clings to it. For the very reason that the Concept has merely permeated the appearance, the appearance itself does not constitute the entire Concept in and for itself, but remains merely a vanishing moment within the Absolute. The task of Christianity, conversely, is to develop the manifestation of the Spirit’s eternal personality—amidst the realm of finitude and its inherent divisions—into an absolute totality; that is, to render it fully adequate to the Concept. It is solely from this standpoint that I shall be able to construct a speculative Christology.

For, in our earlier discussions, we distinguished between a *Being* and a *Knowing* of the Absolute. The in-itselfness of the Absolute remains ever the same amidst the manifold configurations of nature, in the paradisiacal state of humanity, and in those select few of the human race—those individual "hits" amidst the countless "blanks" of the great masses—who knew how to preserve the sacred fire of the unadulterated Idea purely within their bosoms. This Being of the Absolute—as spiritual Being and Being *in* the spirit—is necessarily a Knowing; and Being and Knowing must now—just as Concept and Reality were earlier—be posited by us as absolutely identical within the Absolute. Thus, the Absolute's knowledge of itself is not merely present at a single moment, but always—and thereby eternally—present, for it cannot perish at any stage of the human race's development. The Absolute's *being-known* by individuals—insofar as they exist as such—has, however, varied throughout the course of time. For the individual, it makes a vast difference whether he merely feels, senses, or believes, or whether he engages in logical thought and true comprehension; furthermore, from the standpoint of humanity in general, it matters greatly whether such knowledge is confined to a select few individuals or whether it is diffused throughout the whole of humanity.

I now draw a very precise distinction regarding this human aspect of the knowledge of the Absolute, and I expressly contrast it with the Absolute's own knowledge of itself *within* man. The development of that human knowledge constitutes History—the history of the human race as it has unfolded thus far. This absolute knowledge, conversely, regards this historical development merely as a subordinate, transitory moment in her very self—that which, in accordance with the concept of ideality, is also destined to perish—while the knowledge of the Absolute itself remains eternally the same, beneath the veil of its forms that disintegrate in time.

The development of world history is, therefore, nothing other than this: that the actual personality of the Absolute—which, in a prehistoric state where nature was still the immediate expression of Spirit, existed in and for itself, without any self-awareness on the part of human individualities—should, through the Fall, transform into a yearning for a lost, in-itself-existing perfection—a perfection imagined as already attained within a transcendent Subject that extends beyond this imperfect world; and furthermore, that the more the independence of the individual personality asserts itself against that ideal Subject—amidst the rampant weeds of evil—the more the unifying Idea remains obscured, fading into a mere hope for the eventual restoration of that primordial state; until, at the very pinnacle of corruption—with the universalization of the consciousness of sin—the opposing consciousness of an eternally, in-and-for-itself complete unity of human and divine nature finally takes root among mankind and steps forth into their sensuous perception; so that this reconciliation—which first establishes itself within the inner realm of faith—may ultimately permeate and transform all spheres of the Spirit's reality. This, then, is what I call the truly Christian State—a state which our rulers, too—though I know not, admittedly, whether in this same sense—hold out to us as a future prospect.

What, then, in light of this, is the person of Christ? It is evident that his appearance some 1,846 years ago could not alter the nature of the Absolute. The Absolute has become Man—and indeed eternally so; yet, by that very token, it is so at every moment. The Absolute's only-begotten Son in Heaven—i.e., In the eternal concept—wherein the human race is grasped as a single, universal Person—it is the Logos of the Neoplatonists and of certain Gnostics, who did not appear in the flesh. Nor do we owe to Christianity the conviction that, within individual persons, the universal spirit of the human species bears witness to the universal spirit of the universe. However, the insight that the Absolute is not merely Substance, but equally Subject—and that the Absolute attains its completion only within this Subject—constitutes the infinitely profound gaze that Christ cast upon the nature of things. Yet he cast this gaze not in the form of thinking universality—as did the founder of our school—but rather in the distinctive mode of inspired intuition; that is to say, in the form of individuality.

The Subject that was thus elevated to the level of the Absolute was, therefore, not a universal one—not an absolute Person recurring in all individual persons—but was itself an empirically given Subject; that is, a strictly individual one. For the epiphany of the eternal personality of the Spirit—in the true sense of the term—necessitates its givenness within the realm of experience. Could Christ, then, have chosen any other Subject for this purpose than himself? Impossible. For the matter at hand did not concern an alteration in the mode of being of the Absolute itself. The divine Substance constitutes the innermost essence of a stone or an animal neither more nor less than it constitutes the innermost essence of Christ or of any other human being. In terms of Substance, therefore, all existing entities—and specifically all human beings—are equal to one another. However, human consciousness—man’s awareness of the presence of this Substance within all things—had undergone a transformation. Since Christ was the first among men to articulate the individual Subject as the supreme flowering of the absolute Essence—whereas in the case of the Brahmin, for instance, it was merely the species that held this status—he could, by necessity, refer the truth of this proposition only to himself. He alone possessed the self-knowledge that his own Subjectivity had been wholly dissolved into the immutable Will of the Father; Only he could demand of himself the sacrifice of his entire individuality in order to let the Absolute live within him—a demand he indeed affirmed through his deeds.

That all things are, in terms of their substance, one with the universal essence of God—this is what theological language terms *creation*. However, the fact that Christ knew himself to be one with the Father—this I designate as the *begetting of the Son*; whereby, conversely, he also begot the Father. Thus, Christ did not distinguish himself from other human beings by virtue of his *being*, but solely by virtue of his *knowledge*; yet, admittedly, this very knowledge of the Absolute constitutes the *being* of the Absolute within man. This knowledge alone constitutes the divinity of man. For the Absolute resides just as truly within the sinner; yet the sinner does not know it positively—as his own being—but only negatively, as the sting of conscience. Thus, by virtue of this *self-knowledge-as-God*, Christ is the firstfruits of sonship. He is—to borrow the formula of my noble friend from the East, which he will surely permit me to publicly claim as his own—not a God who became man, but a man who became God; and this, specifically, through his *knowing himself* within God. For sonship is—as Scripture affirms—an adoption, a *taking-into-sonship* (υἱοθεσία) in God, and thus reconciliation with Him.

At the same time, the aforementioned formula is one-sided, in that it applies only to the historical Christ, not to the idea contained within Him; for it expresses only the conscious action of Christians, to whom the founder of their religion gave this example. This activity of the individual, of realizing Himself as God, yet bear in mind that the unity of the divine and human natures constitutes the innermost essence of our spirit—for only that which is necessary within the Concept can be realized. This possibility—conceived here not merely as an abstract thought but equally as an empirical fact—is the manifestation of Christ on earth; he is, therefore, preeminently the epiphany of the eternal personality of the Spirit, because in his immediate existence, that which is destined to be the result of the deeds of every individual is presented as already originally present. From the necessity of this presupposition—which precedes all results—flows the doctrine of the eternal generation of the Son. In this respect, Christ is presented as the Son of God; conversely, in respect of his own deeds, he is indeed the Son of Man—one who has raised himself up to divinity. The further development and consummation of Christianity, however, lies in the fact that all empirical subjects undertake this same act—and that, through this process, the unity of the eternal personality of the Spirit comes to clear consciousness not merely in isolated visionaries, but in every single individual. For, as each person—like Christ—immerses his own subjectivity in the Absolute, we are—like him and through him—all one in the Father.

Thus, in place of the derivative process of creation posited by the "right-wing" school, I view—as the second act in my trilogy of the divine drama—the path of the regeneration of the human race on earth, leading toward the likeness of eternal Reason: that is to say, world history conceived as a spiritual creation. For natural creation—being eternal in its nature—I assign to the primordial, pre-historical epoch. However, unlike the right-wing school, I do not restrict the Trinity to this primordial process; rather, I perceive it as recurring throughout all three spheres—the pre-historical, the historical, and the post-historical—yet in such a manner that, within each of these spheres, a distinct one of its moments is given particular prominence and elaboration. For while in the primordial world—which we may call the Realm of the Father—Thought, as the creative principle of all things in its abstract universality, constitutes the Father *as such*, in this first sphere it is the Son—as *Logos* or Particularity—who represents the system of creative forces: the genera or Ideas of things, which, from all eternity, have brought forth individual instances from within themselves. It is, as John states, the Word through which all things were made, even before it became flesh. However, since in the human species universal Thought has attained adequate existence—inasmuch as Particularity itself has become universal—the Spirit here emerges, through inspiration, within the realm of Individuality. How, then—in our second trilogy (the Reign of the Son, which we are now to examine, and whose focal point is the concept of an empirically given God-Man)—the moments of the Trinity will be modified in accordance with this standpoint, remains to be shown later; and likewise, the third trilogy—the Doctrine of the Spirit—will naturally structure itself in accordance with this very principle.

How, then, do the positive philosophers of the right-wing school relate to this Christology of mine—which I have just now sketched out only in its most general outlines? How am I to come to terms with the left-wing school, which adopts a stance toward the phenomenon of Christ that is solely negative and critical? And finally, how shall I—as a man of the Center, seeking mediation between the extremes—vindicate my reputation as a practical philosopher: a title bestowed upon me by Lykos, even though official duties have now separated him from our circle?

We are, one and all, in complete agreement regarding the necessity of distinguishing two distinct moments within Christology: the eternal Idea of ​​the Incarnation, and its empirical manifestation. It is solely regarding the relative value of these two moments—and their relationship to one another—that a divergence of views exists among the various factions within the school. It is upon this, then, that we must reach an understanding: firstly, by my presenting the Master’s own view; secondly, by my laying before you the various tendencies within the school—some extreme, others mediating—and finally, thirdly, by my offering a concluding statement of my own creed—insofar as it may not yet have fully emerged from the partly negative, partly positive contemplation of all these views.

Each of the two factions within the school grounds itself upon one of the essential elements of that conceptual framework which, in the Master himself, remained naively intertwined—without any prior awareness of the antitheses that would subsequently emerge from it within the school. We must, therefore, first examine the Master’s view; for the various tendencies of the school can be explained only as the disintegration of those very elements—the dialectic of which, however, will simultaneously bring about the resolution of this disintegration, thereby revealing the Master’s totality restored, yet now imbued with the consciousness of the antitheses overcome. And this, precisely, is what I term the practical center of the school.

Proceeding from the idea of ​​the eternal Incarnation, the Master first states: the simple content of this reconciliation of Spirit with itself is the positing-in-unity of absolute essentiality and individual human subjectivity. Implicit in this is that the human spirit—in terms of its concept and essence—is true Spirit, and that every individual subject, by virtue of being human, possesses the infinite vocation to be in unity with God.

Thereupon, the Master proceeds to demonstrate the necessity of the reality of this concept: "Consequently, the demand is placed upon man to give reality to this concept of his—which is initially a mere *in-itself*—for in his inner nature, he is free, infinite Spirit. He is capable of achieving this only insofar as that unity is itself the primordial ground; the goal is simultaneously the beginning *in-and-for-itself*—the presupposition."

In what form, however, the Master further asks, must this reconciliation—existing *in-and-for-itself*—come to the consciousness of the individual? "The finite spirit," he answers, "is to possess the consciousness of God within the finite itself. This consciousness of the Absolute Idea is to be brought forth here, for human beings, in the form of *certainty*. In order for this unity of the divine and human natures to attain the status of certainty, it must assume the form of immediate sensuous intuition; such that this content—in the form of external existence—appears as something seen and experienced within the world. Since, at this standpoint, the concern is with *sensuous certainty*, the unity of the divine and human natures must present itself as *this specific, actually existing human being*. Furthermore, since this substantial unity constitutes the *in-itself* of man—a realm lying beyond our immediate consciousness—it follows that, for others, it must appear as an individual human being from whom they remain excluded." "But on the other hand, the determination of immediate existence is at the same time an essential one—the ultimate focal point of Spirit in its subjectivity. God, as Spirit, contains within Himself the moment of subjectivity, of singularity; His manifestation, therefore, can occur but once."

Here, my friends, you have the two moments at issue in the Master’s speculative Christology—strictly separated, and conceived in apparent contradiction. Scarcely has the Master affirmed the singularity of this manifestation—"A single man is God, and God is a single man"—than he asserts the converse: "It is said that Christ died for all—and this is not a singular event, but rather the divine, eternal history." How do we resolve the contradiction between these two passages? In the simplest possible way.

"Regarding this," the Master continues—namely, that it is an essential determination of the very nature of God—"sensory corroboration falls away; against sensory facts, therefore, objections can always be raised." Consequently, it never remotely occurs to the Master to attempt to prove the historical details recounted regarding the life of Christ as events that objectively transpired. "The certainty of religious faith is not grounded upon a few isolated historical testimonies—which, as such, would admittedly fail to provide that degree of certainty regarding their content which, for instance, newspaper reports afford us concerning any ordinary event. It does not hinge upon the sensory aspect of the manifestation—upon this historical element—as if the validation of Spirit and its truth were to be found therein. For what Spirit enacts is not history, but something absolutely present." Thus, the only aspect of this—in truth—universal event that occurs but once is the act of its empirical realization within the finite spirit; and regarding the sheer facticity of this *coming-to-consciousness* in that specific era, no one disputes. For the entire history of Christianity bears witness to this.

The Master, therefore, never constructs a *real* incarnation in space and time, but always only human consciousness *of* it; for the manifestation of the Idea in finitude is, in fact, nothing other than how it becomes for human consciousness. “The main point,” the Master adds, “lies here in the inner certainty—in the feeling and conception—of this eternal truth: in the faith that bears witness to the truth in and for itself, and is thereby situated within the very interior of the conception.” Faith alone grasps—and possesses the consciousness—that in Christ, this truth-in-and-for-itself is beheld in its unfolding; without the faith of others, this development does not exist—it is solely for faith that this manifestation appears. This need—to know God as Spirit in the form of an actual human being—has been generated by the progression of history, by the formation of the World Spirit. This fact—that the Absolute Spirit assumes the form of self-consciousness—manifests itself such that it constitutes the faith of the world: that Spirit exists as a self-consciousness—that is to say, as a real human being. I would add to the Master's words that even the figure of Alexander the Great, in allowing himself to be praised as the son of Jupiter Ummon, strove for this, whereas the Roman Caesars are caricatured copies of this Christian consciousness.

But because the individualization and isolation of the absolute spirit, although an essential element, is also something always withdrawn into and contained within the Idea, the Master demonstrates, using the phenomenon itself, how it resolves itself through its own dialectic, and how, beneath its veil, the Idea alone emerges as the valid and true. "This knowledge, that self-consciousness is the absolute essence, or the absolute essence is self-consciousness, is now the world spirit. It is this knowledge, but does not know this knowledge; it only contemplates it, or it only knows it directly, as an individual human being. The spirit is not yet, as it is the individual self, equally as universal, Self fully present. With the death of Christ, however, the reversal of consciousness begins. Throughout this entire history, it has dawned upon human consciousness that man is immediate, present God—in a universal form, stripped of all finitude. In the vanishing of the immediate existence of that which was known as the Absolute Essence, Spirit remains the immediate Self of reality—yet as the universal self-consciousness of the community. That element belonging to the sphere of *representation*—namely, that the Absolute Spirit, as an individual, manifests the nature of Spirit within its own existence—is thus here transposed into self-consciousness itself: into its *knowing*, which constitutes the Essence reconciling itself with itself, the element of representation that initially precedes it is thus posited here as sublated."

This primacy, which the Master grants to the Idea, evidently prompted the Left Wing to place its primary value not upon the Appearance, but precisely upon the Idea. However—since his philosophy does not, as an anonymous writer recently attempted to demonstrate in a short brochure, amount to the Kantian proposition that Appearance is merely an operation of our consciousness that does not correspond to reality—the Right Wing, too, was able to extract from his writings the claim—positing itself as the true interpreter of the Master—that the epiphany of the eternal personality of the Absolute, having emerged in time, corresponds perfectly to its Concept. Thus far, no objection can be raised against either of these two directions. But now the distortion begins. The Left Wing dissolves Appearance and Representation into the sole validity of the Idea; the Right Wing drags the Idea down into the realm of Representation, and elevates the moment of Appearance—which, as such, is never adequate—in its singular historical manifestation, to the status of the Absolute Idea itself. While it is true that both moments are contained within both directions, they are combined only in a state of developed contradiction—whereas the Master still knew how to resolve this opposition.

Yet where I am heading, my friends, you may perhaps have already guessed. For if the Right Wing can reproach the Left by saying, "Your Idea lacks existence," while the Left Wing reproaches the Right by saying, "In your view, existence is not adequate to the Idea," then I might offer a mediating view: that Christ—precisely amidst that inadequate existence which, insofar as he *did* exist, must necessarily have been present in him as well—was the very first to raise into consciousness, within his own person, that aspect of existence which *is* adequate to the Idea. We shall now, secondly, examine this School in order to trace each of its factions back to its true justification; in the course of which—and by means of which—our own view, even in its more specific elaboration, will immediately reveal itself as one that has emerged spontaneously from the unfolding of history. In this examination, we shall have to interpose, between the Left and the Right, the mediating attempts of a Center.

The Right proceeds from the unwavering presupposition that Christ is God—or, alternatively, the Son of God; that, 1,846 years ago, he stepped forth from his divine station as the Second Person of the Trinity to assume the form of a servant. To the eternal generation of the Son in the primordial process, there was added—in the derivative process, spanning thirty-three years—an incarnation of the Logos; upon the cessation of this incarnation, this Second Person vanished once again from the earth to resume his former station—or whatever new, third station it might be—in Heaven, only to descend to us once more, in a transformed guise, at some future time, for the fulfillment of all the purposes of the Absolute.

Here, we must now exclaim—together with the most versatile of our fellow thinkers, who oscillates between these two currents: “Such facts contradict everything that philosophy knows; they do not fit within its concepts.” This is accepted by another figure from the Right—Androgeos, from the City of Salt—without, however, allowing his faith to be shaken thereby. “The form in which truth must appear is that of facticity; and since facts contradict one another, truth must appear in the form of contradictory facts.” Quite so! However, one need not content oneself—as one of his contemporaries does—with the mere platitude that the speculative perspective constitutes the solution to this contradiction, which supposedly remains irresolvable only to the natural understanding. Still less, however, may one rest content with the unresolved contradiction as a final state, consoling oneself with the thought that the contradiction is merely *meant* to be resolved.

Now, the fact that the right side is honestly striving to find the solution I will not deny that it seeks to bring about this contradiction in a speculative manner. I merely deny that it has succeeded. But wherein, precisely, does this contradiction lie? It lies in the fact that what is supposed to be eternal, divine history is situated within a specific epoch of time—depicted as an event that occurred but once. "Well," replies the dweller of *Ultima Thule*, "even if the Being *in itself* lies outside of time, its *appearance* is nonetheless revealed *within* time." Of this assertion, only the second half—not the first—is correct. For if the Eternal, being timeless, were to exist *outside* of time—while only its manifestations fell *within* it—then Time and Eternity would, in fact, be mutually delimited. Eternity would then lie *before* and *after* Time. The rejoinder that it also falls *within* Time makes no difference; for the composite of "Time + Eternity" still constitutes a qualitative difference when contrasted with a "mere Eternity" existing solely before and after Time. Thus, precisely by attempting to preserve Eternity from contamination by Time, one actually assigns it a *temporal* relationship to Time—thereby binding it to Time and rendering it coordinate with it. The inability to resolve this contradiction constitutes the *prōton pseudos*—the fundamental error—of the Right Wing.

According to this doctrine, the eternal Incarnation and Generation of the Son—dating from the very beginning—reigned supreme, alone and exclusively, prior to the inception of all Time and continued to do so until 1846 years ago. Subsequently, for a period of thirty-three years, this process is said to have entered into *appearance*; the unity of Concept and Reality thus endured for only this brief span of time, only to vanish once more into the realm of the "mere Concept." At least here on Earth, this harmony is said to exist no longer; and as for the state of affairs in Heaven, the Right Wing is unable to say that remains, for the time being, a mystery. The only formula it can confidently posit—drawing from the tenets of its school—is this: that the "Third Moment"—the Son's return to the Father, his sitting at the Father's right hand—constitutes the *true spiritual reality*, established through the negation of external reality. Granted! According to this view, then, the Third Moment *does* indeed possess reality; and even the First Moment is not devoid of it, seeing as it enjoyed an *eternal appearance* in Heaven prior to its *temporal appearance* here on Earth. Thus, the Right Wing appears—albeit merely for show—to remain faithful to the school’s canon: that the Absolute constitutes the harmony of Concept and Reality. Yet, of the three manifestations, only the second is truly real; the first and the third belong solely to the realm of *Vorstellung* (representation)—and this is not merely because they pertain to the past and the future, but because they are, by their very nature, never intended to enter the domain of the sensuous world—that is to say, the domain of *appearance* proper.

We must now resolve this contradiction—the notion of an "appearance" that is, in fact, no appearance at all. To this end, however, we must first provide a more detailed account of this threefold manifestation as it is articulated by our colleagues. Originally, so the narrative goes, the Universal of the human race—since, as a mere collective unity, it possessed no true reality of its own—found that reality exclusively in the *Ur-individual* (Primal Individual): Christ. It retained this exclusive reality until the moment of temporal creation, when Christ ceded his position to the totality of individual human beings—without, however, relinquishing his own place in Heaven. Thus, the Right Wing posits two distinct manifestations of the eternal Incarnation. The first appears within a purely spiritual Heaven—prior to the creation of the sensuous world—as the Second Person of the Trinity; here, the Father and the Son are immersed in eternal contemplation, existing as pure Spirit—either in solitary communion or, perhaps, accompanied by a celestial choir of angels. The second manifestation—occurring here on Earth—takes the form, initially, of the human species as a whole. Yet, in order to reunite this dual reality—comprising both the Primal Individual and the aggregate of created individuals—back into a single, unified whole, it became necessary for the Primal Individual to emerge *from* this very species as a concrete, actual human being. The purpose of this emergence was to ensure that, through the sacrifice of his own individual human form, he might—at the world's ultimate dissolution—finally reclaim all other individuals into his own Primal Individuality, without thereby compromising their distinct autonomy. This, then, constitutes the third manifestation of the eternal Incarnation—an event destined to take place, once again, within the celestial realm.

We may well accept this narrative; yet, if it is indeed history, it must also bear within itself all the characteristics of history—that is, it must have truly entered into sensuous manifestation, such that a temporal progression separates these states from one another. We therefore assert: the human race cannot, of course, have originated from a single primordial individual—a true *universale ante rem* in the Scholastic sense. For we know from logic that the concept of the One—as the absolute repulsion of itself—both posits and sublates multiplicity. Thus, wherever there is one individual, there are necessarily also many; and the notion that one specific individual constitutes the primordial individual in contradistinction to the others cannot be logically constructed either, since each and every one—just like the first—is, in an equal manner, both repelling and attracting. But even though the multiplicity of individuals has always been necessary for the existence of the species, we can still assert, regarding the primeval era or the first world period I recently alluded to, that the primal individual was the only valid entity. This is because at that time the many individuals were still without distinctiveness or selfhood, without being-for-themselves or independent will, and thus merely prototypes of the species, which manifested itself in them as the primal individual.

In the second world period, which is the historical one, the multiplicity of individuals has grown to independence, but even there, unity is not merely collective, but rather to be understood as the immanent substance of all individuals, which then appears to them as an external entity in the form of a God-man who has appeared on Earth: until in the third world period, all, without prejudice to their independence, know themselves as one spirit, and thus restore in spirit the primal individual as the only real one, which in the middle period partly merged into the creation had emerged—having, in part for the sake of its redemption through the Incarnation of the Logos, descended into a state of abasement. The epoch of the Spirit is now the perfected unification of Concept and Reality, whereas the realms of the Son and the Father stand merely on one side or the other. Yet, should not—even according to Scripture—those "last things" also take place on earth, and not in heaven?

The second epoch, however, is itself a totality—precisely because it constitutes the side of Appearance. For in Paganism, the primordial individual presents itself as the collective unity of the species—or, again, as the Father; in Christianity, it appears as a particular individual—i.e., as the Son; only to finally, in our own day—with the advent of the Spirit (that is, the transition from historical time into the third world-epoch)—synthesize the universal species and the particular individual into the eternal Personality of the Absolute Spirit, which is destined to reveal itself within every individual.

Thus, the consciousness of the Absolute has, at various times throughout the human race, unfolded itself into these distinct differentiations. In its primordial communal consciousness, it apprehends itself as a single, unified Individual; subsequently—with the dawn of world history—it entrusts this universal consciousness to a select few: to favored seers, heroes, and sages; until, finally, it concentrates this same consciousness within the singular consciousness of Christ. Thus, the prehistoric era evolved—rising from a broad base of universality—toward the apex of the pyramid, reaching its culmination point in world history; and now, upon this upright pyramid, an inverted one is being constructed. The epiphany of the eternal Personality of the Spirit within the phenomenal "Here-and-Now" does not forfeit its mode of appearance; rather, this "Here-and-Now"—qua singular subject—is transformed into a universal "Here-and-Now," a universal Self; that which it was initially—albeit unconsciously—it now is with full consciousness. However, the spiritual oneness of the individual with the universal Person constitutes the undifferentiated element amidst the merely psychological distinctions of feeling, intuition, and imagination, of religious impulses of faith, or of logical thought and dialectical reasoning.

Now, the three modes of manifestation of the eternal Incarnation are no longer opposed to the in-itself essence thereof; indeed, this essence itself is no longer situated outside of time, but has rather appeared adequately *within* it. Yet, in the very act of its passing, the semblance of temporal succession reveals itself to be an ideal—a conceptual—reality. Only in this way is time subordinated to the eternal Idea, inasmuch as the latter perpetually holds time within itself as a sublated moment; only in this way do the two sides cease to bear a temporal relation to one another. The eternal Idea admits of no "before" and "after"; it encompasses the entirety of time, and time, in turn, extends as far as does eternity. The distinction is one of dignity, not of duration. The Right Wing, however, grants the sensuous world but a brief duration, only to allow it subsequently to dissolve once more into the abstraction of a merely spiritual heaven. Thus, neither that initial "in-itself" state nor this subsequent return truly pertains to the realm of manifestation. The contradiction—which the Right Wing is unable to resolve—could therefore be formulated as follows: while it readily acknowledges the necessity of the sensuous world for the manifestation of the eternal Idea, it is simultaneously logically incapable of averting that world's ultimate demise. Yet why would the Absolute have ever resolved to create a sensuous world if the ultimate outcome were to be nothing other than that which existed solely at the very beginning—namely, the merely negative determination of the non-sensuous or the supersensuous? The Right Wing will therefore be compelled to concede that, since creation is a necessary prerequisite for the complete manifestation of God, a sensuous world—governed by the conditions of space and time—lays claim to that very same necessity. What, indeed, would be a phenomenon not bound to these categories? Furthermore, what would be a spirit that had no need of them, and did not possess the sensible world as its substrate? An empty abstraction. For subjectivity, singularity, and individuality belong to the nature of spirit; and this nature is bound to a sensible substrate—a substrate through whose very negation the spirit first elevates itself to the realm of ideality. Yet, for sensibility to be constantly idealized, it must also constantly exist. To deny the sensible world is, therefore, to abandon the absolute idealism of the spirit.

This necessity for the absolute spirit—namely, the existence of sensibility—was indeed recognized by the Master's successor when he stated: "The existence of the Idea must not be posited merely within human thought; rather, as fully realized, it must be posited within a specific individual who has stepped forth into the light of the world." But who, then—prior to Creation and subsequent to the Last Judgment—locates the existence of the Idea solely within thought? Surely not we, but rather the successor himself. Surely those who allow the sensible world to come into being only to pass away once more—that is to say, those who assert that natural existence is something chronologically subsequent speak of a primordial Being unknown to us—one that is even alleged to be the Second Person of the Trinity. Yet how, in turn, can they—without entangling themselves in contradictions—regard this primordial Being, in and of itself, as a divine Person? For, according to their own words, the very completion of such a Person requires a human being who has stepped forth into the light—a human being who, by that very token, necessarily postulates the sensible world and natural existence as something just as primordial as the primordial Being itself.

Furthermore, since the Absolute—prior to the moment the infant Jesus lay in the manger—cannot have been devoid of sensible existence, what becomes, within this view, of the individual human being who entered the world both before and after the course of Christ’s life? Is it one and the same individual—merely existing now in a different space and within a different sensible world? Surely no one could assert such a thing—least of all if one regards the sensible world as something that has come into being or is subject to transience. Thus, a realm of mere thought—a realm of mere spirit—is conjured up: a realm that is supposedly situated neither in space nor in time, yet which, paradoxically, constitutes neither actual spirit nor actual thought. In short, the logical categories—which find the true subjects of their existence only within nature and spirit—are, in their state of logical abstraction, treated as self-subsistent realities devoid of any genuine, concrete reality; they become—in a sense—a reality *above* reality, a hyper-physical, extra-temporal mode of existence. It is precisely here that the inherent distortion—so frequently discussed among our circle—of hypostatizing logic becomes glaringly apparent. For just as the second moment of the Trinity—that of *particularity*, embodied as a human being who has stepped forth into the light of the world—cannot be substantiated as existing in Heaven; so, too, will it prove impossible to discover any reality whatsoever for the third moment: the *Spirit* conceived as pure *individuality*. I therefore accept this friend’s assertion: A human being who has come into the light of the world is necessary for the complete realization of the Idea. But precisely for this reason, this "coming-into-the-light" has always taken place and will always continue to take place. Can the Absolute Idea ever, at any point in time, lack existence? However, since Christ did not come into the light of the world for all eternity—but only for a period of thirty-three years—and since the Idea cannot dispense with this "coming-into-the-light" for its own existence, it has always been *other* individuals in whom it has come into the light of the world; and the singularity—which the Master’s follower demands of the Idea’s existence in general, by confining it exclusively to the existence of Christ—applies only to its partial manifestation during the middle period of the second epoch of the world. In primordial times, every individual who came into the light of the world—as a selfless exemplar of the species—was an immediate epiphany of the Absolute Personality. In the second epoch of the world—when, with the emergence of individual selfhood, the state of being "outside the Absolute" began for individuals—the human race finally succeeded in viewing and conceiving of a *single* human being who had come into the light of the world as the adequate existence of the Idea. This initial dawning of the consciousness of the Absolute’s immanence within the subject had to spread—and indeed served as the impetus—so that every individual coming into the light of the world might be able to elevate themselves to this unity with the Absolute; and this, moreover, through their own act, once they had ceased to *be* the Absolute immediately.

Through this transposition of the history of Christ—from the realm of empirical events into that of religious conception—the entire cycle of miracles surrounding him (like a saint’s halo) acquires, for the first time, its true significance. The notion that the eternal Incarnation secured for itself external existence solely at one specific point in time belongs not to absolute truth, but merely to the human faculty of representation—a faculty confined within the bounds of time. We term a "miracle" any immediate activity of the Absolute—insofar as it does not appear as the consequence of general laws, as an eternal act, but is posited at a single, isolated point in time. However, since such a temporal manifestation of the Absolute is not a thing-in-itself, but merely a mental representation, the miracle falls squarely within this psychological domain, wherein it possesses its full validity. The universal miracle is the gradual triumph and dominion of Spirit over Nature; this we no longer call a miracle, and yet it is the only one that is truly real. Particular miracles, by contrast, are of a purely subjective nature—that is to say, they are symbolic, or are to be explained by reference to the psychological state of those present. Indeed, even our friend—that neighbor of the Sarmatians—remarks: "Regarding the conception of Christ by the Holy Spirit, I hold that the sole principle of Christ’s life was the Divine Spirit, which determined His will, and—through His will—His very nature." And when that individual—who has since leaped from the extreme Right to the extreme Left—having previously concurred with the Master that Christ possessed but one Father *in Himself*, went on to assert: if the supernatural conception of Christ is understood as the convergence of receptivity in the Virgin with creative necessity—then, within this still-rationalistic mode of explanation, lies a fundamentally correct insight: that of elevating the miracle into the realm of universal lawfulness. It was this creative necessity—by virtue of which an individual *had* to emerge who could legitimately regard himself as God—that shaped Christ into the figure he ultimately became. Yet it is both naive and narrow-minded to disregard the intermediate causes underlying this final cause. Were one to follow such deductions to their logical conclusion, Christ—precisely by virtue of the uncircumscribed nature of his being—ought to have simply fallen from the heavens, just as he is said to have eventually returned there.

However, the author of *The Life of Jesus* has already demonstrated—and quite rightly so—how, in his very origins, Christ came into being through a regular marriage, the narrative in which Joseph and Mary are depicted as conceiving—and indeed, even in the Gospels themselves, he is still referred to as the carpenter's son, and Joseph's genealogy is presented as his own—until, gradually, the legend began to grow and snowball in scope. For, inspired by Christ's teachings and convictions—and by the sheer moral integrity of his very presence—mankind felt compelled to apply to him the prevailing concept of the age: that of a man who had descended to earth as the unique Son of God. Consequently, as time passed, the story of his life was increasingly embellished with miraculous elements; and here the biographer demonstrates how, in the course of this evolving tradition, Joseph was portrayed as growing ever older—until, ultimately, he was depicted as incapable of procreation—while, conversely, that same tradition placed ever-greater emphasis on the virginity of the mother.

We may, therefore—aligning ourselves with the "extreme Left," which in this regard finds the *Glaubensphilosophie* (philosophy of faith) of Jacobi on its side—exclaim: To assert that the Incarnation is merely an *empirical fact* constitutes a form of obtuse religious materialism; yet we must insert a specific qualification before the phrase "empirical fact": namely, that it is a fact which occurred *once*. For the Incarnation is, in truth, an empirical fact that perpetually repeats itself; nevertheless, the belief—or the conception—that this event transpired but *once* within the realm of external experience is itself something equally necessary. For in order that this eternal history—that is to say, the abstract Idea—may truly become *historical* in every age, it must first have appeared, at least once, as a concrete historical event—an event which thereafter repeats itself unceasingly. Here, however, I hear an objection raised by the very person who—precisely because of his versatility—would be best suited to mediate between the two extremes of the school: namely, that according to this doctrine of mine, existence and the Idea do not correspond to one another, since the manifestation itself is held to be not a true divinity, but merely an imagined one. "The thing-in-itself—the reality of the Idea within its manifestation—cannot be localized in Christ. That singular figure, whose memory history has preserved for us in such a way that we can still form a mental image of his immediate life, alone realized the Idea in its full reality as an individual manifestation; apart from him, no other human being is commensurate with the Concept. But is the thing-in-itself thereby adequately realized—is it truly brought to fruition—simply by presenting itself in a single, isolated expression? On the contrary. By that very token, it is reduced to a mere phenomenon. Neither of us denies that the Idea—in its absolute, intrinsic nature—is temporal; and I fully agree with our friend when he states: 'Insofar as time constitutes the manifestation of eternity—and given that an eternity which did not externalize itself in time would be a mere abstraction—redemption must, by necessity, be an event occurring within time.'" Certainly! Yet, since it is only the *totality* of the manifestation that constitutes the adequate representation of the Idea, redemption—by virtue of its eternal nature—must be an event occurring at *all* times. Thus, by characterizing the occurrence of this event at a specific point in time as merely an isolated moment within the human imagination—standing to the entire chain of manifestation as a single link stands to the whole—it is not *I* who renders the manifestation of the Idea inadequate, but rather our friend. For every specific moment of history is—both by virtue of its duration and its content—necessarily limited; whereas, once the goal of world history has been attained, a whole eternity will be contained within every moment of time—not merely *in itself* (as has always been the case), but then also *for us*. The totality of the phenomenon has thus become adequate to the Idea only in its final stage—the stage that encompasses within itself all preceding ones.

We did, admittedly, state that the very characteristic of the appearance of Christ lay in bringing the perfection existing *in itself* to the consciousness of human beings. Yet this "being-for-us" is merely our conception; it therefore possesses no corresponding object as yet, and only attains reality when the *in-itself* and the *for-us* have reconciled with one another as an *in-and-for-itself-being*—something that remains unaccomplished so long as the appearance of the Absolute remains for us merely a "this," and not yet the universal Self. My objection to the Christology of the "Right Wing" therefore remains unrefuted: namely, that even if Christ were indeed the perfected reality of the religious Idea, he could—precisely by virtue of this specific character—impossibly constitute the reality of the political, aesthetic, and philosophical Ideas. Furthermore, to view moral perfection as the *absolute* absolute—elevating it above those merely *relative* absolutes, as some do—would constitute, on the one hand, a regression into the rationalism of the purely moral standpoint; and on the other, one could find already realized in Socrates precisely what such a view regards as the highest perfection of the person of Christ. The Right Wing is therefore surely correct in seeking the perfected realization of the Idea within human subjectivity; Christ, however, is the highest stage of the reality adequate to the Idea—only *for us* or in immediate intuition, but not yet in the Spirit or *in and for itself*. For he himself points out that the outpouring of the Spirit would occur only after his death. The inconsistency—inherent in the very attempt to locate this perfected realization within a single human individual—is indeed corrected by the Right Wing; yet this correction is achieved only through an even greater inconsistency: that of having added the *non-appearance* of a non-sensuous "This" to the *appearance* of a sensuous "This" on earth—without—quite prudently—specifying more precisely the relationship between the heavenly Person and the earthly individual.

The deficiency of the Right Wing is thus evident: firstly, in having dragged down eternal existence into the mere existence of an imagined primordial Person; and secondly, in having elevated the actual life of a sensuous individual—not merely to the status of a *moment*—but to that of the *totality* of the Absolute's revelations. For the Right Wing, the primary concern is the *empirical* existence of this individual; it seeks to preserve this existence in its sheer *facticity*, while simultaneously elevating it to the status of the adequate existence of the entire process of the Idea. However, since the exclusive divinity of Christ belongs solely to the realm of *representation*, this mode of speculation upholds a merely transient form as the *essential* reality—seeking to anchor it firmly within the general ether of the Idea by means of logical categories. For the Right Wing, historical Christianity is not merely the second—albeit necessary—moment within the historical epoch (as my noble friend already conceived it in his *Historiosophy*), but rather the fundamental intuition of eternal truth in its entire scope. Since these philosophers are unwilling to strip away from Christianity those merely temporal representations that still cling to it, they are, by definition, the *positive*—the *conservative*—philosophers. However, if philosophy consists in devising a subtle dialectical turn to prop up an obsolete dogmatic notion—one no longer sustained by faith—then it is the most futile of all undertakings; for such dialectic remains forever incomprehensible to the masses, while the knowledgeable easily discern the speciousness of such deductions.

Now, while the Right Wing elevated a one-sided aspect of the phenomenon to the level of the totality of the Idea, the Left Wing rejected this aspect of the phenomenon entirely. Yet the notion of Christ’s divinity remains, after all, a psychological fact—and the faith of nearly two millennia is of greater worth than if even a supernatural conception by the Holy Spirit in the womb of a virgin could ever be empirically proven and concretely demonstrated as an external fact. To the Left Wing, however, this notion counts for nothing at all—that is to say, it regards it as a mere mental representation; it adopts a purely negative and dissolvent stance toward it, highlighting solely the eternal Incarnation as a process occurring in the selfsame manner throughout all ages, without distinguishing its various stages of manifestation or demonstrating the necessity of the figure of Christ by showing him to be a link in this chain of the Absolute’s self-revelation—whereby the notion itself becomes a constituent moment in the very being of the Absolute, and thereby acquires objectivity.

This sole vindication of the Idea is introduced by the worthy captain of Dogmatics who—thoroughly imbued with the tenets of the school’s founder, and resting calmly at the very center of the school—simply employs these notions as vehicles for expressing ideas. The underlying antithesis has not yet emerged in any overt manner—much as was the case with the Master himself. Thus, while a theologian devoid of philosophical insight may find the Christian notions fully affirmed within the teachings of our dogmatist, we philosophers, on the contrary, must recognize therein the genuine Idea of ​​the matter, revealed in its unadorned form. As an event—or in a historical sense—this unity of God with man is manifest and real in the person of Jesus Christ, as in no other human being. With this comparison, the judgment—even if Christ is acknowledged as the Incomparable One—still rests upon the ground of experience, which permits no definitive conclusion to the comparison. The truth of the comparison is, in and of itself—or primarily—nothing empirical or historical, but rather the Idea that has become historically manifest in Christ. Every human being who truly lives in faith in God—whether Jew or Gentile—is a witness to the eternal truth that God has become man. As this particular individual—as this specific person—Christ is not the Son of God, but the Son of Man: he who feels hunger and thirst, suffers, and dies. Jesus Christ is the Son of God as the human being who is universal in his particularity, and particular in his universality. In the person of Jesus Christ, the self-revealing God creates the individual with whom He can be most perfectly one.

But is this superlative, then, not a comparison distinction anymore? Ought it not, at the very least, read: "is most perfectly one"? For surely the Absolute must be capable of being perfectly one with all individuals. Thus, although the Idea is consistently proclaimed by our friend as the Supreme—such that events and the historical realm are meant to recede into the background, the individual is meant to be sublated, and the universal is meant to constitute the essence therein—he nevertheless repeatedly concentrates the highest revelation of this Idea within a single, specific moment in time, even though any specific temporal determination can be nothing more than a fleeting moment within the eternal Idea. This is the thread of the "Right Wing"—a thread that our theologian, having supposedly broken free from that camp, still drags along behind him. I, too, admittedly designate a specific era—the third world-period—as the transition point where history passes into the post-historical age, constituting the total manifestation of the Absolute; for it is within this era that the dominion of the Spirit reaches its consummation. Yet, firstly, this final era is not merely a fleeting historical event; rather—once the substantive development of world history has run its course—it stands as a never-ceasing presentation of the very content that history has thereby attained. And secondly, in truth, it is only the totality of time—that is to say, the three taken together across all world-epochs, this constitutes the completed revelation of the Absolute—a revelation which, for a temporal consciousness, therefore remains incomplete until the post-historical era has dawned.

The naive contradiction into which our fellow thinker fell thus arose from the fact that, while maintaining the sole validity of the Idea, he nevertheless viewed a single moment of the manifestation of the eternal Incarnation—and indeed a wholly transient one—as constituting its total expression. To be sure, this is precisely the moment in which this revelation is to *become* for us—that is, the moment in which subjective representation is to apprehend the entire course of that revelation; and for this reason, this moment constitutes, preeminently, the very epiphany of the eternal personality of Spirit. However, this subjectivity of consciousness must be strictly distinguished from the objectivity of the revelation at every stage of its manifestation. Because the objectivity of the Incarnation in Christ stands out in isolation for subjective representation, He alone is taken to represent the aspect of the Idea that falls within the realm of appearance. Yet, since the *knowledge* of this reality—the reality of the divine substance within Him (which is, admittedly, the most emphasized point regarding Christ)—remains an essential stage of the objective revelation—such that this individual man *is* God precisely because he himself, his disciples, and all who believe in him, apprehend or represent him *as* God—it follows that, according to our definitions, the unity of the subjective and the objective is by no means lacking in Christ.

Nevertheless, the aforementioned dogmatist here raises an objection—both against us and against himself: "The Idea of ​​the divine Incarnation possesses, as such, the necessary form of thought; and from the standpoint of this form, it can easily be conceived merely as the *thought-of* magnitude and perfection of Christ—whereby the Idea is reduced to a mere *Ideal* whose sole foundation lies in subjective thought, to which nothing objective corresponds. The subject pays our Lord Christ the honor of thinking and representing Him in this manner; yet the fact that He is so represented is something He owes solely to that thinking subject." Regarding this, it must first be noted that—if representation is indeed the very element through which religion lends objectivity to its dogmas—then in Christianity, representation and objectivity have become entirely identical, inasmuch as the object of the representation is no longer the represented entity, but rather the representing subject itself. Furthermore, it is not merely that some individual subject arbitrarily holds this representation of Christ; rather, his deeds and actions—his thoughts and aspirations, indeed the entire complex of his manifestation—must inevitably have compelled humanity to form the conception that he is the Son of God. This representation, therefore, possesses objectivity by virtue of the universality of its realization in human consciousness; and surely our friend would not wish to demand an *empirical* objectivity within the realm of sensory events—specifically, a carnal act of procreation and conception. However, if one understands objectivity in the manner of "the Versatile One"—namely, that "in Christ, the unity of man with God is established not merely through man (as is the case with us), but equally through God"—then the initial rejoinder must be that the unity of substance is an act of the Absolute itself, occurring in all human beings just as it does in Christ. Yet, the act of knowing oneself as divine—and of making oneself divine—is, equally and invariably, the act of the conscious subject. I concede the point completely only in this specific sense: that since the unity of the divine and human natures had, at some point, to be concretely represented in a single individual, the appearance of this individual emerges as a manifest link within the very process of the Absolute’s self-revelation; it may, therefore, be regarded as a divine act—whereas the deification of man in other individuals need be viewed, in a certain sense, merely as their own subjective, private affair, rather than as an objective act of universal world-history itself.

Thus, even if we grant the "Right Wing" that a divine, world-historical agency lies concealed within the sensuous manifestation of Christ *as such*, we are not thereby compelled to equate this empirical history—in its immediate, unmediated form—with the absolute act *per se*. Rather, we must also, in turn, side with the "Left Wing," which confronts us with its entirely valid canon: that no empirical event can ever serve, in every respect, as the fully adequate expression of the Idea. We do not wish to deny the credit for having highlighted this truth to our friends—whom, in contradistinction to the Right Wing, we may aptly designate as "religious idealists."

The transition to this group is effected by that theologian who, on the one hand, aligns himself with the leading figure of our dogmatics, yet who—through his critical analysis of the Old Testament—has, on the other hand, provided a counterpart to the "Iconoclast of Dogma." He states: "By virtue of its infinity, the subject constitutes, indeed, the reality of the universal Idea; yet—owing to its finitude—it invariably apprehends that Idea only through the medium of some specific limitation. No single personality, therefore, serves as the sole bearer of the Spirit in the absolute totality of its determinations. Therefore, in the Incarnation—within the historical personality of Christ—the Logos divested itself of its absolute universality; the Idea entered into a form ill-suited to its true universality, and this limitation of personality maintained spiritual universality in a specific mode only until it was transcended—raised up to become a spiritual power existing *in and for itself*, fulfilling and transfiguring all personalities—and thereby became the organic, total fullness of the divine life. Thus, the notion that the historical Christ, in his earthly manifestation, encompassed within himself the entire fullness of spiritual humanity is an absurd one; this can be asserted only of the transfigured Christ—he who has been elevated to the status of the concrete Idea."

This doctrine no longer hovers—as the previous one did—in a state of *unconscious* indeterminacy, but rather in a state of *conscious* indeterminacy, suspended between the Right and the Left. Through the ambiguity of its terminology, it laboriously clings to the thread of tradition, seeking to retain its foothold on the Right. While it purports to mediate between the two sides, it ultimately—by fully accommodating itself to the Right in terms of *form*—reverts, in terms of *substance*, to the Left; yet, in doing so, it lacks the latter’s logical consistency, for the perspective of the Right settles within it like a heavy sediment, never failing to periodically cloud the clarity of the entire exposition. For the assertion that the Logos, during the Incarnation in Christ, divested itself of its absolute universality sounds precisely like a historical fact. Yet the true historical event is not that the eternal Idea assumed the *form* of personality, but rather that the human race—beginning, specifically, with Christ—attained the *consciousness* that the absolute Idea is Person. Subsequently, however, our friend once again reduces this very Logos to a "metaphysical abstraction of rational reality." ...so that "the Christ transfigured into a concrete totality" would constitute the Third element, within which the individual has been elevated to unity with the Idea. I can assent to this only insofar as this concrete totality of a transfigured Christ is the Community; yet one cannot claim that this has been stated with any great clarity or forthrightness, given that—lurking beneath those very words—there could just as easily reside a Christ who, in the sense of the "Right," has ascended into heaven. However, I fully concede the point our friend adds: that "historical development transcends the limitations of personality." Finally, in declaring that "the organic fullness of divine life permeates and transfigures all personalities," he has, in truth, proclaimed the third world-period—the Realm of the Spirit; and for this, I extend to him the hand of brotherhood. Yet the fact that he designates this Spirit merely by name—and characterizes it as something impersonal or transpersonal—is precisely what still divides us from one another. This difference might perhaps vanish were he willing to articulate his ultimate conviction without reservation.

Now, however—by asserting the existence of an impersonal Absolute—our friend crosses over entirely to the side of the "Left." In doing so, he ventures to cast off the belief held by the "Right"—namely, that the manifestation of the Absolute in Christ constitutes the Absolute itself, or the totality of its manifestation—given that this manifestation is, in reality, merely a transient moment of that Absolute. Our friend was the first to recognize this truth; and for this reason, he was also justified in identifying the Idea—rather than the specific manifestation—as the truly Absolute element.

I must now examine the perspective of the "Left" as embodied by its purest representative, in order to highlight—in his work as well—both the one-sided aspects and the valid insights he has advanced, and to formulate the final conclusion that compels me, I believe, to part ways with him as well. Let us turn our attention, then, to this purpose initially stood quite firmly on the standpoint of his concluding treatise on *The Life of Jesus*.

"The formula," it states therein—that in Christ the archetypal was simultaneously historical—"constitutes a dangerous point. For if the Ideal is to be acknowledged as real within a single historical person—even if only within the sphere of God-consciousness—this cannot occur without violating the laws of nature through the assumption of a miracle, since God-consciousness, too, in its development and manifestation, is subject to the conditions of finitude and imperfection. Prompted by Jesus’ relative excellence, the community has conceived an ideal of absolute perfection and projected it onto the historical Christ, from whom it now continually draws strength and renewed vitality for its own God-consciousness. But in doing so, do we thereby negate all reality of the Idea? By no means. If reality is ascribed to the Idea of ​​the unity of divine and human nature, does this imply that it must have become real—once, in a single individual—in a manner never to be repeated before or since? That is simply not the way in which the Idea realizes itself: by pouring its entire fullness into a single specimen to lavish upon a few, while begrudging it to all others; rather, she loves to unfold her wealth in a diversity of instances that mutually complement one another—in a ceaseless interplay of individuals emerging and passing away. And is this to be deemed no true reality of the Idea? Would the Idea of ​​the unity of divine and human nature not be—in an infinitely higher sense—more truly real if I were to conceive of the whole of humanity as its realization, than if I were to single out a solitary individual as such? Is an incarnation of God from all eternity not more truly real than one confined to a single, isolated point in time?

This is expressed most admirably; yet I cannot but marvel that the astute leader of the Left could exclaim in response: “Herein lies the key to the whole of Christology: that, as the subject of the predicates which the Church attributes to Christ, an Idea is posited in place of an individual”—seeing as he thereby affirms merely the eternal incarnation, but by no means the historical one. He now transfers all the predicates of dogmatics—which, if ascribed to Christ individually, would be inadmissible—onto the *genus*: such as the union of the two natures, the working of miracles, sinlessness, the Ascension, and so forth. "This," he adds, "is the absolute content of Christology. When the community—prompted by the person and destiny of Jesus—drafted the image of its Christ, the idea of ​​humanity in its relationship to the Divine hovered unconsciously before its mind. The scholarship of our own time, however, can no longer suppress the realization that the reference to a single individual belongs merely to the historically and culturally conditioned form of this doctrine."

You see, my friends, I stand on exactly the same ground as this iconoclast of dogma; and yet, from within this very standpoint, I conceive of Christology in a diametrically opposite manner. Even for the Greeks, the *genus* constituted the Divine—though for them it was unfolded into a pantheon of divine powers, whereas among the Jews it appears as the abstract, universal concept of the One. In Greece, the *genus* unfolds itself within marriage, the state, art, science, and so forth; and these specific aspects of the generic concept are, moreover, hypostatized into individual figures: Jupiter, Juno, Minerva, Apollo, and the like. According to our friend, Christ cannot even be distinguished from these divine figures by the claim that the *total* idea is hypostatized in him, whereas those others represent only specific differentiations of it. For Christ, after all, is said to have developed only the *religious* idea—and thus merely a specific one—such that "it is only within the sphere of religion that one cannot go beyond him." The philosopher of nature would then be justified in portraying Christ as nothing more than the final pinnacle of the ancient pantheon. That is, to be sure, one side of the matter. When Christ still lay in swaddling clothes, the only conceivable notion was, in truth, that the Divine had entered into the finite as such. This constitutes the pagan element within Christianity; and it was for this reason that the Magi came to worship Christ in the manger. Yet this element—as the activity of the Idea—is merely the *presupposition* of Christianity; conversely, the entire conscious life-activity of Christ consisted precisely in realizing this Idea himself as the ultimate goal of all existence. The other aspect of Christ, therefore, is this: that he did not so much teach the divinity of the Idea—as the negative dogmatist, drawing a parallel between Christ’s teaching and that of Plato, would have us believe—but rather that, just as Aristotle had already declared individuals to be more substantial than genera, so too did Christ primarily champion the absolute nature of the individual person, asserting that the individual’s return to the Divine was an act of their own making. Thus, what constitutes the merely temporal and culturally specific aspect of his teaching is not the fact that he articulated the Divine as a Person—but rather the fact that he, and those who believed in him, perceived it solely within *this one* specific Person. This arose from the fact that, initially, they represented merely the opposite extreme to Antiquity, paying less heed to the divinity of the universal Idea and concentrating all their veneration upon this single individual. Our new Christianity, therefore, represents the higher synthesis between the pre-Christian religions and that medieval Christianity. I shall now proceed to elaborate upon this point in a few words.

"Paganism," observes our Master—in a statement upon which the reactionary looks with great covetousness—"is the immediate deification of the finite; Christianity is the beholding of God within the finite." This antithesis is the only one possible within the sphere of religion; hence, there exist only Paganism and Christianity. Beyond these two, there is nothing save the Absolute nature common to them both. The former perceives the natural directly within the Divine and the spiritual archetypes; the latter looks through nature—conceived as the infinite body of God—into the innermost depths and the very Spirit of God. That is to say: to dissolve every individual entity directly into the genus, substance, or Idea—or whatever one chooses to call it—or, in short, to plunge it into the abyss of the Absolute characteristic of Schelling’s philosophy of nature—this constitutes Pantheism and Paganism; and the author of *The Life of Jesus* is—as my Nordic friend has observed—not entirely free from this tendency. We can rediscover this standpoint in all pre-Christian religions—religions which, being merely national in character, I would term "ethnic." 

In contrast to all these ethnic religions, it is precisely the distinctive characteristic of Christianity that the Perfect—the Absolute—is not the genus, but rather the Person. Our friend would merely concede that the Person brings the eternal Idea into reality brings forth; but that wherein the True alone becomes actual is itself, in the first instance, the True. The genus as such lacks *being-for-itself*—the return into itself; it is merely substance *in-itself*. Herein lie the valid grounds for all the arguments of the Right against the Left; yet we must not allow ourselves to be misled by this into positing a dreamed-up, otherworldly Person. For the fact that the actual person—as an individual—is always simultaneously imperfect cannot overturn our assertion regarding the absolute nature of the Person; indeed, I would buttress this assertion with our friend’s own words: “Rather, with the consciousness of imperfection and sinfulness, the conception of the Perfect and the Sinless is given just as necessarily as, with the consciousness of finitude, that of the Infinite; for both conceptions mutually condition one another, and the one is utterly impossible without the other.” Substance, I therefore maintain, cannot be perfect precisely because the concept of imperfection cannot be applied to it; yet with the removal of the one antithesis, the other—its counterpart—falls away as well; for perfection is nothing other than the active struggle to extricate oneself from the imperfect. The key to Christology, therefore, lies rather in the recognition that the predicate of perfection—like all other predicates—pertains not to the genus, but to the Person.

Admittedly, this must not be merely *one* individual person, but rather the universal Person—the one who is truly actual solely within all phenomenal *I*s. To be sure, religion does not entirely lack this identity of the Person; yet, just as it relegated the *unmanifested* Second Person of the Trinity to an otherworldly past—while simultaneously attributing a *sensuous* presence to this Person (whom it had elevated, in its conception, to the status of absolute divinity)—so, too, does it regard the ultimate reintegration of all persons into the unity of the Spirit not merely as something belonging to the future (something it might conceivably concede), but also as something otherworldly—something that is, in essence, *nothing*. Through the outpouring of the Holy Spirit—through the Sacrament of the Lord's Supper—this true third Person of the Divine Trinity does indeed become, even now, partially and perpetually a present and immanent reality; yet the absolute unity remains also a matter of the future—something transcendent—and must remain so, because this elevation into the substantial Idea—from which the ethnic religions never truly emerged—appears to the faculty of representation itself as a singular, isolated fact, which must therefore be situated within a specific moment in time. The philosopher alone knows how to perfectly reconcile this with the eternal Idea, by asserting—with the consummation of the ages—that its perpetual renewal within the individual constitutes the adequate realization of the Concept. That bringing this truth to fruition would be the task of his philosophy is something our Master divined in a prophetic spirit when, in the continuation of the speech cited earlier, he stated: "Whether this moment in time—which has become such a remarkable turning point for all temporal formations, for the sciences, and for the works of man—will not also prove to be such for religion; and whether the era of the true Gospel—the reconciliation of the world with God—is not drawing nearer in proportion as the temporal and merely external forms of Christianity decay and vanish: this is a question that must be left to the individual answer of anyone who understands the signs of the future."

Our Master speaks most beautifully of this religion—which is already announcing itself in isolated revelations—describing it as "a leading back to the primordial mystery of Christianity, and the consummation thereof." To this end, however, the element of the ethnic religions—which stands in opposition to the Christianity of the past—must be incorporated precisely as an integral component of that very Christianity. Let us, therefore, fix our gaze sharply upon this opposition, so that we may recognize all the more thoroughly the unity that results from its resolution. Paganism is anthropomorphic; within it, the gods are fashioned in the likeness of men. For substance manifests itself in its accidents as itself. Because only the general idea holds sway here, we were able to conceive of this epoch as the Realm of the Father—not in its prehistoric *in-itself*, but within a specific historical period. Christianity is apotheosistic; for the Son of Man has become the Son of God through spiritual generation. In the former instance, the divinity of the sphere of ethical powers constitutes the reality, while the humanity of the gods is merely a mental representation; in Christianity, conversely, the actual human being is God, while the incarnation of the divine substance is merely a mental representation. In the former, incarnation is eternal, for it proceeds from the species as a whole; in the latter, it is empirical, for it is predicated upon the transfiguration of a single individual.

The new Christianity—which I, for one, believe to be already dawning—consists in the reciprocal interpenetration of these two aspects. It is, therefore, the confident assurance that—just as this one human person, Christ, is one with God through his self-knowledge *in* God—in precisely the same manner, all other human beings as well to attain this knowledge, and thus the Being, of the Absolute. The historical Christ rises again within every believer; and whereas in antiquity the eternal incarnation was perfected only *in itself*—or only within the species—and in Christianity only in this *one* individual, now, in the new Christianity, it is perfected in *all*. Yet the seeds for this are scattered everywhere throughout the old tradition; we must not, however, leave it as the priestly dominion has shaped it, but rather realize it through the brotherhood of all mankind—a brotherhood that must not remain a mere empty phrase. Do we not—according to Scripture itself—do unto God whatever we do unto the least of our brethren? Now, the All-ness constitutes the species itself in empirical form, and thereby establishes the unity of Concept and Reality.

In this way, I have reconciled the religion of the historical Christ with the religion of the eternal Incarnation, just as I announced in our earlier discussions. And if the "extreme Left" asserts that the "essence of Christianity" lies in the worship of man's own self-consciousness—and that his God is, therefore, merely an anthropological one—then we must reply that man is able to make his innermost essence—absolute self-consciousness—into his God only because the Absolute itself has revealed itself in an adequate manner within man—and, indeed, *only* within man; for the Son of Man is God's only-begotten Son. Thus, it is not merely because we are human that we elevate our human nature to the level of the Absolute; rather, it is also because human nature *is* the Absolute that we worship the Absolute within it. Blessed is the man who—like Christ—has ascended to that stage of purification where, as Plotinus puts it, "the solitary entering into the Solitary," he rediscovers absolute self-consciousness within the innermost chamber of his own heart. The sole failing of the "extreme Left" lies in having dissolved the substantiality of the Absolute into absolute self-consciousness, thereby reducing God to a merely anthropological entity.

However, since this doctrine sprang from the fundamental principle of the "Left"—namely, that of conceiving the Absolute solely as Substance—you can see, dear friends, in this new example, how the extremes meet. And because they—the Species and the anthropological God—touch one another, I have fused them into the concrete totality of the eternal personality of the Spirit, without, as one among you accused me of doing, having linked them together in a manner that creates a contradiction within my view.

If, then, the biographer of Jesus expresses the conviction in his *Dogmatics* that, "in order to advance Christology beyond the standpoint of his concluding essay on the *Life of Jesus*, the very first intelligible word on the subject has yet to be uttered," then I believe that I have indeed already uttered that very word through my discussions thus far.

Yet our friend does himself an injustice when he imagines that, in his *Dogmatics*, he still stands entirely upon the standpoint of that earlier concluding essay; for he clearly demonstrates a visible striving to move toward the Right, and thereby to close the dialectical circle. For, in the very course of his triumphant refutations of the Right, he is nonetheless seized by that correct element which is inherent even in their position, as he exclaims: "But how does the opponent know that the Christology he disputes understands the Species of humanity as an impersonal universal—subsumed under the genus of humanity—and understands participation in that Species as merely substantial, natural, and unfree? To this Christology, the Species of humanity is admittedly not a *person* in the opponent's sense; yet neither is it an *impersonal entity*—like an animal species—but rather a *person-forming principle*, insofar as it essentially brings forth persons from itself and finds its reality within them. If, therefore, the Species is *God-human* only insofar as it is *person-forming*, then it will realize this God-humanity—and with it, Reconciliation—precisely within individual personalities; and in this respect, therefore, we are in complete agreement."

But how, then, does the spirited combatant here dispose of the objection raised by the Right: that whatever forms persons must itself be a person? On this point, I side entirely with the Right. The genus is always impersonal; and that which constitutes personality is in no way superior to the trans-personal principle discussed earlier. The limitation inherent in finite personality invariably lurks in the background of those perspectives that are unwilling to conceive of the Absolute itself as a Person. Yet, just as it is commonly conceived regarding Christ—that he had to transcend the limitation by virtue of which he is called the Son of Man in order to be named the Son of God—so, too, can every individual attain to that Person which is identical in all, and do so only through the negation of their own peculiar limitations; this is the true Person—the one that alone endures amidst the dissolution of all others—of whom we form an image when we think of Christ, and whose nature we recognize when we apprehend it as the Spirit of the Community. It is, therefore, with profound significance that orthodox Trinitarian doctrine likewise designates this Holy Spirit as a Person.

Thus, it is not merely the substance that constitutes personality, but equally the Person constituted by that substance, that constitutes the Absolute; for, as the cause of itself, the Absolute becomes the true cause only through its own effect—a truth that has been rendered vividly evident to mankind through the manifestation of Christ. In order to express symbolically an effect adequate to its cause—it has, in fact, availed itself of the concept of the relationship between Son and Father, who are one in spirit.

What, then—thirdly—emerges from this developmental trajectory of the school as the enduring result of contemporary speculation? To ascertain this, let us once again place the two opposing directions in sharp juxtaposition within a brief overview.

The Left Wing oscillates between two imperfections: the Eternal Idea—because it lacks realization in the *being-for-itself* of an absolute person; and the historical Christ—because a single individual, as such, can never be adequate to the universal genus. The Right Wing, conversely, boldly asserts two perfections: a perfect primordial individual in Heaven—the moment of the Son within the Trinity; and this second person, incarnate on Earth—without concern for how the imperfection of the individual, *other-worldly* particularity might be reconciled with a *this-worldly* perfection of the Absolute.

We reconcile both sides with one another by perceiving the perfect amidst the imperfect, and by elevating the imperfect into the perfect. Like the pagans, we deify the finite—yet we do so solely because, even as Christians, we behold the Absolute within the finite—and indeed, behold it as the sole reality— that is, as the finite itself has brought this reality to manifestation within its own nature.

Furthermore, while the Right Wing—having quite correctly grasped the Idea—proceeds to individualize that Idea into a specific person of the Trinity, the Left Wing, conversely, idealizes the empirical manifestation. Through the Fall—which constitutes a specific moment in the development of finite knowledge regarding the Absolute—there comes to pass precisely what the Right Wing depicts as an eternal truth: namely, the absolute transcendence of a *transcendent* Subject—a Subject that, in the person of Christ, attains to a *relative* immanence for but a fleeting moment in time. Through the dissolution of world history into the post-historical era, that comes to pass which the Left asserts to have been present at all times: the absolute immanence of the substantial Idea within all individuals—while neglecting the Idea’s *temporary* transcendence. To this Absolute of the Left—which, precisely because of such transcendence and the limitations inherent in the individual person, remains merely *ideal*—we therefore add its adequate *existence*, albeit as a future reality. The fact that, in our view, the Absolute is not yet fully realized at this moment does not trouble us; for since time itself is an ideal construct, it cannot constitute the true form of the Absolute’s existence—particularly once we have isolated a single moment of it—the empirical present—from the entire complex continuum of its duration. The demand—which Schelling sought to resolve through his philosophy of revelation—that the Absolute, even in the midst of its *becoming*, is nonetheless *not* a process of becoming, is satisfied only in this manner. Thus, the fact that a single individual—to the exclusion of all others—is elevated to serve as the preeminent embodiment of the Idea constitutes merely one facet of the Idea’s manifestation: specifically, the process by which the Absolute attains self-consciousness within the human race—perceived here as a *transcendent* Subject standing apart from a humanity that has fallen into sin, yet seeking to guide that very humanity back into unity with itself. For just as this antithesis came into being, so too must it eventually cease to be; and this cessation—conceived as an actual, realized state—constitutes the history of the future. Papalism, conversely—and by way of digression—represents the principle of stability and the *status quo*; for it seeks to perpetuate, for all eternity and even within the sensible world, the exclusive divinity of a specific *person*—embodied by his Vicar, the Holy Father. The adherents of Rome are, therefore, those who seek to stifle the dawning consciousness of the absolute Idea—conceived as a universal Self—by elevating a mere *phenomenal manifestation* to the status of the Absolute itself. 

While Protestantism has, in principle, liberated us from this servitude to a "Lama"—insofar as the sensuous person as such is no longer an object of worship—this person nevertheless persists in memory and in hope as a singular entity, albeit a non-sensuous one. The Right Wing of our school has merely philosophized this standpoint of Protestantism, for it, too, withdraws from the historical phenomenon into the *in-itself* of faith in a specific, singular Subject; the Left Wing, conversely—transcending all this—affirms that the adequate realization of the Absolute is already present even within the inadequate temporal development of history. We demonstrate why the eternal Incarnation must necessarily enter the consciousness of all human beings in the form of the historical Christ, so that they—by appropriating the knowledge of Christ—may ultimately bring about the perfect realization of the eternal Incarnation through its manifestation in all individuals. In a word: whereas the Left Wing negates the specific characteristic of the Christian perspective—namely, its restriction to a single Person—we practically affirm this specific characteristic as a necessary means, even if it is a means that is ultimately transcended in the attainment of the final end.

If, then—in light of this—I am now to openly profess my creed, my beloved friends, I must first note that many—both among us and outside our ranks—will dismiss these words of mine as a mere reinterpretation and accommodation that fails to capture the true meaning of Christianity in the remotest degree. But, my friends, what *is* the true meaning of Christian doctrine? Were the councils of the Catholic Church able to establish it for all eternity? Are not the Augsburg Confession and the Formula of Concord themselves merely products of their time? Did not our Provincial Synod refuse to endorse a "legally binding commitment" of candidates to the confessional writings? Must not the Evangelical Church return to the interpretation of Holy Scripture? And as for the latter—even if the sacred writers themselves understood their words in a literal sense—we nevertheless possess the right, in accordance with the concept of inspiration, to interpret them in a mystical—that is to say, speculative—sense. For "You have my Spirit," said the divine Founder of our religion; “He remains with you until the end of the world, and will guide you into all truth.”

To what extent I believe He has indeed guided us—this you may gather from the following intellectual confession of one who, I flatter myself, is a philosopher of the nineteenth century. In doing so, however, I touch only upon that part of the Creed which concerns the doctrine of the Son—since we have already left the Father, with His doctrine of creation, behind us, while the Spirit still lies before us—yet I intend to take into account not merely the simpler teaching of Scripture, but also the traditional elaboration of dogma into a more precise theological formulation, insofar as this is feasible.

“I believe in Jesus Christ, the only-begotten Son of God the Father, our Lord.” That is to say: I know that the eternal personality of the Absolute—as the Father—first rendered itself objective as a person to the consciousness of the human race in the man Jesus; for Christ was the first, in the development of world history, to grasp the absolute presence of the divine Person as the innermost essence of his own individuality. It is by this, moreover, that the uniqueness of his Sonship is conditioned. For although we, too, are called sons of the Absolute, this right derives for us solely from the fact that we completely immerse all our differences within this manifestation of the absolute personality—that is, by becoming one with the Father in the Son. Notwithstanding the diversity of the members of the community, they are nonetheless, one and all, nothing other than the only-begotten Son of God—the unified, and therefore adequate, epiphany of His eternal personality. Our Lord is Christ, however—partly insofar as we perceive ourselves as finite persons subject to the Absolute, without yet having established ourselves in free unity with it—and thus, rather, project the immanent absolute Person outside of ourselves and transfer it exclusively onto this historical individual; and partly insofar as Christ has indeed been our guide, leading us to comprehend the unity of the absolute and the finite person.

"Conceived by the Holy Spirit, born of the Virgin Mary." In terms of the Idea, the Spirit of the human race—the World Spirit—begot the historical figure of Christ as a necessary manifestation. When a revolution in history is ripe, it is never the act of a single individual to conjure forth such figures. Individuals—without prejudice to their own individual freedom—are, in relation to the absolute Spirit, merely blind instruments. The Spirit creates its own means by its own sovereign power, depositing the seeds of a new era into the virginal womb of the future, so that—having received the seed—it may give birth to the Son begotten by the Spirit. Thus, in terms of his spiritual nature, Christ was conceived by the universal Spirit. However, since—as we observed earlier—this does not preclude secondary causes, the assertion that Joseph begot Christ in the flesh does not constitute a denial of his conception by the Holy Spirit. Only if the Holy Spirit were intended to take Joseph’s place would that blasphemous comparison—cited by Paulus prior to the publication of Spinoza’s works—between "Mary’s Dove" and "Leda’s beautiful Swan" become conceivable. The Spirit of World History, therefore, utilized physical procreation merely as a means to an end. Yet the dogmatic conception—that the Spirit proceeds not solely from the Father (as the Greek Church maintains), but also from the Son—is entirely appropriate. In Greek dogma—as in the ethnic religions—everything proceeds solely from absolute substance. The Western churches, however—once the absolute thought of Christ had begotten a world-historical form—maintain, conversely and in accordance with the previously mentioned canon of self-causation, that the universal Spirit—as a Spirit conscious of itself—proceeds from Christ only through His apotheosis. For the Spirit exists only insofar as it knows itself to exist; thus, without the Son—as Jacob Böhme puts it—the Father would be merely a "dark valley," and consequently could not cause the Spirit to proceed from Himself alone.

If, then, it is self-evident that the conception of the Spirit must be understood not according to the flesh, but according to the Spirit, the same must necessarily hold true for the second element of our present inquiry. In His very finitude, Christ is presented as the concrete existence of absolute personality; the expunging of sinfulness within this finite person was therefore compelled—acting as an unconscious presupposition—to assume the specific form of that person’s very genesis: a genesis occurring even without sensual desire. In truth, however, Christ’s mother conceived the World-Spirit within her virginal consciousness; acting empirically as a mediating cause, she furthered this world-historical purpose by—with that earnest gaze so incomparably captured by Raphael in the *Sistine Madonna*—teaching her firstborn son from an early age to subordinate his own particular interests to the universal well-being of humanity, thereby becoming, in a spiritual sense, "born again." Even at the age of twelve, while teaching in the Temple, he asked: "Who are my brothers?" To him, it was all of humanity.

Would Christ possess two natures were the generation according to the flesh not added to the generation according to the spirit? Yet both natures constitute in him—just as they do in all of us—but a single Person; for this self-subsistence of the Infinite within the finite is precisely what constitutes the concept of the Person. The divine Idea in itself—without its realization within the human subject—is a mere abstraction of universality; just as human nature—without its elevation into the absolute Spirit—is merely the abstraction of limitation, negation, and sinfulness. It is this unity of opposites—this inseparable communion of natures, neither of which can be absent—that constitutes the eternal Personality of the Absolute.

Christ fulfilled a threefold office during his life. He is Prophet, because he—before all others—articulated that which serves as the rule of our faith. He is High Priest, because he leads the congregation by example, constantly offering up his own finitude as a sacrifice to the Absolute. He is King, because—just as in the State, where the expression of the general will must appear concentrated within a single individual—so too in religion is Christ the individual in whom all find the objective representation of their own unity with the Absolute; until, in the worship of the Spirit, the flower of eternal Personality—I was almost about to say "spontaneously"—blossoms within the breast of every single person. Then the Absolute is not merely *de se*—as Scotus Erigena puts it—"All in All," but is also acknowledged as such by all mankind. "Suffered under Pontius Pilate, was crucified, died, and was buried." Here, the purely factual reality of external history enters into the realm of the Spirit. Here, we are not speaking of knowledge, but in truth only of belief—insofar as there is absolutely no ground to cast doubt upon this fact (which is, moreover, confirmed by secular writers such as Tacitus), and upon which the Christian myth is founded.

In the context of religious faith, however, Christ’s death acquires a significance of a wholly different order: namely, that of an atonement for the sins of mankind—an atonement that satisfies the justice of God. This, of course, is not to be understood—in the true sense intended by the Church—as implying that a sinner can be justified without repentance and conversion. Nor does orthodox doctrine intend—or indeed *could* it intend—that an innocent party should atone for the sins of the guilty. Redemption from sin through Christ’s sacrifice can signify only this: that finite human nature, as such, is indeed insufficient to embody the perfect manifestation of the Absolute; rather, it is the *act* of the Absolute itself that shatters the negative element within the realm of the negative, thereby rendering the finite an adequate manifestation of the Infinite. For if the finite holds the Absolute as its innermost essence, then, for that very reason, the Absolute also encompasses the finite within itself—so that, within its "Other," and through the negation of that Other, it may truly behold itself. Yet, just as the universal Idea of ​​the Absolute was presented in Christ as an individual figure, so too must the "flesh"—which all human beings are called upon to crucify within themselves in order to rise again as spirits, justified before God—be viewed as an *individual* crucifixion of the flesh, enacted within that specific individual who serves, preeminently, as the representative of the epiphany of the eternal personality of the Spirit. Did not even certain Gnostics declare Christ to be Matter itself—crucified across all times and spaces? And just as, according to the Apostle, all human beings are justified in *one* man, so too have they sinned in *one* man. The general weakness of the flesh is presented as the transgression of an individual—a generality which, however, could subsequently be redeemed only under the guise of Original Sin. It goes without saying, however, that the Crucifixion—beneficial to all, whether as grace or as an objective act of the Absolute—is effective only when it coincides with the subjective crucifixion.

That the negative moment of the Spirit—as a principle universal to humanity—has also, from time to time, been linked to the doctrine of the Son is evidenced, for instance, by the heterodox teaching developed by Böhme: the doctrine of a firstborn Son of God—Lucifer—who fell from grace, and whose place was subsequently taken by Christ. A scriptural echo of this doctrine is, furthermore, the Temptation of Christ.

"He descended into hell; on the third day He rose again from the dead; He ascended into heaven; He sits at the right hand of God, the Father Almighty." Through death, the finite is negated, vanished, and has entered into the realm of the invisible (*eis Aidou*), as Plato puts it. As a *first* negation, this submersion of all that is finite into the Absolute would constitute mere paganism; for in such a view, substance subsists solely within its accidents. The true death, however—the death with which Christianity is concerned—is the death of death itself, the sting of which is sin. The finite vanishes only *qua* finite, so that in its very existence it may reveal nothing but the Infinite itself; thus, only the negative aspect within it is expunged. This negation of the negative is the restoration of the positive—the Absolute itself, breaking through within the finite; it is the apotheosis of the finite, which, acting as a purified vessel, allows one to gaze through itself into the very heart of the Divine. This—which in worship is intended to be encountered by all as their own inner experience—is presented in Christ as external history. This faith itself constitutes the universal and the enduring; yet the notion that a sensuous event should remain a sensuous event occurring at a specific moment in time, while simultaneously serving as a spiritual universal—this is the contradiction that the religious imagination which it harmlessly overlooks, and against which the orthodox side struggles in vain, unable to subdue it.

Christ’s spirit, therefore, does not ascend empirically to a sensible heaven, for it is not situated in space; nor does His body, for it is subject to the law of gravity. If Christ possessed merely a phantom body—as some would claim—then He is reduced to a mere apparition. Indeed, within the Copernican system, a sensible Ascension can no longer be coherently conceived at all; for "heaven" is situated just as much below as it is above, and whatever is "up" at this moment will soon be "down" again. Consequently, if it wishes to salvage the doctrine of the bodily Ascension, consistent Christianity would, in truth, have to compel all future Galileos to recant their Copernican heresies once more; and who knows whether, before long, they will not be accused of having delved too deeply into the natural sciences—specifically for the purpose of undermining the Church. Has not a "literary journal protected by high-ranking patrons" already declared that even the natural sciences must be grounded in revelation, because only revelation can resolve the question of human origins? Would our recent discourses on this point be able to withstand such censorship?

The "New Christianity" is content, in all instances, with the symbolic significance of dogmas. Thus, Christ has ascended into the heaven of the spiritual realm; his individual self has risen to become the universal self. In this way, the one-sided notion—that this specific individual, as a sensuous being, constitutes the Absolute—is corrected; rather, he is the Absolute only insofar as his particularity has been transcended—that is, in the Spirit. Hence, during his lifetime, Christ did not permit himself to be worshipped; indeed, as he himself states, he had to suffer death before he could enter into his glory. He sits at the right hand of God—but only for that mode of representation which, until now, was compelled to view the idea of ​​the eternal Incarnation (as the absolute personality of the Father) as something distinct from the manifest personality of the incarnate Logos—perceiving the latter merely as standing *beside* the former. This separation persists until, through the true significance of the doctrine of the Spirit, the historical Christ—now fused with the eternal Incarnation—dies and rises anew within every individual, just as is represented in the symbolism of the Lord's Supper. Thus, only *one* Person lives in heaven and on earth: the Holy Spirit of the Community—the Spirit in whom all human beings are *one* Holy Being—constituting the true and sole spiritual realm that exists, just as our Master declares.

"From thence he shall come to judge the living and the dead." As long as the imagination still clings to the person of Christ as a *temporal* incarnation—an event that occurred once, nearly two thousand years ago in Palestine, and has not taken place since—then Christ has not yet returned. Yet his return will not be accompanied by sensuous gestures, as Luke himself has already taught. Christ returns as the universal spirit when consciousness has grown strong and the realization has taken root among men that the true personality of man is the adequate mode of existence of the Absolute. He judges the dead; for the deeds of eternity that they performed are preserved in the muse of memory and, in the judgment of posterity—as the verdict of the universal world-spirit—lead an eternal life. But Christ also judges the living, for the universal spirit is the touchstone of all thought and action; and whoever is not in accord with it is thereby already judged, since the universal spirit then appears within him only as that which shatters the negative.

Thus, just as in our sacred traditions the beginning of history was conflated with the beginning of the world—and represented as a temporal act of creation—because, in truth, history began with a great natural catastrophe of the earth; so, conversely, the end of history is represented as the Last Judgment and therefore conflated with the destruction of the world. In this way—as we have seen—the prehistoric era and the post-historic era were bound to transform into the concept of a timeless eternity, whereas in truth, these boundary points of historical time are themselves still temporal processes. If, however, we examine more closely the era in which we live, the question arises: to what extent has its task—which we have already outlined—been resolved, or to what extent is it currently in the process of being resolved? As the third period of the middle historical epoch, it is charged with the task of resolving history, restoring heaven on earth, and establishing the Kingdom of the Spirit within a universal church for all eternity.

This task, however—we may say—was set only yesterday; and we still stand merely at the threshold of this epoch. The specific form of Christianity—in which we first see it emerge in history—must assimilate within itself the element of the ethnic religions in order to expand into a third world-form. In antiquity, the harmony of concept and reality remains the presupposed foundation of the entire worldview. In every single in the individual form of the universe, the Divine was immediately present. Thus, in the correct view of my noble friend, the Absolute manifested itself as feeling, intuition, and artistic genius—just as, even in Greece, religion and philosophy were still works of art. Christianity, having emerged from the consciousness of the Fall and therefore standing squarely within the realm of finitude, begins—by contrast—immediately with this inner rupture; man turns his reflective gaze inward, and the reconstruction of the lost harmony within the depths of the soul remains his primary endeavor. Thus, within Christianity, the inner life of the intellectual world develops ever more fully in opposition to the fallenness of external existence. After religion had fully pressed philosophy and art into its service, the intelligible world—since the Reformation—has been gradually elevated from the realm of representation into the reflective consciousness of a free philosophy. When, finally, religion shall be completely permeated by philosophy—so that it may emerge as that world-religion which I recently proclaimed—then, as my friend’s historiosophy states, nothing will remain for the history of the future but to unveil the wonders contained within the discovered Philosopher’s Stone, spanning from Aristotle to Hegel; that is, to give concrete reality to philosophical thought by cultivating all the conditions of human life into an adequate existence of Reason—whereby the unconscious, objective harmony of antiquity now springs forth from the conscious fullness of inner knowledge itself. In antiquity, the Absolute existed as a fact; in the intervening age, as faith and thought; now, humanity must develop it out of itself—through an organic process—as a concrete act.

Is the time not ripe for such a peaceful transformation? No great individualities rise up any longer within the human race—figures who, in a subconscious intimation of the world's ultimate purpose, yet swept away more or less by the sheer energy of their own personalities, would stray from that very goal and often lead humanity forward only through bloody detours. Napoleon appears to be the last of these heroes. Through the boundless assertion of his own forceful individuality, he compromised the cause of progress—to which he did, admittedly, pay homage—for decades, thereby providing a powerful reaction with the opportunity to incite the wrath of the nations against him. The human race has now come to realize that progress can be fostered only through the enduring influence of the universal spirit—a spirit that must gradually take hold of the consciousness of all mankind. Henceforth, we are to advance not through the blind arbitrament of the God of War, but through the calm, collective deliberation upon the common purpose to be attained. Only in society, and through association, are we strong. Those who have sought to impede the free development of the universal human spirit ever since the days of the Reformation—I refer here to the Order of the Jesuits—have long understood the power inherent in the principle of association. Let us heed their example, so that we may confront such soul-destroying tendencies using their very own weapons. Attacked in the very sphere of religion, the Germanic peoples are at last beginning to stir.

Will not the frenzied excesses of the Roman hierarchy—which, conscious of its impending demise, is now making its final, most desperate attempts at self-preservation—serve to open the eyes of even the Catholics themselves? Has the Holy Coat of Trier—much like Tetzel’s indulgences three hundred years ago—not already borne fruit of a kind entirely unforeseen by those who put it on display? Could it be that Rome’s Machiavellian maxim—to yield ground whenever the universal voice rises up in opposition—has, for once, escaped the notice of its own zealots? The most recent actions of the Catholic prelates would certainly seem to suggest as much. Will not *The Wandering Jew*—depicting with such mastery the priestly deceit and Jesuitical fraud in all its ugly nakedness—strike at the very root of this corruption and bring it down? Have not the open letter of a courageous priest to the Bishop of Trier—regarding the exhibition of the Holy Coat—and the *Confession of Faith of Schneidemühl* become the very foundation for a rejuvenation of the Catholic Church? Have not the new congregations—forming in rapid succession and loudly proclaiming their severance from the fetters of the Roman hierarchy as a fundamental principle—thereby made possible the emergence of a universal church? In the face of such facts, could the Protestants remain inactive? Have they not, through numerous associations and protests, already demonstrated their engagement in the great religious movement that is on the verge of breaking forth? Are we to allow a "little Pope" to rise up over our heads in the person of every village priest? Are we to tolerate private confession and the policing of church attendance? Our superiors are—as they themselves declare—on our side, and have finally condemned those "extreme tendencies." Will the free congregation not soon spread across the entire land? Can we—specifically as philosophers—accept the words of the symbolic books as a *regula fidei* (rule of faith)—especially if a specific meaning, binding for all time, is supposed to be attached to them once and for all? Have not even theologians themselves already recognized the interpretation of the venerable original text of Holy Scripture—an interpretation born of free scholarly conviction and blossoming within the spirit of progressive development—as the sole norm of faith?

Indeed, I believe I discern from the signs of the times that this elevation of religious concepts into the realm of rational thought—and the generation of a new concept arising from this rational understanding—is timely; nay, that dogma can be saved from the assailing doubts of the Enlightenment only in this very manner. Let us, therefore, not fear what our Master says regarding the servile transformation of dogmas: “Reason—if, indeed, it is truly Reason that lays claim to this name has asserted itself within positive religion to such an extent that Kant, in his attempt to imbue the positive form of religion with significance drawn from his own philosophy, failed not because the peculiar meaning of those forms was thereby altered, but rather because those very forms no longer seemed worthy of such honor. Let us recognize, instead—following Aristotle’s example—"what is venerable in the faith of our fathers"; yet let us also see—as he did—that we understand it only insofar as we have just interpreted it; "the rest has been added merely for the sake of easier persuasion." That is precisely the point. The masses do not easily rise to general concepts and eternal truths in their abstract, universal form. To be convinced, they desire to behold and feel—in an individual, concrete guise—that which, as a purely universal truth, is otherwise merely an object of thought. Therein lies the symbolic and mystical element—the persuasive power—of every religion; of every religion that has existed hitherto.

The "New Christianity"—as the dawning religion of reason—represents an attempt to cast off this mystical husk and to recast the rational content into a form of representation that is universally accessible. Alas! I fear that those who stand adamantly opposed to philosophers on account of their "mystical" mode of interpretation will, in their stubborn defense of the *form*, ultimately endanger the *genuine content* itself—insofar as it lies within their power to do so. Yet the spiritual, the triumphant Church is built upon a rock; and the gates of Hell—this mere edifice of formulas—will eventually shatter like an outer shell, bringing the genuine core of truth, unveiled, into the light of day. To contribute to this end through my words is, indeed, my wish and my purpose in delivering this address. But that which eluded Kant—because he sought to execute it from the standpoint of a limited, moralistic perspective—why should it elude *us*, standing as we do upon the vantage point of Absolute Knowledge? The time is ripe for religious inquiry.

Let us cast ourselves, all of us—my friends—step practically right into a vital question of reality, by expressing ourselves clearly and distinctly—without subterfuge or reservation—both to ourselves and to the public. In doing so, we introduce into actual life not merely the philosophers, but—to an equal degree—Christianity itself; for otherwise, amidst theological disputes over dogma, Christianity would degenerate into sterile hair-splitting, or—what is worse—would, in the hands of a political faction, appear as a hypocritical display of piety, serving merely as a cloak to pursue secular ends of domination—even over the royal authority itself—and to usher in a new spiritual servitude. Let us, therefore, confront that ancient, sanctimonious association with an entirely new alliance of science and thought—that very "community of philosophy" already advocated by our Master—especially now that, in every sphere of corporeal life, associations dedicated to the most diverse ends are everywhere springing forth from the consciousness of the people. No longer is a single individual elevated to the status of a divine personality; indeed, even the "cult of genius"—which the "Left Wing" proposed not long ago—has already been outstripped by the giant strides of history. And in this new Christianity—which, having become secularized, does not cease to be a religion, yet is no longer *merely* a religion, but seeks simultaneously to give birth to a new social life—the epiphany of the eternal personality of the Spirit is established by the fact that the universal Spirit reveals itself as the sole Person within the collective body of individuals. "The religion of the Word," as the aforementioned priest observes, "will thereby elevate itself to become the religion of the Deed."

That "world poetry" of which I spoke recently has already acknowledged this trajectory of European humanity; and, since our last gathering—ranging from *The Mysteries of Paris* to *The Wandering Jew*—it has taken a significant step forward. In the first novel, the Germanic Prince was, in a sense, the sole representative of the eternal personality of the Absolute, inasmuch as he cast himself in the role of the swift executor of God’s somewhat "long-suffering Providence." In *The Wandering Jew*, this vocation is entrusted to a society yet to be constituted—one rendered immeasurably wealthy through the long accumulation of an inherited capital—which is intended thereby to stand in opposition to the Jesuit Order. "The Society of Jesus," it is stated, "is one of the most brilliant examples of the omnipotence of association. If a corrupt association—founded upon human degradation, fear, and despotism, and pursued by the curse of nations—could endure for centuries and has often ruled the world through cunning and terror, what might not an association achieve that, proceeding from brotherhood and evangelical love, had as its purpose the liberation of man and woman from every degrading servitude? To invite those to happiness here on earth who have hitherto known only the pain and misery of life recognized? To honor and enrich the work that sustains us? To instruct those whom ignorance corrupts? To foster the free unfolding of all those faculties of the soul which the Deity has bestowed upon man as so many powerful levers?" and so forth.

Thus, the associations for the welfare of the working classes—which we have seen emerge in our midst through a magnanimous impulse—are, more than they might appear, an aspect of the epiphany of the eternal personality of the Spirit, whose immanent presence humanity is beginning to sense within itself. The impulse toward association for the benefit of the lower classes of society flows from a reverence for man *as man*—even in the lowliest of proletarians—as an image of the Deity. The proposition—advanced by our Master—that man *as such* constitutes God’s ultimate purpose within Christianity ceases to be a mere figure of speech the moment it is no longer a single individual who serves as the privileged representative of the divine personality on earth, but rather *all* who work with united forces to fan into a blazing flame—even within the poorest and lowliest among us—that spark of divine likeness which lies hidden beneath the ashes of life’s material hardship; only in this way does the equality of all human beings before God become a reality. In Jesuitism, however, the individual is—God knows—not an end in himself, but rather (as must never be the case) a mere means for other human beings, a blind instrument that, in passive obedience, is expected to be "like a corpse" (*tanquam cadaver*), as their writers put it. 

Should we not also attribute our own society to that very impulse toward association? Let us embrace one another through that 'intellectual love of God'—already called for by Spinoza—through which alone we can advance our common purpose. I, for one, feel within myself that the more the universal spirit gains strength, the weaker the individual becomes when standing alone. Through the association of authorship—which was one of the very purposes of our coming together—the individual is uplifted by the collective. Accept, therefore, what I have here presented regarding Christology—conceived as the theory of the empirically given epiphany of the eternal personality of the Spirit—merely as a highly imperfect beginning: one certainly still capable of much further development and refinement, and one to which only the vital breath of dialogue—breathed into it through your observations and rejoinders—can lend true substance. I am convinced that it is only through your corrections and constructive discussions that the truth will emerge—for me no less than for all of us—from the living struggle of divergent perspectives. 'For,' as Aristotle says, 'the truth is at once difficult and easy to find: difficult, because no single individual can grasp it alone; easy, because it cannot elude the collective grasp of all.'

As a seal upon our unbreakable covenant—should it, indeed, still stand in need of one—I therefore call upon you not to withhold your judgment from me regarding this address as well. Here, too, the *cause* itself is my primary—or rather, my sole—concern; I sacrifice my own personality entirely to it. That cause, however, appears to me to be this: to take possession of that eternal personality of the Spirit—which, amidst all finite individuality, remains the sole enduring and valid reality—and to recognize as our own true Self that which the early community, in the person of Christ, was compelled to conceive of as an external subject. As yet, no literary fruit has emerged from the very bosom of our society—with the unanimous assent of all—to grace our union. We would thereby establish an entirely new kind of literature—not like that found in ordinary journals of every variety, where various writers merely publish their treatises collectively; but rather, in accordance with the statutes of our society, the thoughts of diverse writers—harmoniously weighed and integrated by the authors themselves—would, through the editing and publication of our proceedings, coalesce into an artistic whole.
