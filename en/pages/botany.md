# Botany

Note: This webpage is a revised translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Encyclopedia Nature* (1876) 

The plant is the exact opposite of the universal organism of the Earth, yet it has resulted from the Earth’s own inherent polarity—as much as the Earth itself is already everywhere advancing toward individual vitality. In the place of that dim, inward nature of universal life—which is merely the *potential* for life—we have now arrived at the other extreme: the differentiation of life as an *actual* reality.

1. From this follows, firstly, the concept of the *vegetative organism*—an organism that still lacks the comprehensive universality of life, that neutral unity of internal organization that only the animal possesses. The plant merely unfolds itself into an infinite multiplicity of living entities, and if it is argued that the plant cannot truly *lack* the unity of life, since it would otherwise be devoid of life itself, then at the very least, this unity has not yet become *objective* to the plant as a distinct, specific form existing *within* the life of the plant itself. Thus, this unity has not yet differentiated itself from its particular manifestations, but each of these manifestations constitutes this unity of life *for-itself*—each one being a new individual. This constitutes the concept of *anaphytosis*, wherein every part is potentially the entire plant. The plant transforms itself into a multiplicity of plants—each of which is capable, once detached from its stem, of existing as an independent entity. This is not the case with the animal—with the possible exception of the polyp, whose severed fragments continue to grow indefinitely. Goethe is therefore correct in observing that the more complex an organism is, the more dissimilar its constituent parts appear. However, since in the plant every part is equal to every other part—as much as each part *is* the whole—these parts remain, by that very reason, external to one another. 

    Schultz-Schultzen describes this external nature very well when he states: “We observe two major purposes of life in the morphology of the plant—the union of the *Anaphyta* (as primordial individuals) within the generational series into a corporate organic unity, and yet, simultaneously, the distinct separation and divergence of the individual entities—each free on all sides—fulfilled in an admirable manner through the process of branching; this branching constitutes the relationship between stem and branch, and serves to replace the central organs that are otherwise lacking in the plant.” Accordingly, in contrast to the self-contained interiority of geological life, the plant is the perpetual externalization—the *coming-out-of-itself*—of its own life. Whereas the limbs of an animal each possess a specific function designed to harmonize with the organism as a whole, every single part of the plant serves as the seat of the entire vital function. Thus, the subject of life is has merely diffused into—or dissolved within—the vital process itself, and as a result the plant still lacks the true subjectivity of life. In other words, since this unity does not yet constitute the plant’s own inner self, it remains external to the plant—just as each individual part remains external to every other part. In the geological organism, the process of life is *externally* to its being as a universal subject. Conversely, in the plant, this self-referential subjectivity is *external* to the process of life. This "subject" of the plant is light. For this reason, Schelling was right to assert that, were the plant capable of consciousness, it would worship light as its God.

    However, since the plant’s striving toward this subject is a constant transcending of itself, it never reaches a conclusion in its growth, continually generating new accretions of serial members. This branching constitutes growth—the form in which all its processes manifest. The animal certainly grows, but it does so as a complete individual, through the enlargement of its internal and external organs. Moreover, animals reach an age at which they stop growing, and this growth never entails the generation of new individuals or members—even if some animals are capable of regenerating lost limbs. The internal organs of the plant do not continue to grow, it rejuvenates itself solely through its external parts. This branching is an organic process of generation inherent in all parts of the plant, constituting the plant’s true morphological universal—a universal that is neither the leaf, nor the axis, nor—as Aristotle would have it—the seed.

    The immediate consequence of this incapacity on the part of the plant—this inability to constitute itself as the subject of its own parts—is that the plant exists merely as an aggregate of individuals, or alternatively, that it serves as the bare soil upon which a multitude of individuals grow, much in the same way that the Earth itself serves this function. The younger shoots emerge as branches from the older ones. Yet the soil upon which new individuals sprout year after year is, in fact, the wood—the plant’s own defunct organism. According to Schultz-Schultzenstein, the wood—the tree itself—stands as a *family tree in natura*, wherein grandchildren and great-grandchildren dwell side by side for hundreds of years, forming a single, unified community. Yet these children of the plant need not remain within the paternal home at all, since every part—every cutting or layer—when inserted into the soil, can become a new plant. Thus, strawberries propagate themselves by means of runners (*stolones*) springing from the root, taking root in the soil and forming a new plant. In this way, a single tree growing on the humid shores within the tropics of Asia, Africa, or the Americas can give rise to an entire forest spanning miles. Finally, because the plant serves as the foundation for new plants—its own offspring—it can also take in other foreign plants onto its trunk. This is the art of grafting. Thus, for instance, ten different varieties of pear can grow upon one and the same trunk.

2. From the concept of the vegetative organism, there follows, secondly—as necessary corollaries—several important determinations that serve as the modes of the reality of plant life.

    a. The plant possesses no sensation because its individuality is not a unified subjectivity—one that takes as its object that very principle of unity which relates all the plant's states back to itself. The phenomena analogous to sensation—which occur in certain Mimosa species, such as *Mimosa pudica*, and in other plants—are simply effects of mechanical elasticity, not of organic irritability. Link explicitly attributes the apparent irritability of the plant to a physical vibration or shock, whether produced by the wind or by other external forces. Müller states it very aptly: "One must not confuse irritability with sensitivity: for instance, muscles—even when severed from the body—remain irritable, but they are not sensitive." 
    
    b. Because the plant’s subjectivity is not its own—but possesses it *outside* of itself—it is bound to this very state of being-outside-itself. In other words, the plant does not possess the power of independent locomotion. The plant remains bound to space, for space—in the absence of non-sensuous unity—constitutes its sensuous unity. Although plants are swayed to and fro by the wind, it remains firmly rooted—by its base—within the universal individual of the Earth. With its head, the plant is drawn toward the light. Hence, plants confined to a dark cellar will laboriously stretch and twist their way toward the cellar’s ​​single aperture of light. In the morning, all the flowers in a meadow turn toward the east, and in the evening, toward the west—a behavior most notably exhibited by the sunflower, yet one inherent to all flowers, for they are essentially all heliotropes. The movement of the plant is therefore neither arbitrary nor free, for it does not have mastery over the indifferent externality of space. However, neither does the plant possess the necessary, law-governed motion characteristic of celestial bodies, instead being subject only to the external, contingent motion of local mechanics.

    c. Since plants lack this ideality of material separation, they also do not generate any internal heat from within themselves. They are approximately as warm as the heat of their surroundings, and even if the temperature within the plant’s interior is somewhat higher, this is only due to the fact that wood is a good insulator of heat and the trunk conducts the internal heat of the earth instead. Although Treviranus and Link claim to have observed an elevated temperature lasting for a few hours in certain plants during the fertilization period, they nevertheless attribute this phenomenon solely to chemical decomposition processes—just as the movements of the plant's reproductive organs relative to one another must be interpreted as growth, not as a voluntary determined movement.

    d. Finally, since the plant cannot objectify its Self apart from its individual functions, but has its Self entirely diffused throughout its activities, it is unable to interrupt these activities and is constant conflict with them. While the renewal of the blood and respiration do not cease in the animal either, the animal is nevertheless able—to a certain extent—to temporarily disengage itself from its relationship with the external world. Plants cannot do this since they possesses no intermittent power of intussusception. The plant is always dependent upon external means for its preservation, and these flow toward it in a continuous stream. As Hegel notes, unlike the animal, a plant cannot simply take a sip of water, rather the air and water act upon the plant unceasingly, and the influence of light is interrupted only externally, by night or by winter. Owing to the external nature of this influence, it is also susceptible to alteration. By keeping lamps burning, Decandolle succeeded—after just a few nights—in causing several plants to shift their sleeping cycles. The "Queen of the Night," however, remains steadfast in its habit of unfolding its brief blossom only during those hours when it is daytime in its native habitat. Conversely, plants from more northerly regions alter their winter dormancy when transported to southern climates. The honeysuckle possesses the peculiar characteristic of opening at night and then diffusing its fragrance—a phenomenon that certainly cannot be attributed to any form of spontaneity.

3. The distinct determinations of the plant—which we have identified as the moments of its reality—all demonstrate that the plant is incapable of attaining *real* distinctions. The plant cannot differentiate itself into a dominant *One* that idealizes the material multiplicity of its members, rather every moment of its reality is itself a repetition of that *One*. Goethe very correctly grasped this formal, abstractly identical standpoint of the plant as its *metamorphosis*, and this constitutes the third point we must address in our general description of plant life—the *concept* of the plant realizing itself within the plant's own *reality*.

    Since each of the distinct parts of the plant is essentially nothing other than the whole plant itself, it is simply the identical *One* that transforms into various parts—parts whose distinction is only formal and not substantial. If Schultz-Schultzenstein disparages the theory of metamorphosis on the grounds that it fails to yield *real* distinctions, we must counter by observing that the plant at most makes a tentative start toward unfolding such real distinctions from within itself. Furthermore, if Schultz-Schultzenstein prefers *anaphytosis* to *metamorphosis* as the true theory, the proper rejoinder is that the concepts denoted by these two terms do not that far apart. Schultz-Schultzenstein does indeed maintain that anaphytosis signifies the *generation* of new parts, whereas metamorphosis signifies the *reformation* of parts that already exist. However, this notion of "pre-existence" can refer only to the *archetype* of the part, not to the *real* part currently being formed. For every metamorphosis is, by its very nature, also a generation of new parts, and is therefore anaphytosis. However, the concept of metamorphosis encompasses more than that of anaphytosis. The latter is the abstract repetition of identical parts—a notion already implicit in the preposition *ana*. The preposition *meta* in *metamorphosis*, by contrast, points to a transformation and differentiation in which the archetype is always preserved. Thus, it is not Goethe, but rather Schultz-Schultzenstein, who here falls prey to the error of formal identity. Goethe has already paved the way for his successor—should the latter subsequently seek to discover genuine distinctions within plant structure.

    The concept of metamorphosis is explained as follows: whereas the geological organism lacked a real unity amidst the dispersal of its members, in the plant, conversely, its archetype constitutes its sole reality. However, Goethe identified this archetype as the leaf, conceiving of everything about the plant as a metamorphosis of the leaf. With this, the foundation was laid for a rational examination of plant life. The leaf thus forms the center of the whole, its various parts are simply the enfoldings, expansions, and contractions of the leaf itself. Thus, all other parts of the plant represent either imperfect approximations toward it—as they expand outward from their enfolding sheaths—or further involutions of it, as it condenses and contracts. The germ, stem, bud, blossom, calyx—even the roots and the wood—all possess the form of the leaf. One need not, therefore, conceive of the leaf as something simple, Schultz-Schultzenstein, in fact, describes it as highly complex.This is quite natural since—once again—the leaf is the whole in miniature: a complete tree, with the midrib serving as the trunk, the lateral veins as branches, the intervening tissue as foliage, and so forth. Above all, this serves as the strongest proof of the formal identity shared by all the parts. As a result, any one part is capable of taking the place of any other part. 
    
    But even if everything consists solely of anaphytosis in the plant, it nonetheless must exhibit within itself the three processes of the concept of life as a totality, although not yet in as fully developed a form as in the animal. The life of the plant appears more under the guise of growth—out of which the real distinctions that characterize animal already seeks to struggle forth and arise with varying degrees of discernibility. Just as the product remains largely confined within a merely formal unity, so too do the processes. In this manner, the inner life process of the plant—viewed as a process of formation—firstly reveals only those moments and stages of its metamorphosis that are now to be specified in greater detail: its ceaseless growth. The second aspect constitutes the plant life proper or its nutrition—its self-externalization as a mode of relation to external potencies—which still remains itself a form of growth, until finally—in the case of many plants—it culminates in something analogous to the genus process.

## Growth

Since we have already stated that within the plant, all processes constitute one and the same process of growth, and that morphological formation is the perpetual generation of new individuals, it follows that this is also a genus process. However, to the extent that it is a constant process of externalization, morphological formation is also assimilation. If, however, we choose for the moment to view all of this solely as the *internal* morphological formation of the plant, the question arises: what, *a priori*, must be the elements of this form that we observe in experience? Since the plant remains bound to space and is incapable of detaching itself from it, it is evident from this alone that it cannot liberate itself from the formative elements of space. Given that the plant occupies an intermediate position between the geological organism and the animal organism, it necessarily bears an intermediate relationship to these spatial configurations, relating to them neither as something completely unfree—like the Earth—nor as something completely free—like the animal. In the case of the Earth, the point, the plane, and the crystal appear as entirely autonomous formations, existing externally to one another. In the case of the animal, however, all these spatial forms are not limited to the self-production of the organism, they also coalesce to constitute a unified form. As the point develops into a line, and the line into a plane—and as that plane subsequently curves—the point establishes itself as the center of an ovoid situated within an ellipsoid, much like the celestial bodies. Thus, while these forms may indeed harbor within themselves the latent shapes of the animal, they do so only as a *presupposed* potential—not yet as a form that is perpetually generating itself *out of its own being*. In the plant, it is true that one form is indeed produced from another, but they appear only side by side in space and successively in time—and in such a way that the form which initiated the process—the point, having transformed itself into line, plane, and crystal—ultimately returns once more to the point, whereas in the animal, the total form—as the ideal unity of all spatial configurations—is that which is perpetually self-preserving.

If we now consult experience, we observe that the form of the plant undergoes three metamorphoses. The simple point expands from within itself into a line, then the line becomes a plane, and the plane finally contracts into the enclosed surface. This generates the three processes: the germination of the plant within itself, its outward differentiation in the leaf, and its return to itself in the bud.

### Germination

The germ is the point—the primary element—from which the entire plant takes shape and unfolds into ever-new individuals, as it moves outward toward the other formative elements of space.

The germ, as a material point, is simultaneously a cell—the *primordial* cell. Müller states that the cell—consisting of a nucleus and a membrane—constitutes the primordial organic matter of plants and animals, of which all their constituent parts are composed. At the same time, he notes, it is *potentially* the entire individual, which subsequently develops in *actuality* with all its integral parts out of this very cell. It was Schleiden and Schwann, he observes, who—through their observations—led the latter to the discovery of cell formation as the common developmental principle for the most diverse elementary parts of organisms. In every tissue, new cells form only where fresh nutritive matter is present. From this sprang Virchow’s organic atomic theory and his cellular pathology—a theory which, however, according to Dr. Diesterweg,  is not intended to exclude "the unity of the human being". This "unity of the organism" is to be demonstrated through the mutual dependence of the individual cell groups upon one another.

If we now trace the formative process of the plant further, starting from the initial germ, a line first arises from the cellular point, as a single point transforms into many points—into many individuals. For cells develop from cells, and between the old ones (mother cells), new ones (daughter cells) are constantly intercalating themselves, such that the primordial cell elongates into threads. There are plants that consist of nothing more than this. This longitudinal extension eventually ceases, and at the resulting extremity, a new point forms—variously termed a germ-grain, node, or spore. From this new point, a new thread emerges, and in this manner, point and line alternate with one another. Hegel states: “This plant type manifests itself most immediately in the example of the *Confervae*—which are otherwise nothing other than such green threads, devoid of any further structural differentiation—representing the very beginnings of vegetation in water.” In these nodes, Treviranus claims to discern the first signs of a reproductive process, and indeed, we witness right before our eyes the generation of new individuals. However, they do not originate within a flower, typically  without any preceding, distinct act of fertilization. Hence, these plants are also designated as flowerless plants, or cryptogams. As a result, the simplest of cryptogams are referred to as cellular plants.

To the extent that the form of the plant is metamorphosed from a point into a line through the successive arrangement of cell upon cell, therein lies the transition from unity into duality. The generated line possesses two ends, which may be regarded as opposing poles. The concept of polarization is pertinent here, for the plant—this tracing of a line—constitutes a progressively generative morphological activity that does not merely remain within the realm of ideal movements, but gives rise to concrete forms. Having first observed the germ transform into a simple line, we now see it proceed, in its further unfolding, toward two lines. This constitutes, secondly, the internal differentiation (*diremtion*) of the germ itself, expanding in two distinct directions within space: a doubling of that very process of externalization to which the plant is subject. One direction is toward the earth, wherein the plant finds its anchorage, and the other is toward the sun, wherein it seeks its true self. On the other hand, however, this double line also transitions into the opposition of line and plane. For when—in the case of more developed plants—the germ is placed in the earth, it germinates there under the influence of moisture and warmth, bursting open and differentiating itself into these two spatial configurations. "The germ," observes Willdenow, "consists of the radicle (*rostellum*) and the plumule (*plumula*). From the former arises the root, and from the latter, that portion of the plant situated above the ground." In certain plants, the radicle is absent, and in such cases, the plumule performs both functions single-handedly. Thus, it is demonstrated once again that every individual part is capable of being the whole.

Now if the root is the line directed downward, and the cotyledon the surface struggling upward into the light, then within the germ, this directionality of line and surface is thirdly superseded to the point where the surface encloses itself into an outer surface. This is the style or stem (*caulis*), which grows forth from the germ, and within this initial configuration of the plant, already attains the totality of the third dimension. To the extent that the germ has thereby become a totality within the stem, the cell has grown into a cellular tissue—the further connections of which are the vascular bundles—which are found in vascular plants, i.e. in the higher cryptogams (such as ferns) and in phanerogams. This cellular tissue constitutes the body of the stem, and as we have seen, the constant generation of new cells causes the stem to grow outward and strengthen ever more, until it becomes a trunk.

However, because the totality of the plant is already contained *per se* within the stem, it does not stop at this initial state of inwardness characteristic of cell formation. Instead, by utilizing these cells, it subsequently turns outward, generating from them precisely the tissues and vessels by means of which it establishes a connection with external potencies and assimilates them into itself. These constitute the organs of assimilation—the spiral vessels in which the fibers of the plant round themselves into a surface. 

Having thus been rendered capable of drawing nourishment from the external world, the plant inwardly turns back upon itself to form the organs that process the substances it has absorbed and utilize them for the completion of its internal structural development. The organs of this germination process are those vessels which Schultz-Schultzenstein designated as "vital vessels", the specific function of which we shall have occasion to describe in our account of the plant’s second process. These are identical to what—in an earlier exposition by Link—were termed scalariform, pitted, and dotted vessels, which he posited as originating from the spiral vessels: “The spiral vessels transform into scalariform vessels as the coils of the spirals—taken in pairs—fuse together. Through the growth and expansion of adjacent parts, the spiral vessels are subjected to tension or compression; this action gives rise to the wave-like undulations of the transverse markings, as well as to the apparent bifurcations of the cross-lines—resulting from two coils having been displaced one atop the other—and perhaps, in some instances, to actual fissures.” Schultz-Schultzenstein, however, adamantly rejects the notion that these vital vessels originated from fused cells, arguing that cells are rigid structures, whereas the vital vessels are characterized by the properties of organic contractility and irritability. Seubert simply enumerates four distinct types—spiral vessels, annular and reticulate vessels, scalariform vessels, and pitted vessels.

### Vernation

If the germ has revealed itself as the plant's being-within-itself—notwithstanding that it also unfolded itself into all the formative elements of space—then secondly, we must consider its external differentiation as such: the plant's actual being-outside-of-itself. Here the starting point is not the point representing the being-within-itself of space, but the line—the opposition already established within the point. Specifically, this line was a dual one—extending downward and upward—manifesting as root and stem. Since we already know that the stem and root are mutually interchangeable, the root participates in all the functions of the stem, and whatever we shall say regarding the latter applies to the former. The line of the stem—which here constitutes the fundamental determination—now successively posits all the dimensions of space as the germ had done, except it now does so in the mode of externality, projecting them forth from itself.

First, the vertical line of the stem transforms itself back into a point—positing itself as a germ that has been turned outward. Specifically, as the seed-grain—which is no longer deposited *into* the earth, the stem itself has now *become* the basis from which new individuals sprout forth. From its points, ever-new lateral lines are continually formed. From the points or nodes of attachment, branches and twigs arise. From the bud (or *gemma*)—within which the as-yet-unfolded leaf is enclosed—there first sprouts the leaf-stalk, followed by further lateral lines which—within open planes—form the angles that were previously mere mathematical configurations. However, as these open surfaces continually generate ever-finer new lines within themselves until the empty space is filled, and the diverging lines converge once again, they coalesce into solid surfaces, thereby giving rise to that real part of the plant that we call the leaf.

With the leaf, we have secondly arrived at the true focal point of the plant’s form, at the innermost turning point of its entire life. The leaf is the most definitive organ of the plant’s external manifestation, and it therefore embodies the life of the plant as such. For this reason, Goethe rightly regarded the leaf—in a certain sense—as the primal phenomenon of the vegetative organism, because within it, the growth of the entire plant is recapitulated. For along the midrib—which is to be viewed as a continuation of the stem—new branches and twigs are generated, multiplying *ad infinitum* until they merge with one another through the very tissue of the leaf. As much as the root—by likewise ramifying and branching out—undergoes precisely the same process as the stem, its sole distinction from the leaf lies in the fact that it lacks the tissue which transforms the ever-finer and ever-converging fibers into a continuous surface. The leaf possesses line, surface, and enclosing exterior—all in the form of a plane that rounds itself off into a self-contained whole. With the leaf, the plant has attained its inner completion, and everything that follows is merely an ever-increasing condensation and materialization of its physical form, as the leaf surfaces progressively contract into rounded structures.

This omnipotence of the leaf—by virtue of which it assumes all the functions of the plant (even that of reproduction, as observed in the case of the linden leaf)—also enables it to take the place of any other part of the plant. Thus, if we have found the stem to be interposed as the intermediate link between the root and the leaf, it is nonetheless dispensable and is absent in many plants. Indeed, that which first sprouts from the embryo for its further development is a leaf—the cotyledon, or seed-leaf. Hence, it may happen in the development of a plant that leaves result directly from the embryo, without the mediation of a stem. This constitutes the great generic distinction in plant development that Jussieu discovered—though Linnaeus was as yet unaware of it—upon which the subdivision of plants into their species and families must be based. For the fundamental distinction between plants lies in whether no cotyledons, or one, or two—or indeed even several—result from the embryo. This forms the primary classification of plants into Acotyledons, Monocotyledons, and Dicotyledons—the contrast between the simpler and the more complex plants.

The Acotyledons lack flowers, and even leaves in some cases. The Monocotyledons never progress to forming a true stem or proper trunk, remaining in the form of a leaf, but for this very reason, the leaf itself never matures into a flat lamina, but rounds itself into a stem-like structure, thereby taking the stem's place. The Monocotyles were most widespread in the primeval world because it still contained the land in its unbroken unity. Grasses, ferns, and palms belong to this group. Palms do have a trunk, but it consists only of leaves that continually grow anew from the base, rounding off as they go. Palms have no twigs or branches because they are absorbed by the large leaves. Palms have only crowns, which spread out immediately into buds and flowering branches. Just as palms are only leaves without stems, so too are there cacti that are only stems without leaves, where stem branches out from stem. Dicotyledons represent the totality where all the structural elements of space and all the forms of the plant fully develop.

If Jussieu, through his great discovery, provided the foundation for a natural system of plants, then the system established by Linnaeus was called artificial, since he only annotated a mechanical-mathematical principle of classification, namely the arithmetic differences of the various numbers that appear in the individual parts of the flower. On the one hand, it can be argued in defense of Linnaeus that the plant already exhibits a mechanical aspect of its form quite naturally, even in its spatial configurations. Linnaeus's error would then be only this one-sided characterization. However, Seubert also objects to such an arithmetic principle of classification, arguing that the numeric ratios to which such great importance is attached in this arrangement are not infrequently fluctuating, so that species of the same genus and individuals of the same species, indeed even different flowers of one and the same plant, show differences in this respect.

Schultz-Schultzenstein, who on the one hand claims to follow Jussieu's natural system, and in contrast to Linnaeus, wants to establish an organic, living principle of classification, then sees this principle more precisely in the three different growth types from which he wants to derive the classification of plants into genera and species. Within each genera's specific physiological class, the different growth types are supposed to repeat themselves, thus, in a sense, the subdivisions of the to form main classes. Since branching constitutes for him "the essential and dominant element", the growth types present themselves as various modes of branching. In *columnar growth*, the central shoot undergoes the greatest development, whereas in *wall growth* and *terminal growth*, the lagging central shoot is overgrown by the lateral shoots. Here, Schultz-Schultzenstein once again encounters the same dialectic into which he had also fallen during his polemic against Goethe: namely that of contesting the axial theory—formulated by Turpin and Decandolle—at the very moment he adopts it. While it may be true that not all flowers—but only the *columnar flowers*—possess axes, the contrast posited by the axial theory—between a vertical axis and horizontally and circularly arranged appendages—is precisely the same thing that Schultz-Schultzenstein terms the central shoot and the lateral shoots. Even his polemic against Aristotle is ill-advised, since the latter appears to have actually anticipated Jussieu’s classification when he states: "The forms of plants are based upon the nature and quantity of the seed"—namely, the cotyledons. In Schultz-Schultzenstein’s *terminal growth*—as the lowest form, such as that of the grasses—figuration of the *line* can be discerned, in the intermediate form—*wall growth*, *forked growth*, or *vine growth*—that of the *planar* quality characteristic of climbing plants, and in the highest form of growth—the *columnar flowers*—that of perfected *corporeality*.

Finally, Seubert aims to have combined all the natural systems of the plant kingdom established by various botanists into a single system, thereby summarizing the results obtained to date. However, his basis for classification lies primarily in the greater or lesser degree of floral development. Thus, he posits six classes: 

A. Flowerless plants, or Cryptogams: 

Class I, Thallophytes (Thallophyta) 

Class II, Leafy Cryptogams (Cryptogamae foliosae)

B. Flowering plants, or Phanerogams, which are either Monocotyledons (Class III) or Dicotyledons—the latter comprising three subdivisions: 

Class IV, Apetalous plants (with a floral envelope) 

Class V, Monopetalous plants (with a fused corolla)

Class VI, Polypetalous plants (with separate petals)

Each class subdivides into numerous families, which—both within their respective classes and within the system as a whole—are distinguished from one another by an ever-increasing degree of structural complexity. In the first class, the progression ascends from the family of Algae to the family of Fungi, in the second, from the Mosses to the family of Root Ferns, and in the third, from the Grasses—via the Palms and Lilies—to the Najads. In the fourth class, we proceed from the conifers, through the plane trees, willows, birches, oaks, and all the way up to the aristolochias. The fifth class seems to provide a quite clear example of just how one-sided it is to make the development of the flower—rather than the stem and the entire branching structure of the plant—the sole basis for classification. In the sixth class appear lindens, flax, mallows, maples, pumpkins, and myrtles, and the conclusion is formed by apples, roses, hard-fruited plants, and legumes.

To the extent that the plant has posited itself—within the leaf—as this totality, such that its being-turned-outward constitutes its entire inner nature, it must now posit this being-outside-of-itself as its own moment as well. Thus, this state of *otherness*—from which ever-new individuals arise—is no longer the Earth (conceived as a soil that is alien to the plant), but is itself a product of the plant. From its external differentiation, the plant returns so profoundly into itself that it divides itself into its own living individuality and into the dead soil—conceived as the *Other*—from which that individuality sprouts. The plant generates such soil for itself through the process of lignification. The wood is the residue of this process—in a sense, the plant’s skeletal framework (which is internal rather than external)—that falls outside the scope of its vital processes, just as, in the case of the geological organism, we distinguished between the process and its residue. However, just as that residue was not entirely devoid of life, so too is the wood in this instance not entirely bereft of vital activity.

Even the wood grows—like the plant in general— through *anaphytosis*: it builds itself up layer upon layer as nodes and annual rings form, from which subsequent layers and branches are produced. These annual rings, as Link observes, appear suddenly around the very time when the buds for the following year also make their appearance. The new individual is present in its finished form immediately, rather than having developed gradually. Monocotyledons—even if one chooses to attribute the quality of "wood" to them (such as in the trunk of a palm tree)—invariably lack these annual rings. As a result, the pith and the bark are not separated by them, as is the case with dicotyledons. In the latter, a new layer of wood is inserted annually between the bark and the existing wood. The continued growth of the wood is a process of hardening that can often proceed even to the point of petrification, and thus, for example, beech wood throws off sparks under the lathe, just like flint. Otherwise, however—as that which is dead is also combustible—wood is carbon. The more layers are superimposed, the more life dies out in the regions below. Hence, the older the tree becomes, the more it sheds its lower branches and sends forth branches and leaves only at its crown. However, by virtue of the universality of the leaf, it transcends even this opposition between the living and the dying plant. In the form of bark, the leaf also encompasses the wood within itself. The leaves fall away every year, but in the bark—whose leafy fibers are unmistakable—the leaf becomes enduring. Thus, within the bark, the leaf takes itself—that very process of the plant's essential 'externalization'—back into the plant once more, for the bark is that which encloses the wood. In the bark, the leaf has transformed itself into the total crystal of the plant's inner organization. This totality—arising as a newly self-organizing individual—is the bud.

### Budding

Thirdly, within the bud, the point of the germ, the line of the stalk, and the surface of the leaf have transformed themselves into the enclosed surface of the plant. Flower formation is the highest formative process of the plant—one that subsequently transitions into the reproductive process, just as the leaf-process transitions into assimilation—whereas germination is limited to the self-contained nature of the formative process as such. In the bud and its unfolding—the flower—all the formative processes we have hitherto observed are now repeated and united. The germ—acting as the stem of the flower, from which the remaining floral parts grow forth like branches—is the floral axis (torus, receptacle). In flower formation, therefore, the contrast between columnar growth and apical growth is also repeated, as much as the floral petals may be formed just as readily from lateral shoots as from central shoots. Here, where the petals are formed from successive segments of the central stem standing one above the other—the branches of each petal possesses their own distinct stem, or else the branches of several petals spring from a common stem.

In turn, every part of the bud is a complete plant—a condensed, coiled, and multiplied leaf—as evidenced by the fact that some of these parts fully retain the leaf-form. First, the bud opens and arranges its green leaves in a circle around a central point: the calyx. This encloses within itself a new ring of colored leaves: the corolla. From this arises the filaments, which are crowned by the anthers, and in the center stands the pistil, bearing the stigma and connected—by means of a tube, the style—to the ovary, wherein the ovules are formed, returning the end of the formative process to its beginning. That everything originates from the leaf form is further evidenced by the fact that, in the *Celtifoliae*, even the filaments themselves transform into leaves. With the formation of the bud, growth is held in abeyance, unless one regards the bud's internal development as a form of growth occurring within itself. The fruit may also manifest as a tube—the termination of the root—as is the case with the potato, even though the blossom naively continues to appear at the top of the stem. Simultaneously, with the unfolding of the bud, the plant's relationship to the external world—specifically toward the light—is re-established. Therefore, we are led to view the process of assimilation or nutrition as the second of the vegetative processes—the process into which the formation of the bud naturally transitions.

## Nutrition

The external objects that the plant requires for its nutrition are not yet individual formations—precisely because the plant itself is not yet an individual formation that is self-contained. For this reason, just as it lacks a discontinuous intussusception—through which it might exclude itself from other individuals—so too does it relate to the general forces of nature—light (and heat), air, and water—through uninterrupted activity in order to absorb them.

### Photosynthesis 

The most fundamental process for the plant is photosynthesis, for light is precisely the plant's own Self—albeit as something external—which it seeks to attain above all else. Just as, in the process of growth, the plant continually transforms itself into other Selves that remain external to it—producing a succession of distinct individuals that exist outside of itself—so too does it arrest this growth once it has finally attained this Self of its own within the flower (where the plant's relationship to light is most clearly revealed)—even if that Self remains essentially external to it. The conclusion of the formative process is, therefore, the inception of the assimilative process. Yet, the formative process of the plant has borne within itself its relationship to light not merely at the stage of flower formation, but from the very beginning, for the plant could not exist without this external Self of its own. Even the initial differentiation between root and stem constitutes the polarity of darkness and light. 

All the plant's striving and yearning is directed toward the light, and as the plant succeeds in this struggle to extricate itself from the dark womb of the earth and ascend toward the radiant light of the heavens, it thereby opens itself up to the realm of color. The roots of the plant remain white, and indeed, if the plant to remain perpetually in darkness, its entire structure would remain white. With the arising of the stem—and most notably in the leaf—the color green appears: the neutral totality of color, representing the identity of yellow and blue. For the leaf is, in essence, the neutral totality of the plant itself, as much as all other parts of the plant arise from it through a process of particularization. It is only within the flower—where the plant's specific nature finds its full expression—that the plant unfolds into distinct, specific colors. Here, green breaks apart into the polar opposition of blue and yellow, ultimately intensifying to its highest pitch in the color red. Spring flowers tend more toward yellow than toward that first, still entirely superficial dimming of the light, even depicting pure light as white. Autumn flowers, by contrast—such as asters—are predominantly blue, they belong to the already fading light of autumn, for the sun, by now losing its strength, can no longer bestow upon them any dazzling hues. In general, however, black is absent from the cheerful chromatic realm of plants.

Light grants plants not only color and form, but also life, vitality, and fragrance. In tropical lands, plants are distinguished not merely by their more brilliant colors, but also by their more intense scents. Thus, light also facilitates the plant's interaction with the air, thereby mediating its transition to it.

### Carbon Fixation

The subsequent process of nutrition directed outward is carbon fixation via constant intake of carbon dioxide and exhalation of oxygen. Since the leaf—as the central element of the plant's form—represents this outward relationship in its purest manifestation, the leaf constitutes the essential organ of this process. Willdenow describes the stomata, through which the leaves maintain this interaction with the air, though these fissures appear in other parts of the plant as well. The air—much like light—exerts its influence upon all parts of the plant, even though light finds its specific focal point in the flower. Similarly, all parts of the plant absorb water, even though the root serves as the specific organ for transpiration. The intake and exhalation of air manifests as a decomposition of that air, so the plant maintains a distinct relationship with different gases as a result. The leaves and green parts of the plant absorb—in contrast to animals—carbon dioxide and exhale oxygen. However, at night and in the shade—as well as during states of disease or wilting—they absorb a portion of the atmospheric oxygen and exhale carbon dioxide, though in lesser quantities than they absorb during the day. In any event, these are not only chemical processes, but complete transformations of the universal elements of nature into the specific individuality of the plant.

The air specialized within the plant and assimilated into its very individuality constitutes its scent. Since the plant exudes a fragrance, its activity of assimilating and internalizing the air is also an act of externalizing itself. This externalization even proceeds to the point of the plant's complete self-alienation. For in the very act of transforming the air into its own substance, the plant simultaneously imparts this substance of its own back into the air. The plant gives off its fragrance and evaporates into the air as it emits its scent, and in this manner, it gradually withers away as it does so. This positive process therefore passes immediately into the negative.

### Transpiration

Transpiration, or the process involving water, by contrast, is assimilation—a process that, emerging from the plant's relationship with the external world, has become a return of the plant into itself. It constitutes the internal digestion of those potencies absorbed from without, whereby assimilation is then transformed back into growth. It is water, above all, that enables plants to thrive—even though light and air remain highly essential conditions for this to occur. However, an excess of water can also prove detrimental, causing plants to rot, and moreover, the water requirements of different plant species vary considerably. In fact, water is such a fundamental requirement of the plant that, provided it has access to water, the plant even can dispense entirely with soil. Plants are even capable of sustaining themselves amidst pebbles or crushed glass, provided they are continuously supplied with moisture. Duhamel, for instance, successfully kept an oak tree alive for eight years using nothing but water. In higher plants, the transformation of this elemental water into organic water is effected through the circulation of sap—a phenomenon observed by Corti, Amici, and Schultz-Schultzenstein—which has frequently been likened to the circulation of blood in animals. This process unfolds through several stages—a graduated sequence of operations—through which the plant undergoes internal growth and maturation, ultimately attaining its completion in the flower: that final node which marks the point beyond which the plant can no longer extend itself.

The initial phase of this process involves the absorption of water—primarily through the roots, and specifically through the woody fibers (for the roots themselves are essentially woody fibers)—from which the spiral vessels subsequently draw it up. At this juncture, the water—now in the form of wood-sap—remains only minimally differentiated; this raw nutritive fluid—which Senbert terms "spring sap"—possesses merely a faintly sweetish taste. It is “the still imperfectly assimilated nourishment of the plant,” says Schultz-Schultzenstein, “which is only later more highly organized and transferred into the circulatory system.”

It is the spiral vessels that conduct this sap—as an ascending current—into all the plant's extremities, and once it has reached these parts—particularly the leaves—it is assimilated there within finer vessels, reorganized, and rendered consonant with the specific nature of the plant. This constitutes the vital sap, which is often milky in appearance and colored. While the nutritive sap, derived from external sources, manifests itself within the plant primarily during the spring and autumn—thereby giving rise to the midsummer and midwinter shoots—the internal circulation of the vital sap proceeds ceaselessly. It is a streaming movement of white, transparent globules—perpetually in a state of formation and dissolution—flowing from the center toward the periphery, and from the periphery back toward the center, and intertwined with this horizontal current is another flowing both upward and downward. These two opposing currents do not intermingle, even though they flow side by side within one and the same channel, undivided by any separating partition. This movement takes place in all the external parts of the plant—the roots, stem, leaves, flowers, and fruits—and, indeed, certain structures come into existence only through the very process itself. Once the vital sap has been conveyed into the blossom, it has reached the highest object of its nutritive function, and its activity now concentrates primarily upon the development of the flower and the fruit—a process that constitutes a form of growth as well. It is only when this second sap has thereby completed its circulatory cycle—and that portion of it which the fruit has not utilized has returned—that it converges in the zone between the wood and the bark, with the woody sap that has migrated to that same region. 

While this first sap moves in a manner entirely distinct from that of the circulatory system—to which its motion stands in polar opposition—it nonetheless ultimately forms a neutral product in conjunction with it: the cambium. This third sap does not move, rather it constitutes the delicate embryonic form of the entire plant. It generates the pith and the cells, and in nourishing the inner bark, it produces the annual growth ring. Thus, whatever is not utilized by the fruit is returned to the processes of growth and lignification. As much as the bud thereby embodies the culmination of both nutrition and morphogenesis, it represents, in its entirety, the vital process at its very apex—and that is the genus process.

## Reproduction

The notion that the flower and fruit are the result not only of growth and nutrition, but also of an actual process of sexual union, constitutes a major controversy in botany—one that is entirely understandable, given that no sexual organs—or parts analogous to them—are to be found within cryptogams. It is sometimes even said that these plants conceal such organs out of modesty, whereas the phanerogams, in their innocence, display them openly—even stretching them heavenward. But the claim that these parts are true sexual organs remains doubtful, if only for the fundamental reason that the plant as a whole possesses no distinct organs with distinct functions, but is only the metamorphosis—or *anaphytosis*—of one and the same organism. Even if we believe to observe traces of reproductive activity, it will ultimately become apparent that this process also resolves back into the fundamental processes of morphological formation and growth. Nevertheless, it cannot be denied that the flowering of the plant—much like the reproductive process in animals—constitutes its highest form of activity, an activity that comes to fruition only in a state of absolute health, and only when all the vital conditions for the plant’s existence—light, air, and water—are present in sufficient measure. Should this not be the case, development proceeds no further than the production of leaves, never reaching the stage of flowering. Initially, the leaf bud and the flower bud differ only in that the latter is more robust and substantial. However, should the influx of vital sap prove insufficient, that which aspired to become a flower bud remains arrested at the lower developmental stage of a mere leaf bud. 

The first aspect of the blossom is this: that as much as growth—conceived as a movement *outward*—is inhibited, it manifests instead as an *inward* swelling of the bud. This inhibition can also be induced artificially by cutting rings into the bark—for instance, in olive trees—a technique known as the "pomological magic ring." For when the sap is thus arrested in its flow and consequently diverted in greater abundance to other parts of the plant, its fertility is enhanced. However, if the rings are excessive—thereby causing a fatal loss of sap—the tree will perish. As much as the plant, in the act of blossoming, has encountered its own Self as something external, it has simultaneously—through this very act—constituted that Self as its own and has posited its Self *for* itself. This specific mode of relation—whereby the plant’s Self relates to itself as an "other"—is precisely its *genus process*. This also holds true regarding its organs, its temporal course, and its ultimate result—the three points that we shall now proceed to examine in turn.

### Pollination

The organs of the genus process are the moments of flowering described in the developmental process of budding. The anthers, which form the tips of the stamens, contain within their capsules the male pollen, while the stigma—borne by the pistil—is regarded as the female sexual organ upon which the pollen falls. However, this description of plant organs—borrowed from the animal organism—cannot entirely withstand comparison with it. In animals, the organs are present from the very beginning, and with the individual's maturation, the drive arises that draws one individual toward another in order to generate—through their union—a third individual, thereby reproducing the genus. Now, there are indeed plants in which the sexual organs are distributed across two separate individuals—the dioecious plants, such as palms, hemp, and hops—which are therefore regarded as the primary evidence for the existence of the generic process. Yet even here, the sexual distinction is secondary one, something that manifests in the plant as a new individual, such that such plants often transition from one sex to the other. This is even more emphatically the case with monoecious plants—such as melons, pumpkins, oaks, etc.—where the male and female sexual organs are combined within a single plant (hermaphroditic). Here, it is not two distinct individuals standing opposed to one another as independent selves of the same genus, but a self-differentiation of one and the same plant within itself. Naturally, the same applies to polygamous plants that bear flowers of separate sexes alongside hermaphroditic flowers. In every instance, the flower represents the production of new individuals—just as every single part of the flower already constitutes a multitude of individuals.

This notwithstanding, there is a persistent desire to interpret empirical observation—e.g. as has been observed in palm trees—that the female palm never bears ripe fruit without the pollination of its stigma by male pollen, from which one therefore infers a prior act of sexual union. But even if this is indeed the case, it neither proves to be a universal rule nor a matter of necessity. New individuals come into being even without fertilization, or in other words, the plant is in a state of perpetual fertilization, enabling it to constantly bring forth new individuals, even through the mere process of growth. Thus, sexual union in plants reveals itself to be a diversion—a luxury within the life of the plant—one that resolves itself back into the fundamental process of growth.

### Fertilization

This becomes all the more evident when we now examine more closely what actually transpires during the process of reproduction. The stages of fertilization are such that: first, the anthers burst open, and the pollen—which is usually contained therein loosely in small grains—lands upon the stigma, which retains it by means of its moisture the moment the pollen touches it with its resinous, oily substance. According to Amici and Brongniart, tubes grow out from these grains, pass through the stigma and the style canal to reach the ovule—located within the cavity of the ovary—in order to penetrate its micropyle. This is something that Seubert regards as an established fact, but the effect of this initial activity is simply that the pistil withers—which is the second, purely negative aspect of copulation. The third stage is the swelling and ripening of the ovary, but once again, this positive aspect of copulation has reverted to mere growth. Whereas in the animal, copulation represents the supreme blossoming of its life, in the plant, it signifies only its demise. Schelver identifies the oil acting upon the stigma—which he considers analogous to animal fat—as the lethal force within the plant, even going so far as to term this influence a "poisoning" of the plant. However, this withering of the pistil can also occur independently of fertilization—as was observed when Spallanzani severed the male flowers in monoecious plants, e.g. from melons—and yet obtained ripe, viable seeds from the unpollinated fruits. Schelver, however, demonstrated that even on the cultivated fig tree, the fruit can ripen without fertilization—namely, through the puncture of an insect. He cites Julius Pontedera, noting that the latter termed this puncture a "beneficial bite" (*morsus meliens*), and the entire process has come to be known as *caprification* because that specific insect resides solely on the wild fig tree (*caprificus*)—which is therefore planted in the vicinity of the cultivated variety (*ficus urbana*) to facilitate the ripening puncture. 

### Ripening

Since the result of the genus process is the ripening of the fruit—effected by the swelling of the ovary—the inhibition of the plant's outward growth is transformed into growth within the plant itself, i.e. its fruit. The genus process has itself become growth, the end of the vegetative process has returned to its beginning—yet not without having gathered all the plant's processes and forms into its complete concept. Thus, the plant first withdraws itself from the totality of the flower's organic curvature back into the very point from which it had originally developed. For the innermost core of the fruit is the seed, from which new plants subsequently arise. Seed formation remains the highest potentiation of plant life, for out of the single initial seed-grain, a multitude of seeds are now generated within the fruit, from which many new plants may spring forth. The totality of the fruit—standing in relation to the seed and enveloping it—now constitutes the fully realized plant product. This is the fruit-case (pericarp), which can range from a green pod, as in the legume, to succulent flesh, as in orchard fruits, the succulent flesh, or a wooden shell, as in the nut. Here, the result of the assimilation process—particularly with regard to water—manifests itself in the taste of the fruit, just as the image of the formative process arose within the seed. It is a matter of contingency whether the kernel containing the seed is situated internally or externally—as with the cherry stone versus the walnut shell. Likewise, it is equally immaterial whether the palatable element resides on the outside or on the inside, such as in the pear or the nut. Yet the growth and maturation of the seed is, in turn, nothing other than a decay of the fruit—just as the withering of the pistil constituted the ripening of the fruit.

While the plant’s striving to attain its own Self is merely the constant negation of this series of individuals that displace one another—the seed dies within the stem, the leaf within the bark, the pistil within the fruit, and the fruit within the seed—the negation of immediate individuality is posited, but simultaneously returns to itself—as a mediated entity—through its very negation. In this manner, the many individuals of the plant become moments of the One Self. This resurrection of the many independent individuals—as ideal entities—into the true unity of life—whereby they become moments, organs, and members of One Subject—forms the transition from the vegetative organism to the animal organism, which will constitute the conclusion of our reflections and the completion of the Idea of ​​Nature.
