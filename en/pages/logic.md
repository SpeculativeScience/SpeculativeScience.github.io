# Logic

Note: This webpage is a translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Encyclopedia Logic* (1876) 

After providing a general introduction to philosophy, we must now offer a specific introduction to Logic, for we are required to set forth the concept of this science, just as we did with philosophy, in a preliminary manner. Because philosophy is the encyclopedia of all sciences, in that it furnishes the principles for them all, Logic relates to philosophy and the sciences as the transition and intermediary link between them. In Logic, philosophy thus moves within its proper element, whereas in its other two branches (Nature and Spirit) it points toward the remaining sciences and strives toward them. If philosophy—by virtue of containing the principles of all sciences—stands in the relation of the universal to the particular, then Logic—as the universal science—in turn encompasses within itself the principles of philosophy. Therefore, Logic is philosophy *par excellence*—the "first philosophy," as Aristotle termed it.

If we examine more closely how Logic merits this primacy, its very name suggests that it is, preeminently, the science of reason, i.e. of *thought*. To be sure, the other philosophical sciences also concern themselves with reason and thought. The Philosophy of Nature examines, within natural objects, the eternal thought that constitutes their essence. The Philosophy of Right does not examine this or that body of law—whether Roman, Canon, Germanic, and so forth—but the Idea of ​​Right that follows from absolute reason. In such sciences, however, we are concerned with another object, one that is not thought *as such*, but belongs to the sphere of appearance and finitude. In other words, Nature and Right are not *merely* thought. Nature has a sensuous existence, and Right—as the realization of the rational will—is not pure thought, but is bound to natural objects (such as property). Logic, by contrast, concerns itself with thought that is thought through and through—with the thought that is nothing other than thought. Logic is thinking, and its entire subject matter is thought, such that it is pre-eminently the science in which thinking appears as the thinking of thinking. Since thinking constitutes our own innermost nature—that realm in which we are entirely at home with ourselves—Logic is the freest of all the philosophical sciences. Every element of foreignness is rendered transparent and is effaced such that there is only thought and thinking. Thus, if philosophy—in accordance with the injunction of the Delphic God—is self-knowledge, then Logic is precisely this self-knowledge in the noblest, most perfect sense: as the knowledge of our true essence.

From the concept of Logic follows three corollaries that contain their very opposite within themselves. Firstly, because there is nothing more to Logic than thinking, it might seem to be the easiest of all the sciences, even doing so boastfully—much like Bias: "I carry everything with me." One need only to think and think about one's own thinking to discern therein the nature and principles of objective thinking or Reason. Thus, philosophy springs just as surely from observation and experience, a domain that is by no means the exclusive privilege of sciences that have hitherto arrogantly styled themselves as "exact" ones. However, this very ascent into pure thought—wherein we cast off everything that might disturb us—is actually the most difficult task of all. Our ordinary consciousness is a blend of thoughts and mental images, it is still subjective thinking or *Understanding*, not objective thinking or *Reason*. It is the most arduous undertaking of all to liberate the pure metal of truth from the dross of consciousness—to smelt the "King of Gold" from the common ores. It is only through the greatest exertion that we attain this pure element, and it is only when we have laboriously scaled the highest peaks of knowledge that we breathe in the purest mountain air of philosophy—an air that only the strongest lungs, after a long and gradual process of acclimatization, are able to endure without distress. Therefore, even though Logic is the science that is closest to our being, requiring no external knowledge whatsoever, it nonetheless presents itself as the most difficult of the sciences, if for no other reason than that it marks the *beginning*—and every beginning, by its very nature, is always difficult.

Secondly, that Logic is the first science means that it is also the most abstract of all the sciences. To the extent that as it concerns itself with absolute thought—before thought has refracted itself from its pure light into earthly color (to borrow Newton’s metaphor)—it stands as the holiest of holies within the temple of knowledge. Within Logic, thought that generates itself from within itself, unfolding into a series of different forms that constitute the most universal predicates of all things. This system of pure thought—viewed as the original categories of things—is certainly to be regarded as the series of definitions of the Absolute, each of these predicates represents one facet of its complete and total self-definition. But for these predicates (τὰ κατηγορούμενα), Logic still lacks the subjects (τὰ ὑποκείμενα)—the particulars and actualities—in and through which thoughts prove their validity and thereby acquire an actual foothold and foundation. We certainly do not wish to hypostatize them within a transcendent subject, as Gabler (somewhat theologically) had done. As much as Logic does not yet engage with the concrete richness of the universe—nor does it yet demonstrate how these thoughts take on flesh and blood—it gives no immediate insight into many questions that we might want to see answered by philosophy. While the Science of Spirit—where objective thought (existing here as self-conscious thought) presents itself in the process of its own realization—is, for this very reason, the last and highest of the sciences. Hence, Logic is the most abstract science, and when one studies it for the first time, it may even appear to be the driest of them all. It is only when one has seen—through the exposition of the other philosophical sciences—how these thoughts constitute the primal source of the entire fullness of Being, does one grasp the full value of Logic.

From this, thirdly, results the solution to the controversy of whether Logic is devoid of content or partakes in the highest content. With respect to the opposition between form and content in philosophy, there exists a long-standing view that Logic has nothing to with content of thought and has everything to do with the form of thought. Since logical thinking does not yet engage with the more concrete objects of nature and spirit, one jumps to the conclusion that Logic only teaches us *how* to do philosophy, not *what* philosophy is. The latter instruction, it is held, is reserved for Metaphysics, that is to say, for the two other sciences of Nature and Spirit that follow the Logic. In this view, Logic only has to do with the manner in which the operations of thought proceed within the human mind, developing the laws of thought, teaching the formation of concepts, and demonstrating how to form judgments and draw inferences. Even if Logic does not teach one *how* to think *per se*—since, as Hegel observes, one can, after all, think even without having studied Logic (just as one can digest without having studied physiology)—it nevertheless teaches us to be more rational and consistent in our thinking. Logic is deemed highly useful to the extent that it disciplines the mind, diverts us—much like mathematics—from being preoccupied with the sensory appearances of things, thereby exercising and strengthening our faculty of thought. Only after this has been accomplished, it is argued, can one turn one’s thoughts toward the various subject matters of philosophy—toward God, the world, and the soul—and thus proceed to study Metaphysics. This constitutes the one-sided conception of Logic as a merely *formal logic*, or a *logic of the understanding*—a conception that has prevailed in the history of this science from Aristotle to Kant. 

Against this erroneous view of Logic, the following must be asserted: that if it were the case that Logic concerned itself only with the form—and not the content—of philosophy, then Logic itself—being devoid of content—would also be devoid of thought, for one cannot think without thinking *of* something. Even if Logic does not deal with a sensuous material, thought is nonetheless its own content. As the *praedicabilia* of things, logical categories constitute the principles of things, and since these principles are the truth of things, Logic is Ontology—a discipline which historically formed the first part of Metaphysics. Logic and Metaphysics, therefore, cannot be separated from one another no more than form and content can be separated. Instead, it is the standpoint of speculative philosophy to combine both sciences into a single unity. Nevertheless, within the context of Logic itself, they can still be distinguished as its two distinct aspects. If we consider Logic from the perspective of its content, then it is an ontology, for the unfolding of pure thought reveals the supreme principles of all things.

Conversely, if we secondly consider Logic from the perspective of its form, then it is a method. Logic demonstrates the manner and mode of the progression within the unfolding of thoughts, and shows how one thought unfolds from another to form a complete system. If thinking were merely a subjective activity occurring *within* us, one might certainly be justified in asserting that Logic, which simply traces this movement of thought, possesses no content.

Thus, having long since discarded this distorted view, the only two questions that remain are, thirdly, how form and content are connected within Logic, and how Logic identifies ontology and methodology as inseparable. The self-evidence of the answer follows directly from considering that thought—by virtue of its creative activity—constructs itself. As thought transitions, in terms of form, from one thought to another, one thought generates the next, thus arrives at ever-new content. Because Logic depicts this immanent movement of absolute thought, methodology and ontology coincide within it. As the immanent movement of the subject matter itself, it renders its moments—the aspects of its form—into aspects of its content as well. Then, in the further development of these principles—as they reveal themselves to be the essential content of Nature and Spirit—the sciences of Nature and Spirit are themselves constructed from this immanent movement to the give more concrete content of the universe, thereby completing the metaphysics that already began with Logic, just as cosmology, psychology, and theology constituted the remaining metaphysical sciences alongside ontology. In these disciplines, we shall therefore have recurrent occasion to point back to the categories of Logic that have proved themselves to be the generative forces behind all content.

It now remains for us to specify more closely the nature of the method—though once again only provisionally, since it will ultimately arise of its own accord through the very development of the thought itself. As the activity of the self-moving subject matter, philosophy is a self-validating science, and therein lies, above all, its exactitude. The method contains the blueprint for the self-construction of the entire science, possessing as many facets of its content as the method has necessary moments in its progression. Because philosophical thought is not one-sided, but is driven from unified thought into a realm of opposites, and because the truth results from the union of these opposites, it follows that every logical category requires three moments in order to be complete.

The first activity of thinking is the activity of Understanding. In order to be unite opposing thoughts, they must first be separated, and this is precisely what the Understanding does. Its activity—being an abstracting activity—draws apart what is connected, and holds each thought fast in isolation, without relating it to its opposite. For example, if the Understanding separates Being and Thinking, then Being is only Being, and Thinking is only Thinking, without the one passing over into the other. The Understanding is also absolutely necessary for the practical purposes of everyday life. Judgment requires the Understanding since the man of action, in order to accomplish anything, must commit himself one-sidedly to something specific in every decision that he makes. Here, the conjunctions "Either—Or" apply—just as the Roman judge was handed one tablet marked with an A (*absolvo*) and another marked with a C (*condemno*)—though even in that context, a third option—N.L. (*non liquet*)—was not absent. When applied erroneously to philosophy, the dilemma of mutual exclusion constitutes what is known as Dogmatism—a category to which, in ancient philosophy, Stoicism and Epicureanism belonged, and to which, in modern philosophy, Wolffian philosophy belongs. The Stoic asserted that the universality of thought is the only principle of philosophy, the Epicurean asserted that the individuality of being is the only principle of philosophy, and Wolff sought to determine whether the world was finite or infinite.

The second activity of thinking supersedes the rigidity of the Understanding by revealing the contradiction by virtue of which that category passes over into its opposite, whereby the one springs forth from the other, and in this manner, both mutually destroy one another. Therefore, the result that the one opposite holds no more validity than the other opposite. It is within this second activity of thinking that the true driving force of the method resides. Dialectic—as this self-movement of the subject matter itself—is no external shuffling of opposites, but is the very source of all development. It stands on a higher than Understanding or abstract thinking, for it does not rest content with the "either-or" of dogmatic assertions. Although its capacity as a "neither-nor" yields only a negative result, its true core is still Reason, to which it leads of its own accord. If one were to remain fixated upon this purely negative result, one would indeed fall into Skepticism—a stance which, when coupled with a self-serving ulterior motive, degenerates into Sophistry. These isolated opposites constitute merely the *appearance* of things, not their true *being*, hence why Plato rightly confines the Sophist to the realm of mere appearances. From this also stems the ancient maxim that the Sophist possesses the skill to dispute *in utramque partem*—arguing both sides of an issue—and can thus prove the exact opposite of any given proposition, depending entirely on what gives them the advantage over who they are arguing against. However, Skepticism merely serves as a transitional stage on the path to attaining the positive results of Reason, and precisely because of this underlying purpose, which even shines through the process itself, this dialectical activity may be aptly termed the "negatively rational" activity. Kant observes that the contradictions of Reason are inevitable, yet they are contradictions whose reconcilation is inevitable. For this reason, philosophy can also be defined as the faculty of bringing one's thoughts together.

The positive result is, in fact, already contained within the movement itself and awaits being brought to light. For in that the first thought transforms itself into a second thought through the contradiction within it, this second thought, for the very same reason, transforms back into the first—each opposite is negated only in their one-sidedness, not in their interconnection. Dialectic therefore does not yield nothing in the absolute sense as its result, rather the negation of two categories—viewed as specific entities opposed to one another—constitutes their positive interconnection within a third concept in which both are contained as identical. This third concept—as their truth—stands as the concrete reality in contrast to the abstract opposites. In truth, the opposites result *from* this third concept as their common root, even though in the course of dialectical exposition, it may appear as if the opposites were, on the contrary, the source of the concept. This third activity of thinking is, by virtue of its result, the *positively* rational or *speculative* activity. For speculation signifies precisely the apprehension of opposites in their positive unity—their "both-and." All truth is, therefore, the third term that arises from opposites. Jacobi is, consequently, mistaken when he asserts that between two opposites there can be no third term. There is *always* a third term, Hegel counters, and philosophy exists only to the extent that this third term exists. However, this third term does not fall *outside* of the other two, rather the three terms constitute a unity in themselves, and it is only the unified movement—weaving its way through these three stages—and not the static, lifeless result, that constitutes the truth. The Trinity is therefore to be regarded—even from a philosophical standpoint—as the highest expression of Absolute Being. The structural organization of science—both in its broad outlines and in its minute details—is thus trichotomous in nature, for the number three—as recognized as early as Pythagoras—constitutes the very form of truth. 

Accordingly, the system of pure thought must also traverse a threefold progression. The division of Logic is such that we must first consider thought as simple and immediate—and this is the thought of Being. Secondly, Being is internally differentiated as Essence, as much as it has an inward aspect and an outward aspect. Thirdly, the opposition within Being itself resolves itself in the Concept, which constitutes both the Essence of things and their immediate Being. However, the recognition that Being, Essence, and the Concept are thoughts is a reflection of our own thinking, for self-conscious thought appears only within Spirit, whereas in Logic it exists only implicitly without yet being aware of itself as such.
