# What is Reason?

There is one question that every philosophy in its beginning must grapple with – what is Reason? 

The purpose of philosophy is the knowledge what rationally is. However, to even know what rationally is presupposes that thought already possesses knowledge its own knowing. This reflexivity of knowing, or knowledge that knows its own knowing, is Reason. For if Reason did not exist, then knowledge would be unknowable and thereby indistinguishable from mere opinion and belief. It is a complete impossibility that Truth could even be denied as such. The relativist who asserts that Truth is not unconditioned, that everything is conditioned and so relative to one another, are themselves making a claim that is unconditioned, contradicting the prior assumption that everything is conditioned. While all other philosophies have at least attempted to begin their journey in knowing their own knowing, then the relativist has refused to begin at all.

Some have attempted to determine what Reason is by making the claim that there is an unconditioned first principle that is foundation for all knowledge. One need not even one glance at the history of philosophy to speculate what first principle this foundation could be. Formal logic, mathematics, computation, nature, mechanics, life, the psyche, the will, language, ideology, archetypes, God, etc. have all been argued to have no logical principle prior to it for various reasons. This approach presupposes what Reason is because it begins with a logical result, a determinate concept that has logical relationships internal to it. Whenever someone claims that their foundation is self-evident, they ignored the fact that their first principle has logical relationships. So either the substance-centric philosopher is forced to admit that the relationships are there, meaning that the first principle is not unconditioned after all, or deny that there are relationships, which of course would mean that this first principle is meaningless.

Others have tried to figure out what Reason is by instead shifting their attention towards their own consciousness. Historically, this attempt has often developed a response the failure of substance-philosophy to justify its own first principle as self-evident. Because consciousness has now been placed as the origin of knowledge, we refer to this position as subject-oriented philosophy. One kind is direct realism, which argues that the source of knowledge is our own sense-experience. That is, evidence of the thing is the correspondence between the thing experienced and mental “copy” of the thing in our minds. And yet, evidence is itself a determination of Reason, so to claim that you have evidence of some thing presupposes that you already know the thing to be evidenced, thus contradicting the prior argument that sense-perception is the source of our knowledge. The position of indirect realism is a logical consequence of such a realization, asserting that there is no such necessary link between the thought of a thing and its being. This perspective presents its own problems as the thing being perceived is no longer unified. It is not a thing, but two different things that exist in two seperate realities – my own mind or the physical world (or as Descartes put it, res cogitans and res extensa). Since there can only be one valid source for knowledge and the determination of one precludes the reality of the other, the only logical conclusion that remains is that being itself is a thought and the thought of being is the thought of a thought – i.e. thinking.

The reflexivity of this conclusion has given us our first clue as to what Reason really is. From the standpoint of Idealism, the rationality of consciousness (subject) is the same rationality that is in the world itself (substance). Reason subsumes and determines the reality of the subject and the object. It is only concepts, as determinations of thought, that are self-determining and reflexive. The content of a concept is its semantics, its meaning and interpretation for Reason, whereas its form is its syntax, the rules and patterns of Reason. To even arrive at the standpoint of pure thinking, we cannot assume any pre-established syntax, forms or rules of inference. Applying a formula that is known to be valid to reason about content does not prove anything because the formula is itself empty and has no relation to the content it acts on. Moreover, if the content that is input for the formula is known to be false, then the output conclusion would be false as well. Garbage in, garbage out. To confuse formal logic with pure thinking would be to abandon philosophy from the outset. Logic is, and can only ever be, an onto-logic.

If we are to satisfy the criterion of Logic as an ontology, then the only way of doing so is to think immanently about the form and content of concepts. To deny immanence while atempting to give an explanation for a concept would be to posit something outside the bounds of Reason itself. However self-explanitory this may seem, it may surprise the reader to learn that no theory of epistemic justification can be considered a first philosophy. These methods of justification are forms of mediation that are derived retroactively from their content. In trying to justify the thing, we already presuppose knowledge of it. There is quite literally no reason that can justify our prior assumptions of what Reason is, so the only remaining answer is to let go of our assumptions and simply investigate the structure and development of thought itself to figure out what Reason really is. This investigative process is the speculative method.
