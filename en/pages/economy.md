# Political Economy

Note: This webpage is a direct translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Natural Law, Vol. II* (1866) 

In observing the satisfaction of drives enter the sphere of ethicality, we see nothing other than the mediation of these particular ends by freedom itself. This was not the case in antiquity. The well-being of the individual was not secured through the free activity of all, but through the labor of the slave. The free citizen, who perceives themselves as a living member of the body politic, becomes consciousness of the universal ends of public life. The enjoyment of domestic life for the free citizen is a product of their own property, whereas for the slave, who is excluded from the enjoyment of property by being the property of another, the reproduction of their existence as a slave constituted the wage paid to them by the master in exchange for their labor. There existed no laws, not even laws of blind necessity, by which the free activity of all could generate the particular well-being of each individual. Political economy had yet to become a science at this time, for it derives its being from the fact that its subject matter encompasses and comprehends the production undertaken by individuals as interwoven into collective production of the society as a whole.

While the wealth of the moderns has its origin in labor, the wealth of the ancients had its origin in conquest. The subjugation of defeated peoples furnished the victors with an enslaved class of laborers that were captured from the conquered lands. Within the conquering nation itself, the ever-increasing wealth of the few engendered the ever-increasing poverty of the masses, as was observed in the historical society of the Spartans. Just as in antiquity, the individual citizens of one and the same state did not enrich one another through mutual service and cooperation, so too was this not the case between different states in antiquity, for each nation rose only through the downfall of another and enriched itself at the latter's expense. But slave labor is far less productive than free labor since fear is a less effective motivator than the self-interested desire to secure enjoyment. In the conception of man's natural existence, the mutual relationship of human beings is still absent, for each family secures the satisfaction of its needs in isolation and purely for the purpose of the survival of the family. Now, however, within the communal life of human beings, the distinctiveness of their individual interests and goals has itself become an indispensable aspect of their freedom, and as a result, the ethical fabric of society is far more enduring, at least to the extent that the universality of common goals have encompassed the sphere of the individual's particular goals. The free, autonomous activity of the individual has now itself become an integral component of communal life, enjoyment, labor, and property are transformed into achievements that are shared by all without forfeiting the private aspect of each, and only in this manner is the wealth produced shared by every citizen.

The fundamental principle and linchpin that generates the science of political economy is this: that the egoistic end that each and every individual sets for themselves in satisfying their needs is immanently and necessarily transformed into activity that serves the ends of others, since each person obtains what they need only by exchanging with others for what they need. Everything, so declare Bastiat and Proudhon in the same breath, is exchange and the reciprocity of services. Thus, it is only through the freedom of labor that both nations and individuals are called to share in equal wealth, whereas in antiquity, the labor of the slave served only to satisfy the ends of the master. Now, however, the more an individual produces their specific goods, the more they serve to satisfy the needs of others, even as they do so to satisfy their own ends. Hence the maxim of love also holds true here: The more I give, the more I also possess. The individual's objective becomes, even without their conscious intent, a universal objective, and by pursuing their own well-being, they simultaneously pursue the well-being of all. Even if the individual imagines themselves to be engaged in fierce competition against everyone else (like the Hobbesian war of all against all), the negativity of competition negates itself into cooperation and mutual support. Hence, in the sphere of political economy, the productive activity of individuals for the sake of satisfying their own ends becomes so well orchestrated that these activities serve social purposes as well. Necessity, as Plato observed, is the true creator of states. However, ethicality is the higher reason that, as Hegel puts it, has the sufficient cunning to realize, behind people's backs, a universal social purpose that they neither suspected nor intended. Proudhon, in contrast to Bastiat who proceeds from "economic harmonies", has termed political economy a "contradictory science". Both claims are parts of the whole truth, for harmony arises out of the struggle of these very contradictions. Although individuals are personally independent from one another, they are still materially dependent upon one another. In antiquity, the free citizen endeavored to unite all productive activities within their own household as much as possible. The Greek family strove so earnestly to be self-sufficient that, in the Homeric age, even the queens produced for their own needs. Each paterfamilias was essentially independent from the others, linked to each other only by the bonds of the state, whereas for the modern man, the mutual dependence individuals through their needs has become the inner necessity of a new ethical bond.

From what has been said, the following questions arise: What needs does man possess that draw him so deeply into community with other human beings? How can he satisfy these needs collectively? That is, firstly, what is the system of needs that unfolds within social life? Secondly, we must examine the means by which man procures and secures his livelihood. This is labor, which much like the system of needs, weaves a common bond between human beings through the interconnection of various professions. The third and final the outcome of labor is the good, which constitutes the general wealth of the people just as surely as it embodies the immediate means of subsistence for the individual. In the case of labor itself, it could still remain uncertain whether it would indeed result in the acquisition of property and the enjoyment of its fruits.

## Needs

### Natural Needs

To the extent that man, as a spiritual being, is discontent with the satisfaction of basic, natural needs, his needs are not left in their original simplicity. An animal, for instance, is satisfied with eating same food, inhabiting the same dwelling, and so forth. The needs of man, however, increases continuously the moment he becomes accustomed to satisfying the needs he previously desired. Through habit, a state of dissatisfaction sets in. A new drive to satisfy a new need suddenly arises, one which, because the means for its satisfaction lags haltingly behind (and may not even exist yet), becomes a painful goad that forces man to seek out further means and forms of gratification. The needs and means for their satisfaction are multiplied ever further, breaking down every natural need into a multitude of aspects and striving to discover or invent ever more precise and appropriate means for each of these specific aspects. Thus, to the needs bestowed upon him by Nature, man adds acquired and imagined needs, thereby increasing his material dependence. But material dependence upon whom? By the very nature of need, we are, in the first instance, dependent upon Nature. The Cynics and Rousseau sought the perfection of the free, spiritual human being by reducing this state of dependence to the absolute minimum. "It is the prerogative of the gods," says Diogenes, "to need nothing at all, and of god-like men, to need as little as possible." And for the very same reason, Rousseau advised man to return straight to the forests and subsist on acorns. But did the Cynics truly attain freedom through this restriction of their needs? They still required a cloak, bread, a begging-sack, a barrel, a walking-staff, and so forth. They did not escape their dependence upon nature, for no one can live by weaning themselves off the activity of living itself. 

Even if man's independence from nature cannot be achieved through this path, it seems utterly paradoxical to attempt to achieve spiritual freedom through the multiplication of needs, which only ever increases man's dependence. At the same time, it is through this very contradiction that man's independence is won. By creating and satisfying needs for myself, I overcome the needs that are imposed on me by nature. In place of natural need, I substitute the multiplicity of needs generated by my own intellect. I no longer depend on nature, but on myself. By determining myself through my surroundings, my dwelling, my clothing, etc. into a living work of art, I perform an act of self-creation, whereas the natural man remains limited to products of nature. And thus we see the Cynics degenerate from their strained, lifeless spirituality into shameless beggars and parasites, without ever attaining the higher spiritual cultivation that philosophy intends to bestow. Therefore, since we cannot become free by restricting or suppressing our natural needs, there is no other option but to transform them into free, spiritual, and human needs, so that we may cease to depend on nature (even while existing within it), and instead depend upon ourselves so that we may overcome our state of natural dependency through the power of art. 

However, in the satisfaction of the needs they have created for themselves, every individual depends not only upon themselves, but also upon other human beings. How many hundreds of hands do we not require to accomplish even the simplest of tasks! It is certainly impossible to achieve everything we need entirely by ourselves. At every moment of our lives, even in satisfaction of our most basic needs, we depend upon the labor of hundreds of people, just as they, in turn, depend upon us. This mutual dependence of human beings simultaneously liberates the human being from nature. For within the reciprocity of this mutual dependence, the autonomy and enjoyment of each is preserved. Through only a few days labor I can buy what I alone could not have produced in a hundred years, and what could have only been created so swiftly and affordably through the social labor of many people.

The differentiation of needs ultimately completes the liberation of man from nature, in that greater diligence and care can now be devoted to satisfying every means and form of gratification. As the means becomes ever more suitable to the need and the need is satisfied ever more effectively by the means, the activity of gratification also becomes as refined as the needs and means by which they are satisfied. Since animals satisfy their needs in a simple manner, their needs are met only imperfectly. The cave allows wind and weather to penetrate through its entrance, through crevices in the rock, and so forth. Man, however, ensures — by means of roofs, windows, doors, and the like — that not a single drop of rain can enter, that the natural air is artificially warmed, and so on. In short, the comfortable arrangement through which man increasingly shields himself from all the adverse influences of nature thereby immerses man in a "second nature" (i.e. the reshaping of nature by human beings through habit) that brings his liberation from nature to its completion.

### Social Needs (Fashions)

While man remains within the sphere of natural existence in satisfying his needs, it is precisely the spiritualization of these needs that substitutes his natural individuality for universality. In the state of nature, everyone possesses their own particular drives and satisfies them according to their whims. However, if I do not have the means for satisfying my needs and depend upon the will and activity of others to satisfy them, just as they depend upon mine, then neither of us can simply follow our own arbitrary whims, but must accommodate ourselves to each other's needs. The need becomes a common one that everyone wants to satisfy in the same manner, i.e. a fashion. Since I do not create the means of satisfaction myself, but accept them as others offer them to me (though I exert an influence upon the production of these means), isolated, individual needs thereby evolve into universally recognized, social needs. One person wishes to satisfy the same need in the same way as another, and so everyone follows the same fashion. Likewise, it the mark of the eccentric to seek to distinguish themselves by promoting goods that nobody wants. Anyone who aspires to this would certainly be better advised, following Rousseau, to retreat to the woods and eat acorns, than like Diogenes, to play the part of the unsociable contrarian in the midst of society, and to seek to attract attention through clothing, gait, demeanor, for no other reason beyond attracting attention.

Hence, what is essential to fashion is equality. Everyone wants to have the same things that everyone else has. However, since the proliferation of needs and the means to satisfy them is never at a standstill, but constantly seeks out novelty, inequality disruptively intrudes upon the prevailing equality at every moment. Those who want to satisfy a need imagine how it could be improved, in order to achieve greater convenience for themselves, and those who produce the means for satisfying this need exert their ingenuity in perfecting it, in order to be able to sell it more easily. Yet, each inequality that arises is once again swept away by the current of equality. For once a new product is invented, everyone wants to appropriate this new product, to make use of it, and be like everyone else in having it. It is then said that, through this universal imitation, the new invention has become fashionable. However, no sooner is equality restored than a new inequality arises. The old falls out of fashion and is displaced by something new, a progression that becomes endless due to the alternation of equality and inequality. Because of the cyclic nature of fashion, it is often the case that the old becomes new again once people acquire a nostalgia for the fashions of the past. The universality inherent to fashion, however, remains external, for every inequality that asserts itself invents a new particularity of content that is obscured only by the fact that everyone adopted it for period of time, thereby invariably finding in it an expression of their own shared striving and will.

### Luxury Needs

If the inequality of needs is a result of innovation, and conversely, the equality of needs that renders this innovation universal is a result of fashion, then we can conclude that innovation and fashion perpetually strives for the other as the other's purpose. An invention that has become fashionable soon falls out of fashion and gives way to a new fashion. By the time the last ones have adopted a fashion, the first ones who adopted it have already moved on from it and are in the pursuit of a new trend. This trajectory of the social condition aimed at the unbounded multiplication and differentiation of needs, means, and enjoyments is luxury. The epitome of the surplus product transcends the simple necessities dictated by nature, and in doing so, it is precisely what makes man a human being. In this sense, luxury is a phenomenon of the most general nature, one that pertains not only to affluence, but equally to wherever wealth exists. For who would be content with only what Nature provides? In-itself, luxury is in no way an evil, least of all a vice, since it is the very prerequisite for higher education. Physical well-being serves to elevate intellectual well-being as well. The person who is only ever content with the satisfaction of natural needs remains, by the same token, anchored at the level of the animal. Wherever the struggle against raw, untamed nature remains an ongoing battle, higher intellectual and cultural development has yet to find the opportunity to take root. Innovation and the spirit of invention spur man on to turn his mind toward nobler, higher pursuits and pleasures, once he has first satisfied his immediate, natural needs. Intellectual education goes hand in hand with luxury, and the most educated nations have invariably been those in which the greatest luxury has developed. Only after all the necessary needs of a nation had been satisfied, observed Aristotle, did people begin to philosophize, that is, to seek to satisfy the innate drive for knowledge (and this is why Hegel suggests that philosophy is a luxury need).

In contrast to the beneficial aspects of luxury, however, its rise has indeed been accompanied by moral corruption and an outbreak of vices of every kind. Human self-interest compelled the cunning of society to become ever more inventive, such that ever more human beings want to satisfy their needs, having been elevated an infinite degree, at the expense of their fellow human beings, thereby creating crimes of every variety. Yet, even for this reason, economic philosophy cannot counsel us to give up and retreat to the forests. We must not seek to evade the contradictions already alluded to by Proudhon, rather, we can only overcome them by boldly confronting them head-on. Anyone who regards the flourishing of art and science as the supreme blossom of the tree of social life and desires to see this end realized cannot neglect the attendant evils that stand in opposition to it. For both aspects are necessary consequences of the production of luxury. The evil serves as the origin for the good, and the latter serves as the cure to the former. Good and evil are inseparable, and to abandon the good out of fear of the evil would be tantamount to throwing out the baby with the bathwater.

But luxury also has other disadvantages in its wake. The wealthy squanders their assets to no useful end, and money is wasted that could feed hundreds. No sound economic philosophy would cite the extravagance of the court as a valid justification on the grounds that it provides work for thousands, or moreover, that it serves to appease their discontent. Nevertheless, it is true that the luxury of some invariably effects the prosperity of others. A person who indulges in luxury must be all the more industrious if they want to afford the costs of their lavish lifestyle. They must earn more than their expenditures increase, to say nothing of the additional benefit of putting money into circulation. Admittedly, political economy must distrust the form of luxury that consumes without producing, the luxury of the so-called "idlers" (les oisifs) described by the Saint-Simonians. Yet even in this instance, the spendthrift still puts money into circulation should they happen to squander their inheritance in the process. Hence, luxury is just as much the cause of prosperity as it is the cause of precarity. Within this web of mutual interdependence, the individual ceases to constitute a self-sufficient unit comprising themselves and their family, and instead satisfies only a single specific type of need through their labor. Not only must one redouble one’s efforts just to keep pace with everyone else and to keep up with the latest fashions, but it may well come to pass that, with the shifting tides of fashion, the particular product they have specialized in manufacturing suddenly finds itself devoid of demand, leaving them unable to find any buyers for his wares. Thus, the more that luxury increases for some, the harder it becomes for others to procure even the most basic necessities. A new contradiction, between that of wealth and labor, has now appeared, to which we will return later. On the gold of the carpets and garments that the rich man displays in his home hangs the lonely, weeping toil of the worker who cannot enjoy the luxury for whose production he works. The endless pursuit of luxury transforms into an endless state of dependency, misery, and destitution.

## Labor

The liberation of man from his dependence upon nature, a liberation that consists of him overcoming, negating, and reshaping nature to satisfy his needs, constitutes the concept of labor. The animal does not labor, for even when it tears apart its prey, takes a sip of water, or however else it appropriates nature as an object of its desire, it purely accepts this object as it is given by nature. Even man in the primordial state of nature freely subsisted off the fruits of the Earth, and only expending labor whenever his survival depended on it. Yet since man is spirit, then the simplest satisfaction of his natural needs already constitutes the activity of labor through which man recognizes himself as the master of nature and therefore as free. If, with the multiplication and innovation of needs and pleasures, both labor and liberation likewise increase, it is with good reason that man's expulsion from Paradise has been interpreted not only as man’s laboring "by sweat of his brow," but also as the realization that he is made in God's image, and therefore is his first step toward rising from a state of naturalness to one of freedom and spirituality. 

While the perfection of labor, luxury and higher education go hand in hand, in antiquity we find these aspects neither linked by a causal relationship nor even coexisting within one and the same person. Labor was neither the source of luxury nor education, whether for an individual or an entire people, since the latter two fell only to the rich, while the former fell to the slave. In Christianity, admittedly, these ills are intended to be remedied. Yet, even though labor has indeed become the source of luxury and education, the opposition between luxury and education on the one hand, and poverty and wealth on the other, has not ceased to exist, and this is true even though labor has become free. The opposition between poverty and wealth, between luxury and labor, and so forth, has been reduced to a quantitative distinction one that is on its way to becoming increasingly and organically balanced. However, because in antiquity this opposition was rooted in the relationship of mastery and servitude, it constituted a qualitatively insurmountable barrier, one that could only find its resolution within the dialectic of that very relationship, which also entailed moral corruption as an inevitable consequence. For the free citizen, who possessed a consciousness of moral purpose only within the sphere of public life, could, following the loss of political liberty (a loss that occurred in Greece after the Peloponnesian War and in Rome with the fall of the Republic), find reflected in their own wealth and luxury the particular interests of their own gratification, devoid of any universal purpose, and bound to forfeit every moral anchor. Christianity, which struck its roots deep into the mire of this universal moral corruption, therefore arrived at the pronouncement that a camel would pass through the eye of a needle sooner than a rich man would enter heaven. By contrast, the consciousness of the servant, following the loss of public life, retained the capacity to grasp the possibility of ethical purpose amidst the particularity of the self-seeking interests of civil society. For the slave, the sole laborer of antiquity, remained confined the labor they provided for others. Through arduous labor in the service of the master, the slave divested themselves of their selfishness, and acquired self-consciousness of inherent in the nature of their condition, whereas the master beheld their own freedom reflected in the self-consciousness of their slave. Thus, Christianity reserved the Kingdom of Heaven exclusively for the poor, the weary, and the heavy-laden. It is in this way that, during the Middle Ages, the artisan gradually evolved into a free laborer themselves.

Regarding the nature of labor, since man no longer labors solely to satisfy all his needs, man has been reduced to a cog in the intricate machinery of the collective system of needs. Each individual occupies a specific position within this whole and performs a particular task by whatever means. Therein lies the origin of professions, social orders distinguished by the specific kind of labor they undertake, serving as the means by which our collective needs are satisfied. Within the industry, individuality becomes objectified and is explicitly elevated to the form of universality, for an entire profession of workers belongs to a specific activity through which they recognize themselves as a collective entity. 

### Agriculture

Since labor now relates to nature in a transformative manner, it turns to nature to take possession of its bounty. Because the labor is limited to gathering, harvesting, and the like, these workers remain in a more immediate connection with nature. The efficacy of this labor depends more on nature than on man, such as, the course of the weather on seasonal harvests. Here, Adam Smith observes, nature performs a full third of the labor herself. By virtue of the power and beneficence of nature, this profession still lives at her service. Thus, we may term this kind of labor to be agricultural labor. I intentionally refrain from calling it the class of country dwellers in contradistinction to city dwellers, for that distinction of the Middle Ages (which necessarily imposed different forms of labor upon city dwellers than upon villagers) is in the process of vanishing, no matter how zealously some may currently strive to preserve or restore it where it has already disappeared. Indeed, it has been necessary to permit agriculturalists within the cities, and urban trades within the countryside. However, because this opposition of the city and countryside persists, the most extreme reactionaries, fearing the resistance of city dwellers, wish to see the latter’s habitations wiped from the face of the earth so that everything might revert to the rural simplicity of humanity's primordial condition.

Yet, the characteristic feature of this profession is not the external aspect of its location, but the fact that, even though an artificial division and multiplication of needs has already occurred within it to a certain degree, it nevertheless remains closer to the undivided wholeness of nature than do the other professions. This is because, in the extraction of natural products, the division of labor is not present to such a high degree, as we shall observe later in other professions, and the labor itself remains, in certain respects, less complicated to manage. Here, the family constitutes even more of a self-contained, self-sufficient whole. The rural family is more self-sufficient than other families, though it cannot entirely dispense with them, nor does it completely isolate itself, as did the "Cyclopean" family unit. The agricultural worker bakes their own bread, makes their own clothes, and may as well have built their own house with their own hands, much like the backwoodsman settlements in America. Nevertheless, the agricultural worker cannot do without the exchange of other people's labor. They will be obliged to purchase iron tools and similar items, books, furniture, and so forth in exchange for the treasures they have wrested from nature. Yet family life still remains the central priority, and precisely because family ties are so strong here, the ethical ties that extend beyond the family are correspondingly looser. It is a vestige of the primordial state of humanity, of that era when mankind led a life that was predominantly patriarchal. And so Steffens rightly observes that, in the rural life of the agricultural worker, Paradise has not yet entirely vanished. The vestiges of Paradise are retained within country life and can be rediscovered without also retreating to the forests with Rousseau.

Since the division of labor originates even here, though it relates less to the refinement of needs than the acquisition of natural products, the internal classification for this profession has less to do with the diversity of needs than in the objects that man extracts from nature. If we divide nature into its three realms of the mineral, the plant and the animal, then man draws his wealth from each of the three realms of nature. The miner brings subterranean treasures to light, the gardener and forester occupy themselves with growing and harvesting the products of plants, while the fisherman, hunter, and herdsman bring the animals into man's possession. At the center of all these occupations stands the farmer, to whom the treasures lying beneath the surface of their soil rightfully belongs, and who, depending on the nature of their land, can also be a gardener, hunter, forester, or fisherman, and who finally must engage in livestock farming in order to obtain manure for their soil. The peasant estate of the Middle Ages, which we may term the class of rural landownership, possessed enough land to combine several activities within itself, but not so much that it would necessitate a division of labor. Its subterranean treasures, to be sure, will not extract themselves, rather, they may sell or lease the mineral veins running beneath its fields, or work them jointly, with others. The farmer, together with their family, will cultivate their garden and fields and tend to his herd personally, nor will they be hindered from taking down any game that strays into their enclosure. In short, they approache the simplicity of paternal life, and much like the American settler, they may often lack the hired hands with whom to share the labor. At the same time, a division of social strata appears between landowners and day laborers, and between the small and the moderate-sized landholder. There may be day laborers who serve solely as herdsmen, and in this same manner, fishing, forestry, and hunting may also exist entirely without the ownership of land, instead appearing as forms of hired service or tenancy. All these occupational groups, the farmer included, may in turn enter into service as hired hands, day laborers, or other contractual arrangements under a large-scale landowner (the historical nobility) who reintegrates all these divisions of labor (which were not yet present in the life of the independent farmer) into the unified structure of their own agricultural estate.

This constitutes the large-scale economy, as distinct from the small-scale economy. In the small-scale economy, the labor performed is not yet refined, as each individual must undertake all rural tasks by themselves. As a result, the small-scale economy makes little progress and remains closest to a state of nature. In the large-scale economy, the division of labor becomes far more pronounced. Each day laborer performs only one specific task, they execute it more skillfully and completes it more rapidly, and the time thus gained can then be devoted to new undertakings. Indeed, as labor becomes more mechanical, it can be further supplanted through the increased use tools and machinery. The plow is perfected, and threshing machines, seed drills, and similar devices are created. Whereas the small-scale economy requires more human labor and fewer machinery, the reverse is true of the large-scale economy. By economizing on manual labor, the large-scale economy is enabled to undertake an even greater variety of ventures utilizing its surplus human workforce. Sugar beets are cultivated, and sugar refineries are established, spirits are distilled from potatoes, bread is baked and so forth. The residue remaining after distillation serves in turn as animal fodder, while livestock manure is used for fertilization. Drainage systems and artificial fertilizers are employed to improve the soil. The farmer thus establishes himself as the master of his natural environment, a mastery extending even to the alteration and amelioration of the local climate. Timber felled from the forests is sawn into planks for construction, and brickworks are established. Through large-scale farming, the industrial diligence that advances through the division of labor and innovation cannot fail to assert itself, even in agriculture, although simplicity and undivided labor remains the foundation.

### Industry

Secondly, labor consists in not leaving the products of nature exactly as they are. Only a select few natural products, such as fruit, can be enjoyed without preparation. The vast majority of natural products require further processing and shaping to become suitable for human needs. As we have already observed in the theory of property, this form of labor is of a higher, intellectual nature than the means by which the farmer acquires property through either occupation or accession. We designate the professions of society that shape the raw products of nature as the industries or trades, in contrast to agriculture. Since the form, as a product of the mind, is the purpose that is impressed upon the material, it becomes immediately evident that the true locus of intellectual cultivation and innovation is to be found here. However, this is not to say that elements of formation and invention are not present within agriculture itself, as this distinction cannot be strictly maintained. Within the industrial sphere, the division of labor immediately constitutes the necessary foundation and starting point of activity, whereas in rural occupations, it appears at the end point of refinement. Historically, it was within the industrious trades of the cities that self-esteem, self-reliance, and the spirit of freedom first awakened, for these professions recognized that they no longer owed everything to nature, given that the value of raw materials are vastly enhanced by the form they receive, thereby rendering them useful to others in the first place. Yet, just as the distinctions among rural workers were determined by the specific products of nature, so here they depend directly upon the various human needs and only indirectly upon the material employed to satisfy them. 

#### Artisanry

In truth, the distinction between the artisan and the manufacturer is a relative one. The means for satisfying the needs produced by the former may certainly constitute more of a unified whole than do the products of factories. However, apart from the fact that the artisanal trades can also be conducted on a factory-like scale, even within artisan trades the division of labor is already so extensive that the most fundamental needs fragment into a multitude of specific categories that are satisfied by a multitude of specific trades. For instance, there is no trade exclusively responsible for providing food, another for clothing, and a third for housing. To speak first of the most fundamental need, that of sustenance, consider the sheer number of trades involved the preparation of food. While millers, butchers, and the like undertake the preliminary preparation of various natural products, it is the chef, the artisan whose trade most comprehensively synthesizes this division of labor, who brings them to perfection.

Likewise, there are a multitude of trades required to provide individuals with clothing, from shoemakers, to tailors, glovers, milliners, and so on. Moreover, to the extent that the fluidity of fashion is evident in matters of form, such as the cut of a garment, the spirit of competition between artisans manifests in the invention of ever-newer forms and in the flawless execution of those already conceived. In this manner, artisanal competition becomes a source of perfection, and to abolish it would result in the stagnation and decay of the entire trade. This spirit of competition drives the development of machinery to serve as instruments for the perfection of the trade.

Furthermore, no less diverse are the trades dedicated to the construction of housing, whether it be masonry, carpentry, joining, locksmithing, and so on. Here, the division of labor has advanced to such an extent that no individual even produces a complete component of the house, in contrast to the tailor who produces an entire coat, or the shoemaker who produces an entire boot. Instead, joiners, locksmiths, and painters must coordinate their joint efforts in order to create a door, which is itself only one part of a room, and thus only one part of the building as a whole. The joint labor involved in creating a single door may itself be subdivided among several workers: one cuts the boards, another planes them, a third assembles them, a fourth smooths the surface, etc. This specialization of labor renders the worker more dexterous and talented at the activity, as they are able to focus their entire mental energy upon this single object. However, since the final purpose is the creation of a unified whole, these specialized activities must be reintegrated into a single cohesive unit within the workshop under the supervision of the master craftsman. Technical oversight becomes as essential to artisanal trades as they are to agriculture. The more extensive the division of labor, the more imperative becomes the act of cooperation.

#### Manufacturing

The division and socialization of labor takes place on the grandest scale within the manufacturing sector. Here, the infinitely numerous means are produced, without which our great needs for food, clothing, and shelter could not be satisfied at all. Factories process the sugar for our meals, needles for fastening garments, nails or bricks for construction workers, and so on. Since the work performed within them becomes so increasingly mechanical and monotonous that machinery is required at each step of the process, and to which human labor can allow the machine to work in its stead. It is beneath human dignity to perform what a machine is capable of executing. Instead of such deadening labor, man can devote himself to tasks of greater dignity. In this way, a greater quantity of labor power is available, and the greater quantity of goods obtained thereby is also of superior quality. When Richard Arkwright invented the cotton-spinning machine, he reduced the labor required for this task by two-thirds and rendered it twenty times more productive than it had been previously. Yet even prior to this invention, the division of labor alluded to earlier, along with the spirit of competition and the workshop environment, contributed in a high degree to the perfection of the industrial progress. If, for example, in a needle factory, one worker prepares the wire, a second cuts it into lengths, a third sharpens the points, a fourth produces only the needle heads, and a fifth finally assembles the various parts, the manufacturer not only produces a more perfect needle but is also able to produce a greater quantity of goods with the same quantity of labor. Furthermore, if the introduced machine is more productive and costs less to maintain than the worker, then any industrial enterprise acquires the limitless potential to manufacture anything that society demands. Once machine power has taken the place of human labor, the division of labor proceeds ever further, and the perfection of machinery heightens competition beyond all measure. One factory constantly outstrips another, yet simultaneously consolidates and unifies the division of human beings from each other. Through its mechanism, it can perform a multitude of operations simultaneously, thereby taking the place of the workshop and its master craftsman. Thus, advantages are once again multiplied through consolidation just as they are in agriculture, where large-scale farming compares to small-scale farming.

It is only fair that the individual who, through their own efforts, ingenuity, and risk, has advanced industrial enterprise should be rewarded for doing so. An invention is, initially, their own secret, one that may be carefully guarded in order to benefit from it. Since it enables them to supply goods of superior quality and at a lower cost than their competitors, they will command the largest share of the market and steer all buyers toward their business. However, this reward for their labor would elude them were their invention to become public property. This consideration leads economic theory to advocate for the granting of exclusive selling rights. The inventor is granted a privilege, albeit one limited to a few years, regarding their invention, and during this period, no one else is permitted to utilize their secret. This, Proudhon observes, is the well-earned prize of the victor in the competitive struggle of industry. Admittedly, they could potentially abuse this privilege to exploit buyers were they, in the absence of competitors, able to set their prices arbitrarily high. Yet the industriousness of others presents a remedy against this, for the inventive spirit of others cannot be stifled. While the inventor's secret may not be divulged, no one can be prevented from attempting their own invention, and indeed, through the very act of imitation, inventing something even better or more advanced. Thus, competition through imitation ultimately nullifies the privilege of the individual, rendering it a shared asset. 

#### Commerce

However, if everything constitutes an exchange of goods and services, then to satisfy the heightened needs of all, the means that everyone mutually offers one another for this satisfaction must be capable of being put into circulation with ease. Effecting this circulation constitutes a new form of labor, and as a task distinct from all other forms of labor, it is the specific business of commerce. Civilized man is not satisfied even with the artificial pleasures he obtains through the labor of his neighbors, seeking out wherever goods can be produced more effectively and cheaply instead, whether the region has superior raw materials, technology, expertise, etc. Every nation, climate, and geographical region possesses its own distinct products that they are best suited to produce, and to which they create with the utmost excellence and refinement. Thus, all the inhabitants of the earth exchange their products with one another.

##### Transportation

The task of arranging the transport of products and labor to all destinations is undertaken by freight workers, and associated with them, serving in a supporting capacity, is a host of trades ranging from teamsters to boatmen. Also belonging to this category are the postal service, steamships, railways, telegraphs, etc. Transportation can no more be a state monopoly than anything else, and only through free competition will all these enterprises achieve the highest level of excellence in their performance. 

##### Trade

But just as the freight worker manages circulation in response to specific orders and requests like the artisan produces a work for a specific client, so does the merchant correspond to the manufacturer who mass produces goods generally in anticipation of future demand. The merchant does not wait until a specific individual requests that a particular good be transported to a specific location. They anticipate that, at a given moment, a certain good will be in high demand in a particular region, and scan the entire globe to discover where that good can be obtained most advantageously and cheaply at that moment. They buy where the supply is greatest and sell where the demand is greatest, keeping the arbitrage as the reward for their labor. Without the foresight of the merchant, the good in question would either not have been obtainable at all, or would have only been obtainable at an even higher cost. However, the merchant’s calculations can prove to be erroneous, and in such a case, the merchant has speculated incorrectly. For indeed, one may aptly term this act of calculation "speculation", a realm where the highest degree of self-directed activity prevails, where the merchant owes absolutely nothing to nature and everything to their own foresight. However, once merchants have made arrangements in accordance with their calculations, they naturally desire that events unfold exactly as they had envisioned to avoid financial loss, and so their freedom remains inextricably bound to their merchandise and calculations. Their souls, as Fiesco puts it, are "pressed into bales of Levantine wool." They desire peace so that the stocks they purchased at a low price in anticipation of that very peace might rise again, enabling them to sell them off again at a higher profit, and so on.

##### Finance

Since the merchant now buys in bulk with the aim of reselling what they have bought at the opportune moment, they require a substantial amount of capital to execute such extensive transactions. In the final sales price, they must recoup the interest incurred during the period in which the goods were held in storage. Given that the merchant requires money to purchase all other goods, money must be just as readily available as any other good if the circulation of wealth is to flow as regularly and unobstructed as the circulation of blood. However, the specific section of the commerce that assumes the task of satisfying this particular need is the banker. People with surplus funds lend them to the banker, and those in need of funds borrow them from the banker in exchange for interest, i.e. the use-value of capital. The difference between the interest earned on money lent out and the interest paid on money borrowed constitutes the banker's profit.

Since money that is lent or borrowed passes into the legal ownership of the recipient, this entire business rests upon trust, taking the form of "credit." However, as much as subjective trust requires a objective or tangible form of security, such as a pledge or collateral, the banker's creditors can only feel secure if the banker possesses tangible assets equal in value to the capital entrusted to them. Conversely, the banker themselves is secure only if their own debtors pledge their unsold goods to them as collateral. For credit is required only by those who are unable to realize the full value of their goods at any given moment by converting them directly into cash. If this were not the case, credit would be entirely unnecessary. That capital increasingly facilitates the satisfaction of needs becomes evident from its various forms. If capital is granted to a craftsman, he can continue working even if his last finished piece has not yet been sold, whereas without it, he have to wait until he had obtained the funds to purchase new raw materials through the sale of his existing stock.

### Academia

Finally, the purely intellectual activity directed toward the satisfaction of intellectual needs (i.e. the third and highest level of needs) constitutes the specific domain of the *scholars*. The scholars are workers just as much as the others, for they presuppose the existence of both the natural and artificial needs of the other professions.

#### Healthcare

We begin with the physician who, by virtue of the means they employ, is a scholar of medical science and thus belongs to academia, even if the end they seek to achieve, i.e. health, is a natural need. However, since health constitutes the prerequisite for the satisfaction of all needs, the physician occupies a central position between natural and intellectual human needs. As a result, the work of the physician also extends to the human mind, encompassing the education and ennoblement of the individual. The physician’s motto is, above all, *mens sana in corpore sano* (a sound mind in a sound body). Therefore, the physician should not merely to restore health once it has been lost, but their attention must be also directed even more intently toward preserving it through exercise, therapy and healthy habits in general. While the physician already contributes to the national economy by virtue of the health entrusted to their care maintains the existence of labor, which is the foremost source of well-being for every individual, they go even further by strengthening the health of the public and increasing the population by delaying the onset of old age and death. The national economy must therefore take great care to ensure the provision of healthcare. For the greater the available workforce, the greater the quantity of goods produced to satisfy human needs, and the more easily are those needs met. Nor should one raise the objection here that an increased population merely generates a corresponding increase in consumers. While this is indeed true, the collaborative efforts of a larger group yields more advantageous results than those of a smaller one. Hence, the quantity of goods produced increases geometrically in proportion to the number of workers. Furthermore, the division and socialization of labor also applies to this profession. Whereas among the Greeks, the apothecary, physician, and surgeon were not yet distinguished, the Egyptians, as Herodotus notes, had a physician for every specific body part, we also have specialized doctors for the eyes, teeth, childbirth, and so forth.

#### Art

The labor of the artist presupposes the labor of the physician in that the satisfaction of the need for beauty originates with the body. The drive for beauty, however, is not limited to the beautification of the body. It is in the very nature of this drive to gradually and progressively transforming all sensual needs with the halo of beauty. But in this we can only see the goal of all commercial activity in general, not that of free art. For even the craftsman must not produce the goods solely in such a way that they satisfy the need in question and are therefore useful, but also in such a way that the universally pleasing and appealing is combined with the useful. Fashion in particular seeks to achieve beauty in dwellings, clothing, etc., but the highest merit of luxury is precisely to allow the free art to flourish alongside the beauty of utility. The artist’s true and final task is to satisfy the sense of beauty by depicting the Archetype, presenting truth and goodness to the senses and the imagination to guide for their spirit. The division of labor arises naturally through the various arts, with the notable exception of artistic geniuses, like Michelangelo, who excel at several arts at once. 

#### Religion

If we now wish to see the Archetype (the Idea of ​​the Good, the True, and the Beautiful) not merely presented to the senses, but truly brought to the common understanding of all people, we turn to the religious teacher. To this category of scholars we here, for the first time, assign for the very first time the name of the *cleric*. For they are the first to satisfy a purely spiritual need in a purely spiritual manner. But even in this regard, they render a service only in exchange for service. One requires shelter, another religious solace, and so forth. Everything constitutes an exchange of services that, as in any exchange, must be appraised according to their value. However, I cannot accept the exceptional status that Hegel claims for this profession, as well as for all subsequent professions to which we shall presently turn. "From direct labor," Hegel asserts, "aimed at satisfying material needs, he must be exempted—either by virtue of private wealth or by being indemnified by the State, which lays claim to his activities; such that his private interest finds its fulfillment within his labor for the common good." As if only the so-called "higher estates" labored for the common good! As if all estates were not, in truth, workers in the Lord’s vineyard! As if every individual, precisely by attending to the common good, did not find the fulfillment of their own particular interest!

To the extent that the state, the very concept of which we have not yet even developed, has been presupposed by Hegel, we must limit ourselves to mentioning only those of its current activities that are requisite to enable it to grant a fixed salary to certain workers who cater to intellectual needs, namely, the levying of taxes, even upon those citizens who may have absolutely no desire to satisfy such needs. But this it overturns the fundamental principle that services should be exchanged for services solely by one’s own free choice. Why, then, does the state commence this practice of salaried remuneration only with the clergy? Why do the artist and the physician not receive a salary as well? Is it that the former serves merely to provide luxury? Should the salaries of physicians to be restricted to local medical officers? Why shouldn't *all* professions be salaried, much like Cabet’s state-appointed cook? Therefore, one must either transform all professions into state functionaries, as was the case among the Anabaptists and in Louis Blanc’s National Workshops, or none at all. There can be no question that liberty demands the latter course. The system of state coercion proceeded from the premise that the various professions that satisfied higher needs had to be funded by the state if they were to exist at all, since amidst the sheer struggle for survival during the nascent stages of history, man sought to satisfy only his most indispensable physical needs. However, as soon as the advance of luxury, as well as the culture and refinement that invariably follow in its wake, awakens within man a sensibility for these higher needs, there is absolutely no cause for apprehension that intellectual needs will fail to demand satisfaction with the same intensity as natural ones. "Man shall not live by bread alone," says Christ. Even religion, the earliest of intellectual needs, would flourish all the more naturally if it were left to its own strength.

#### Education

Finally, the same holds true for the profession that satisfies the need for science *as such*, namely, the scientific teacher. According to Aristotle, pure knowledge affords the greatest pleasures. Indeed, he refers to it as "divine bliss," representing the state in which the gratification of human drives has reached its absolute zenith. Everywhere, the phenomenon is observed that the more cultivated a people becomes, the more widely the need for science spreads. The drive for knowledge pervades ever deeper through the population until it has ultimately taken hold of them all. Only once a people has completed the cycle of its development, when the most refined forms of luxury have become indispensable to it, does it also demand, as the ultimate goal of its intellectual evolution, that the highest knowledge, presented in a universally intelligible form, be made as accessible to everyone as the beautiful is in a work of art. But at the same time, it demands that knowledge ceases to be a luxury and becomes a necessity. The more that the products of the drive for knowledge become the common property of all, the more that science, in turn, will contribute to enhancing the refinement of labor dedicated to the satisfaction of all other needs. For instance, the more perfected the science of chemistry becomes, the greater the strides that will be made in manufacturing and agriculture. Through rational industrial diligence, that personal sense of self-worth matures into a fully realized sense of freedom. Truth liberates us primarily for this very reason: because whatever man owes to science, he owes, above all, to himself.

## Wealth

If labor designates the share that each individual holds in the general wealth of society, to the extent that the inequality of abilities is resolved into equality through the principle that each receives according to their contribution, then labor is not the only source of this general wealth. For the bounty of nature, in providing its natural products, constitutes the opposite aspect of wealth. However, in order to completely comprehend this concept, we first have to address the specific contradictions that arise from these two sources of wealth. Even taken in isolation, an original, general contradiction inheres in the very opposition between these two aspects.

If the services rendered to others constitutes the sufficient legal basis for wealth acquired through exchange, then in a state of total isolation, such as that in which Robinson Crusoe lived, or a paradise where nature provides everything without the need for labor, wealth does not exist in the strict sense of the word. In both cases, no product has an exchange value. This by no means implies any lack of satisfaction or use value on part of the very goods that nature bestows, whether offered as a gift or exchanged for labor. It would be one-sided to view wealth exclusively in terms of exchange value or use value. We could more accurately assert that in such a paradise we are absolutely wealthy because nature offers us the means to satisfy our most diverse needs entirely without any cost. 

The self-sufficiency of total isolation and the natural abundance of a paradise are rare exceptions to the general rule of wealth. Indeed, even in the earliest, most primitive forms of society where the refinement of needs has yet occur, every raw material provided by nature underwent some form of processing that endowed it with an exchange value. The more difficult and arduous labor is in the infancy of society, due to a lack of technical skill and industrial development, the more costly it will be. As a result, the goods produced will be expensive yet of poor quality, possessing a low use value but a high exchange value. However, once the obstacles hindering the development of labor have been overcome through the influence of higher levels of skill and education, through the refinement of labor processes, natural forces have been increasingly harnessed to serve mankind. Thanks to this ever-increasing bounty of nature, fewer and fewer human services will suffice to produce the same object of human satisfaction, and to produce it better while incurring lower costs, since equal human services command equal value in exchange. Owing to the gratuitous nature of nature's gifts, exchange value declines in proportion to the extent that human labor can be supplanted by nature. The more the utility of a good increases, therefore, the more its value decreases, use value and exchange value stand in inverse proportion to one another. Natural materials and natural forces, possess no intrinsic value whatsoever, they hold value only to the extent that human labor has been invested in them. Thus, of two products that have the same utility, that one possesses less value is the one in which the arduous labor of man has been more extensively displaced by the gifts of nature.

From this, two entirely different assessments of wealth have appeared. For it is evident that if wealth consists in the quantity of possessions, we are all the richer the more objects of satisfaction we are able to procure for ourselves and can procure all the more of them the less exchange value they possess. Since utility represents absolute wealth, the wealth of the society, while exchange value represents relative wealth, it would seem that Proudhon is right to locate the poverty of the individual in their inability to transform use value into exchange value. The wealthy individual, by virtue of an abundance of money, acquires an abundance of use values ​​in exchange for minimal exchange values, and will thus be able to procure for themselves a wealth of enjoyments. Conversely, the poor individual, precisely because of the minimum exchange values, will be able to obtain far fewer use values since they are unable to convert their possessions into money. Furthermore, as the level of education rises, the means required to satisfy intellectual needs, an area where nature’s gratuitous bounty is less readily available, will possess a higher exchange value than the means required to satisfy sensual needs, where the gifts of nature play a more significant role. Thus, the poor individual will find themselves even less able to procure intellectual enjoyments than sensual ones. In this way, according to Proudhon, the quantity of utilities and values ​​on one side rises ever higher precisely as it falls on the other. A society amidst an excess of wealth, as Hegel observes, is not wealthy enough. How, then, do we resolve this fundamental contradiction that has intruded upon the very concept of wealth? 

Proudhon, initiated into the depths of German dialectics by his friend Grün, regards this opposition as a matter of necessity. Bastiat therefore mocks him, recounting that Proudhon once told him he found Paradise boring because all contradiction would cease to exist there. Indeed, in Paradise, absolute wealth is simultaneously absolute poverty because everyone enjoys the gratuitous utility provided by nature without labor. Bastiat, for his part, seeks to explain away all such opposition as mere appearances. However, one may well find a contradiction to be grounded in the very nature of a thing, yet still not despair of finding a solution to it. This also was not Proudhon’s intention, even though he rarely succeeds in penetrating through to an positive resolution. Though it may also be conceded to Bastiat that harmony, no less than contradiction, is inherent in the very concept of wealth.

Even if we grant Bastiat this doctrine regarding the gratuitous utility of nature, we need not, for that reason alone, deem it worthless in every respect. Robinson derived great utility from his animals, his fruits, and so forth, even if he was unable to convert them into exchange values. Therefore, for the solitary individual, absolute wealth and relative wealth coincide. Since exchange value is generated through exchange, Bastiat regards this exchange as the pure embodiment of property, the individual sphere whereby each person, through increased activity, enhances their own relative wealth. However, since the resources nature contributes to the production benefits everyone gratuitously, Bastiat identifies in this process the true form of communism, not, as Lassalle did, in the diminution of private property. Bastiat says very well that the domain of the community is constantly expanding while the domain of the individual is constantly shrinking, as the diminishing exchange value of property is passed over to the community. Yet this does not preclude the resolution of the dichotomy between exchange value and use value. As use values ​​rise uniformly for society as a whole, the decline in exchange values ​​is compensated for each individual by the fact that they now can produce a greater quantity of goods with greater ease. As a result, while the aggregate sum of values ​​has not diminished as the aggregate sum of utilities has increased, for a larger quantity of goods now possesses the same total value that a smaller quantity held previously. Therefore, absolute and relative wealth are brought into harmony within the society.

Finally, the greater the education required by the various professions of society, the greater the means must be produced to satisfy intellectual needs. Likewise, the more easily and affordably these means will be procurable through the general dissemination of education, and so the contrast between mental and physical labor, between education and the lack thereof, between poverty and wealth, will vanish. In this way, the divisions in education on basis of profession will also vanish. "Equality, Liberty, and Fraternity," Bastiat aptly observes, "do not exist in the state of nature, but are the product of society." The social order advances from primordial equality, passing through the stark inequality of the intermediate stages, toward a final equality in the possession of well-being and truth. Though this general resolution of the conflict between collective and individual wealth does not spare us from encountering new contradictions at every juncture, for the collective wealth of society cannot be conceived of independently of the wealth of the individual.

### Land

The first source of wealth that presents itself is the land, the universal potential that nature provides for generating the materials to satisfy our needs. While in our previous discussion, we deduced from the concept of free personality the transition, originally posited by Kant, from a primordial communal ownership of land to individual ownership through acts of appropriation, and conversely had to reject the proposal of a provisional distribution of land to cultivators by the authorities as a form of state tutelage over the citizenry, labor now furnishes us with the most compelling justification for land ownership. Though at first, the very opposite seems to follow from this premise. For if the right to acquire land is conferred only by the worker’s act of shaping and cultivating it, then it is the worker, not the landowner, who ought to be recognized as the rightful owner. Thus, we immediately arrive at the fundamental conflict between the landowner and the worker over who constitutes the legitimate owner.

The solution to this apparently difficult question is quite simple. Everyone possesses an equal right to be the first to cultivate land that is uncultivated. Yet, whoever is the first to do so thereby acquires the right to exclude anyone else from it. The utility offered by nature is, and remains, the common property of all, a point Proudhon emphasizes when he asserts that the property owner wrongfully demands payment for nature's gratuitous gifts. However, while the property owner is certainly paid for their labor, they were nonetheless the first to apply their labor to elicit the services of nature, and with the ownership of the former, they acquired ownership of the latter as well. It was their labor that rendered nature's gifts accessible, since the field does not yield a harvest of its own accord. If I have plowed the field and erected buildings and barns upon it, then I also possess the right to sell the grain. Yet acquiring the grain itself costs nothing to me, the only cost is the labor I expended to exchange it. Nor does the soil itself cost anything. "The government of Arkansas," observes Bastiat, "sold agricultural land for one dollar. That is indeed cheap, but where does the dollar come from? It would appear that even a dollar is too much to pay, given that no one has done anything to cultivate the land. The dollar represents the purchase price for the government's guarantee of the security of the acquisition." But why then, following the first landowner, cannot a second worker proceed in exactly the same manner? This is because the first owner, when selling their grain, does not exchange the substance of the entire service (the act of cultivation) for another service, or for its equivalent value in money, but exchanges only the value of the use of their service. Through the labor they have invested in the land, they acquire a return not only in the form of grain, but for all time to come. Even if the landowner is not currently performing manual labor themselves, they are still compensated for by the labor of previous owner, with whom they constitute a single continuous entity, i.e. the formation of past labor. More than that, the landowner must renew their labor every year, they continually invest even more labor into their land, thereby improving and increasing its yields, an advantage for themselves that is also to the advantage of all. Thus, they do not receive their rent, "their capital profit," as Lassalle somewhat dismissively terms it, gratuitously from nature, as most economists would have it. However, because the gratuitous utility of nature is here most strikingly apparent, landowners were the very first to face the accusation that they exact payment for the gifts of God, and thus their property rights were the first to be undermined, just as agrarian peoples were themselves easily conquered.

However, all industrialized nations originally began with simple land ownership and agriculture, and even viewed these as the primary source of wealth. Even Adam Smith still leans toward this view, regarding the annual produce of a country’s land and farm labor as its true wealth, and likewise considers agriculture to be the most productive form of labor. For this reason, it was held that the landowners should constitute the ruling class. Since large-scale farming, which may be likened to a machine, is more productive than small-scale farming, it would follow from this premise that a nation’s greatest wealth would be found in a system where the land is concentrated in the hands of a few large proprietors, the nobility, while the mass of the people serve them as dependents, tenants, or day laborers. This is, indeed, the phenomenon we observe at the inception of every nation’s development, especially in the case of agrarian states. Contrary to appearances, however, this state of affairs by no means represents the greatest possible wealth. First, the mass of the people exists in an unfree state of dependence, whether in the form of serfdom or wage-labor. Second, they also lack education, as large-scale farming simplifies the labor required by each. Lastly, the labor performed is not self-motivated because they know they cannot earn more through more diligent labor. All these factors serve to render labor less productive.

The owner of a vast tract of land often does not know what to do with it. They usually cultivate only a small portion and the large portion he utilized as pasture, or perhaps leaves it be altogether. Another portion serves as woodland dedicated to the "noble" (though not particularly lucrative) "passion" of hunting. Since agrarian economy in its stagnant state does not advance, it contributes little to the accumulation of wealth, so its conditions must be abandoned. While the agrarian economy has potential for the development of large-scale farming, this potential remains unrealized because industrial diligence, the division of labor, and all the attendant forces of technical progress have not yet appeared. This progress becomes possible only when a greater number of the populace become landowners, taking a far deeper interest in their own property than they would have they just cultivating the lands of their masters. The greater the number of landowners independently cultivating their own land, the higher the daily wages will rise for the large landowner, as fewer laborers remain available to serve them. With the ever-advancing subdivision of land taking place among all nations, all of them will, to a greater or lesser extent experience their own struggles over agrarian laws, much like the Romans did. Then the farmer will undertake improvements, and agriculture, too, will be swept up into the current of progress. 

The endless fragmentation of land ownership has indeed fulfilled this legal imperative we advocated, but at the expense of the general prosperity of the masses. Through this very excess, it has produced an outcome diametrically opposed to the one we previously posited. For the very fact that land ownership has passed into the hands of too many individuals has caused it to become fragmented to such an extent that it was no longer sufficient to sustain any owner, causing the creation of a rural proletariat. The resulting paradox is this: while the technical progress agriculture, through the improvement of large-scale farming methods, rendered the land that remained in the hands of large estate owners ever more productive, the smallholder, through the fragmentation of inheritances and similar processes, fell into ever-deeper decline, until they ultimately reverted to the status of a day laborers, as soon as their parcel of land was no longer large enough to sustain even a single cow. This constitutes the particular contradiction confronting the national wealth, specifically with regard to land ownership, in the present day. The question now arises: how do we resolve this dilemma? How do we reconcile prosperity and liberty, forces that are currently locked into conflict, to bring them into harmony? As of now, we do not have everything we need to prove this, that is, to demonstrate the possibility that the growth of the national wealth can simultaneously result in the enrichment of every individual citizen.

### Production

Secondly, if we turn to products, on the one hand it consists of the raw materials supplied by nature, wherein human labor is only preparatory and extractive, and on the other hand, of the products of industry, wherein human labor constitutes the entirety of the value, albeit still applied to a raw material, until finally, the movement of exchange itself, that is, circulation, creates new values. Here as well, it will become evident that labor alone is remunerated.

#### Raw Materials

Firstly, regarding raw materials, one might accuse nature of the injustice that she offers one people such an abundance of resources over other peoples, that she supplies these very same products to one people with less effort and in better quality than to another. If southern fruits thrive less well in the North than in the South, then the southern peoples possess a greater wealth in this respect. But we already know that this constitutes absolute wealth, the wealth that belongs to the community and benefits everyone without requiring them to pay for it. The English would fall short of the Spaniards only if, for instance, they obstinately insisted on cultivating oranges as artificial hothouse plants, at great cost and effort, only to find them sour and inedible. They would be pointlessly squandering their labor-power, whereas they could purchase these fruits cheaply in Spain, where they grow naturally and require comparatively less effort on the part of the workers who gather them. The same fate would befall the southern peoples if, instead of procuring coal from the English and tallow from the Russians, they attempted to produce these goods domestically. Such goods, as Adam Smith rightly observes, should not be manufactured oneself, so that one’s labor-power can be more efficiently applied to other branches of industry. Since, in the case of any good, only the labor expended in its creation is ever paid for, every nation is precisely as wealthy as it deserves to be by virtue of its labor. The greater the exchange value a nation generates through its labor, the greater the quantity of foreign goods from other lands it can acquire in exchange for its own goods. Thus, they acquire the utility of nature from every climate as part of the bargain. For, as industry is compared against industry, the greater bounty of nature is of no avail to a people. Indeed, it can even prove detrimental to them, and herein lies the contradiction to which we are led by the wealth inherent in a nation’s natural resources. For the nature of the land requires less exertion to yield its riches to man in the first place. 

The fact that the bounty of nature can become a source of poverty for a people was demonstrated particularly in Spain following the discovery of the Americas. Precious metals, so it is said, are the source of the greatest wealth. For since they are used as currency, and have thus become the standard by which the value of all things is measured, whoever possesses them can acquire every object capable of satisfying their desires, and thereby has the greatest wealth of all. But what is it that renders gold so precious? The very same circumstance that renders the diamond so. By itself, gold is worthless, yet because gold is so rare, the labor involved in searching for it is immense, and this immense labor is compensated at a very high rate for the finder. Thus, when vast quantities of gold were discovered in Peru and Brazil, the necessary labor needed to mine it diminished, and the price of gold consequently fell. The more gold the Spaniards imported into Europe, Montesquieu observed, the higher the prices of all other goods rose. In other words, the Spaniards grew progressively poorer in terms of exchange value the more natural wealth they accumulated. This stands as a striking example of the inherent contradiction contained within natural goods. The use-value of gold lies in its function as a medium of exchange. However, an increase in its quantity diminishes its exchange value without simultaneously increasing its use-value. A smaller amount of money serves the same purpose as a larger amount, since the lesser amount simply represents a greater quantity of other goods. So when the task at hand is to assess the wealth of a nation, the crucial question becomes this: do exchange values ​​fall because natural utilities have increased, or because there is a greater quantity of money in circulation? In the case of the Spaniards, the latter was true, and thus in comparison to other nations, instead of growing richer, they became ever poorer the more they were awash with gold.

#### Products

The second source of wealth is the product, the form that urban industry, the artisan and the manufacturer, imparts to raw materials. For these professions, the aspect of wealth derived from the gratuitous utility of nature recedes into the background. Instead, we are concerned primarily with the other aspect, i.e. the exertion of power embodied in human labor. If only the labor devoted to production is remunerated, and if this factor is significantly more important in industry than in agriculture, then it becomes clear that industry constitutes a greater source of wealth than agriculture. Nations typically begin with agriculture, only to turn as their level of culture rises and their wealth grows—toward the industrial system, which then itself becomes a source of even greater wealth. Colbert transformed France from an agrarian nation into an industrial one, laying the foundation for its subsequent flourishing. As soon as the initially impoverished middle classes, enriched through industrial labor, succeed in breaking free from their dependence upon the nobility, participation in the benefits of society becomes more widespread. Adam Smith sees the preponderance of trade and industry in the unfavorable situation of the countryside, in contrast to the privileges of the cities, rather than it lying in the nature of the matter. Since agriculture cannot accumulate as many products because they spoil more easily, it also incurs more costs, improves more slowly, is exposed to greater dangers, etc., than the work of industry.

However, right at the very inception of this wealth, enhanced as it is by productivity, we are confronted with a fundamental contradiction. Since the value of a service rendered depends upon the quantity and quality of the labor expended, such that services are exchanged only when they are in equilibrium in this regard (whereby quantity and quality are be balanced against one another), it follows that the more productive forms of labor possesses a greater value. Thus, since the growth of industrial system results in an increase in the value of labor, the daily wage of the rural worker diminishes in comparison to their urban counterpart. However, if this increasing productivity aims at ever-greater enhancements to the utility of nature while simultaneously reducing the demand for human labor, then industry appears to be self-destructive, devaluing the very wealth that labor produced. This contradiction, however, is only an apparent one. Because the necessary human labor is reduced through greater productivity, the utility of nature increases, and the absolute wealth of the community increases with it. Even if wages fall, the relative wealth of the wage-earner rises as better and more abundant goods become available, goods that are now able to be purchased at a lower price. Yet, were we to examine in greater detail the accumulation of wealth resulting from the specific, incremental advances of productivity, we would no longer be able to speak of apparent contradictions, but that they are intrinsic to the nature of the matter itself.

##### Division of Labor

As the division of labor advances, the worker sinks to the level of an ever more insignificant cog in the industrial machine. On the one hand, the complexity of the task that each worker performs is reduced, eliminating the need to switch between tasks, but on the other hand, their work becomes ever more insignificant, mindless, and stultifying, precisely because it is increasingly fragmented. The more the craft advances, the more the craftsman declines. Likewise, the greater the wealth and enjoyment of the purchaser of industrial products, the more miserable becomes the lot of the producer, through whom the purchaser partakes of all these satisfactions. Who gives a thought to the craftsman whose trade has gone out of fashion and who has, as a consequence, been thrown out of work? When wax candles replace tallow candles, then oil replaces wax, and later gas takes the place of oil, the worn-out worker is cast aside like a squeezed-out orange peel. Indeed, some economists even teach that one must simply let things take their course, and that anyone who has fallen behind in the current must pull themselves up by their own bootstraps or else perish completely. But how exactly are they supposed accomplish the former? Through the dulling induced by the very nature of their work, the laborer has been reduced to a beast of burden, and the spark of divine likeness, which was naturally present within them, has been utterly extinguished by society.

When other economists propose artificial wage increases to improve the conditions of the workers of one industry, they entangle themselves in even greater contradictions. For an artificial wage increase implies giving some individuals more in exchange than the value of their services, thereby robbing others of the opportunity to acquire this very sum. Moreover, even this measure would ultimately be of no avail to those whom it was intended to benefit. For an artificially mandated increase in the value of one type of service drive up the values ​​of all others as well. One would then be compelled to commit yet another injustice by issuing yet another arbitrary decree to forbid this consequence. Thus, political economy devolves into a state of slavery and a war of all against all. And if it is proposed that workers be uplifted through education, the all-encompassing demands of mechanical labor leaves them with no time left over for such education. It is saddest of all to observe how such higher education is immediately denied to the next generation, who are forced at an early age into the workforce to support their parents in earning a livelihood, wasting away physically, intellectually, and morally, within the confines of the factories. The increase in wealth, prosperity, and education generated by the division of labor on the one hand thus brings with it, on the other hand, poverty, misery and vacuity in ever-increasing measure, as the chasm between the haves and the have-nots grows ever wider.

##### Machinery

The machine was originally invented to relieve man of undignified labor, rendering the forces of nature subservient to the human spirit at a much higher degree. On the one hand, the labor that is saved by machinery leaves the worker with more time to cultivate themselves as an individual, and on the other hand, it enriches everyone by enhancing the utility of nature. Since more is produced with fewer expenses, lower prices allow a greater number of consumers to partake in the enjoyment of goods. But this very source of wealth one and the same source of unspeakable poverty, the former for the factory owner and the latter for the worker, specifically because the introduction of machines diminishes both labor and wages. The benefit conferred by machinery accrues initially to the factory owner, as they are freed from their dependence on their workers. Conversely, labor has fallen into a state of absolute dependence upon the owner of the machine. That which the worker was once able to provide to their employer through their own manual skill, the employer has now acquired as their own property through the machine. Rather than relieving the worker of undignified labor, the machine has diminished the quality of that labor alongside its quantity. The manufacturer requires fewer workers, and those who remain are compelled to perform labor of an even more undignified nature, reducing them to mere handmaidens of the sophisticated machine. Furthermore, the superior ingenuity embodied in the invention of the machine is credited to the mind of the manufacturer and is remunerated as a consequence. The diminished labor that remains for the manual workers is compensated at a lower rate, and as a consequence, one half of their number are cast out of employment, while the other suffers a decline in prosperity. 

The initial effect of the introduction of machinery was, therefore, that workers protested and revolted against their introduction. But they soon realized that the increased production (the reduction in labor notwithstanding) actually demanded even more labor than before. Workers had the opportunity to work again in newly emerging manufactories, but it was much less remunerative. The factory owner, despite the lower cost of his goods, continues to profit even more, for they can sell an even greater volume of goods and drive down their workers' wages. The workers, however, derive no benefit from this arrangement, neither from the relative wealth of their employer nor from the absolute wealth of the community. The only conceivably favorable outcome for them would be if, through perfected inventions in all other branches of labor, all goods were to become cheaper across the board. But this outcome is just as unlikely to happen all at once as it is improbable to happen in each sector of industry, for it would only cause overproduction and scarcity in one and the same instance. "No fraternity can exist," exclaims Proudhon, "between creatures as the division of labor and machinery have made them."

##### Competition

The more the collective strives for greater wealth, the fiercer that competition becomes, eventually degenerating into a war of individuals against one another. While it cannot be denied that this possesses the positive aspect of technical progress, it comes at highly counterproductive and inevitable cost, exacerbating the misery of the vanquished as the prosperity of the victors are enhanced. For as each individual seeks to produce goods that are more marketable and less expensive in order to increase sales, the one who outpaces the others in this endeavor grows ever wealthier. Meanwhile, the producer unable to sell their less marketable or more expensive wares sinks ever deeper into poverty. Proudhon characterizes this competition as an "auction to the lowest bidder." Were one to attempt to curb the surplus generated by the drive for lower prices by halting production, the resulting harm would fall most heavily upon both the worker and the factory owner: the former would be left without a livelihood, while the latter would see their capital yield no return.

Even if society as a whole were willing to tolerate all of this, even if the manual laborer could sink no lower, the deepening poverty would eventually seize the manufacturers themselves. The very accumulation of wealth causes poverty to ascend into ever-higher strata of society. As the worker succumbs to the capitalist who, by virtue of their financial power, is able to acquire machines, and as the larger capitalist, operating with greater resources, in turn triumphs over the smaller capitalist: competition ultimately destroys itself, just as Louis Blanc predicted. The victor remains alone upon the battlefield until they are compelled to renew the struggle with the appearance of new competitors. The longer this battle continues, the more combatants are incapacitated, and the more the victor's wealth is augmented. As long as they know themselves to be without rivals, they can raise prices once again.

This truce is, in a sense, sanctified by the exclusive right of sale, for it elevates the victor in the competitive struggle, for a certain period, above the disadvantages inherent in that struggle, rendering them the absolute master of the situation. A healthy economy must not neglect such a prolific source of wealth, and all the less so, given that this prerogative extends its dominion over agriculture, industry, commerce, and all else. On the other hand, all professions suffer as a result, and once the renewed rivalry has blunted the edge of this exclusive right, the damage has already been done. It appears impossible to prevent the rise of poverty alongside the rise of wealth. From this bleak paradox we are cast into the formative labor of industry.

#### Circulation

Could it be that the circulation brought about by the mercantile profession offers a remedy for this? At first glance, this does indeed appear to be possible. For since the merchant no longer deals, or at least not exclusively, with raw materials that possess no inherent value, but rather with the values ​​embodied in services, the intermediary figure situated between producer and consumer, whom we call the merchant, supplies us with nothing but new values. Firstly, the mediation of commerce spares us a great deal of effort in exchanging products across locations, and secondly, it lends support to industriousness by stimulating consumers through the sheer volume of products available, and by encouraging producers to exchanging their products for others. thus, as a creator of products and a facilitator of exchange, it contributes immensely to the advancement of wealth. Although commerce produces nothing by itself, its labor is by no means unproductive. Indeed, the distinction between "productive" and "unproductive" labor appears to be untenable. Even the counsel provided by a physician or a jurist is productive, for it lightens our burden of labor by safeguarding our health or our property. The performance of a musical piece does the very same, for by offering us recreation, it restores our strength for work. Even the activity of the soldier is by no means unproductive, to the extent that the armed forces establishes security, provided of course, that the quantity of soldiers does not become a burdensome expense. However, if we stated that the merchants constitute the intermediaries, then strictly speaking, since no one creates matter, all producers are intermediaries between nature and the consumer.

##### Transportation

Now we can examine the specific manner in which trade appears to confer these benefits. First, trade causes the surplus of goods that exists at one point, and the lack of goods arising at another, to vanish through a regulated circulation that extends across the entire globe. The faster this transport is effected, the faster that excessive prices are eliminated. Ease of communication thus significantly increases wealth, for it swiftly supplies a deprived population with necessities and counteracts the impoverishment of producers by opening up new markets. The construction of railways, highways, and roads in general appears to be, without question, an effective means of increasing wealth, so it would seem there are no irreconcilable contradictions. Yet, this only seems to be the case. When the means of transportion are greatly improved and multiplied, goods can be conveyed with ease even to places where no shortage is yet felt, dumping products into a location that could not have otherwise occurred so readily. In short, the dichotomy between abundance and scarcity is generated by the very fact that competition extends freely from place to place, from country to country, and spreads across the entire globe. Thus, for instance, small towns experience a palpable shortage of food and other products ever since railways began channeling everything toward the capital cities.

##### Trade

This unfettered competition, which through unrestricted circulation, opens up all countries to everyone, is what constitutes free trade. It is the great question among the scholars of political economy whether it should be permitted unconditionally, or whether the well-being of nations does not, on the contrary, demand its restriction.

Unrestricted free trade, that is, external trade with all nations, seems to present itself immediately as the most natural state of affairs. For if the gifts of nature are bestowed gratuitously, but each nation can only produce certain types of goods while having to forgo the production of others, then the diversity of products across nations serves as a signal to nations to trade with each other. Without it, we would have to do without the unique products of foreign countries, since it cannot reasonably be expected of us to accept any insipid imitation of a product that is not one of its kind. Free trade, conversely, offers every nation the opportunity to acquire the riches of anywhere in the world at minimal cost, since goods are invariably procured from the places where they can be produced most easily. Moreover, through unrestricted free trade, every nation is able to realize the greatest value from the goods it produces itself, since the entire globe stands open to it. Thus, external trade constitutes the most prolific source of national wealth, and through this exchange, no party suffers a loss. One sells one’s own goods at a higher price abroad, while purchasing foreign goods at a lower cost. A Russian, for instance, can sell tallow to a Frenchman at a higher price than he would to a fellow Russian since, even at this elevated rate, the price appears cheap to the French relative to what it would cost them to produce the tallow domestically (and the case of French literature, the converse applies). Thus, all nations gain from the difference between more expensive and cheaper goods because this difference applies to various products. Trade is always advantageous to both nations, if only for the simple reason that mutual advantage would be impossible otherwise. Foreign trade unites all nations into a single society, extends the division of labor and competition to all of them, conserves much of their energy, and serves as the most powerful lever for their cultural development. There is no question that, for this very reason, that global trade is vindicated and contributes to a nation's prosperity to a greater degree than agriculture. Even Adam Smith conceded that the agrarian economy ultimately leads to the decline of industry and commerce, whereas the commercial system elevates agriculture to its highest level.

Yet, this rosy view of free trade represents only one side of the matter. We cannot escape the converse reality that foreign trade, while an undeniable source of wealth, is also a source of unspeakable poverty. Should we truly admit, freely and without hindrance, all goods from all nations, including the very types that we naturally produce domestically? We can further consider the scenario in which a foreign nation, by virtue of its greater accumulated wealth, is able to procure superior machinery and more sophisticated tools. In such a case, our domestic producers would be completely unable to compete with their foreign counterparts, and our own labor force would be completely paralyzed. Not only would manufacturers be reduced to destitution, but the thousands of workers previously employed in their factories would face irretrievable ruin, even if the aggregate wealth of consumers were to increase. Unrestricted free trade, many therefore argue, Proudhon among them, would be an act of national suicide, and result in the utter destruction of domestic industry. At this juncture, there are even some, such as Fichte, who go so far as to advocate the diametrically opposed system of a "closed commercial state," viewing it as the ultimate archetype of national perfection, for it is precisely by barring all foreign products from entering national borders that incentivizes the domestic workforce to produce everything for themselves. We can grant them the advantage that shielding the domestic workforce from foreign competition can enrich the nation as a whole. Specifically regarding domestic goods that are absolutely incapable of competing with corresponding foreign goods, it would seem that an unconditional ban on such imports would be needed. Yet, precisely in the case of such goods, this measure presents significant drawbacks. For if nature has denied us the ability to produce fine Swiss cheese and oranges, the inferior and expensive domestic substitutes could easily lead the populace to avoid purchasing such goods altogether, and so import ban would fail to increase the wealth of the producers as well. Indeed, even if the self-isolated nation did not abstain from these pleasures, it would still be impoverished simply by having to pay a higher price for goods that it could purchase more cheaply abroad.

According to this view, only the branches of industry may be cultivated from which we can hope that, in time, and utilizing the same amount of labor, they will yield a return equal to that offered by other nations. At least in the case of nascent industries, it appears economically justified to support them through import bans until they are able to compete against foreign rivals. Otherwise, the tender shoots of such industrial enterprise would be immediately stifled by unrestricted free trade. However, the political economist must carefully consider the implications of their actions, for an immediate contradiction arises: how is a nascent industry to gain strength if it lacks the incentive to compete in the first place? Therefore, Ganilh argues, one should not resort to an absolute prohibition so that healthy competition is not excluded, but this branch of industry should be supported and protected by the state so that it gradually learns to tolerate foreign competition from abroad. Hence the rule arose that if a government was so terrible that it did not support domestic labor at all, it had to eliminate competition with foreign countries through prohibitions. A good government gradually brings domestic labor out of the hothouse and into the open air, granting them an advantage over foreign industry through moderate protections, until it can rely entirely upon its own strength.

The contradiction inherent to protective tariffs is, however, immediately apparent. Either the tariff is so high that foreign goods become more expensive than domestic ones, in which case, assuming the goods are of equal quality, the protective tariff amounts to an outright prohibition, and so all the benefits of foreign trade, including competition as a source of wealth, are completely lost. Or the tariff is so low that foreign goods are still imported, in which case the protective tariff failed to achieve its objective, but without shedding the objectionable character of inequitable wealth redistribution, a character inherent in the fact that it invariably transfers the tax on foreign goods from the pockets of consumers into those of the state or the producers. Furthermore, since in our modern states the large-scale factory owners are situated far closer to the legislative power than the consumers, they will tend to set protective tariffs too high rather than too low. They will not gradually lower them to the point where a strengthened domestic industry can withstand completely unfettered competition, but will instead constantly claim that "the time has not yet come." As a result, this specific branch of industry, having no competitors, falls into stagnation, and without striving for improvement, dumps goods of inferior quality and higher price onto consumers, goods that they could have otherwise purchased more cheaply and of better quality abroad. Smuggling will render such flawed laws partially ineffective, though it does so, on the one hand, at the expense of ethicality, and on the other, to the detriment of the state, which had sought to benefit from these very tariffs.

We have hitherto cited two reasons why it seems highly advisable to exclude foreign trade, the one was consideration for domestic workers, the other stemmed from the nature of the goods and the branch of industry in question. A third determining factor is that we would be impoverished if we placed ourselves in too great a state of dependence upon foreign nations by sending too much money out of the country to purchase the products of foreign labor. "The balance of trade," it is said, "would turn to our disadvantage; rather, we must ensure that the scales tip in our favor; if we sold more than we bought, we would grow ever richer." It is almost as if the abundance of money a nation possesses doesn't constitute its wealth, as if the abundance of gold that Spain accumulated had not actually brought about its impoverishment. Money serves purely as a standard of measurement between two services rendered, so where money is plentiful, goods become expensive. "It is therefore immaterial," observes Montesquieu, "how much money is present; it invariably represents the entire aggregate of goods." If money flows into a country to such an extent that the country possesses an excess of it, it becomes cheap within that country and naturally flows back to where it is scarce. Although money, as Count Verri notes, is the universal commodity, its value is measured by all the commodities it represents, just as money, in turn, measures their value. Thus, the equilibrium between buying and selling in foreign trade, and consequently between imports and exports, establishes itself spontaneously. A nation can only buy as much as it sells because it exchanges services of equal value, and if a nation discovers this same value within its own borders, it will no longer seek that good abroad. "Exchange," says Bastiat, "reduces the ratio of labor input to gratification. Exchange will cease when the satisfactions obtained through the labor of a foreign nation become less than those yielded by an equal amount of domestic labor. If the labor of one nation amounts to 12 units, while that of another, because it is able to harness more gratuitous natural forces, amounts to 8, then the first nation will continue to import until the costs of the exchange reach the figure of 4. From the moment this occurs, exchange will cease, without the need for much legislative intervention." Yet, even if imports and exports must invariably maintain a balance, one nation can still become wealthier than another. For the capital of a society increases when the exchange value of its annual output exceeds that of its annual consumption. However, the equilibrium between output and consumption is entirely distinct from that between imports and exports.

Unrestricted free trade and protection (or prohibition, since without preventing imports, protection is ineffective) have, in the view of several economists, proven to be two opposing extremes that, in terms of their harmfulness, hold one another in equilibrium such that, regardless of whichever extreme is embraced, the inevitability of human misery persists whenever the result was expected to bring wealth. This time, contrary to his usual habit, Proudhon does not stop at this negative opposition, nor does he only hint indirectly, as he otherwise tends to do, at the positive solution. Instead, on this occasion and by way of exception, he claims, in a very Hegelian fashion, to have himself grasped the very point of convergence in the differential tariff. It would certainly seem that all nations could enter into commercial exchange and competition with one another simply by adding the principle of equality to that of liberty. But on the other hand, the very same drawbacks persist. In the absence of tariffs, goods produced in a country that is better favored by nature or more advanced in its industry could be purchased at a lower price. A differential tariff would eliminate competition in precisely the same manner as a protective tariff does. For if working conditions in another country improves, such that the competitive spirit, previously stifled by the differential tariff, could once again surge forth, then the tariff would then have to be modified, and if this is not done, the tariff ceases to be useful without ceasing to be unjust. Thus, Proudhon has not truly resolved the contradiction. In this same predicament finds themselves all statesmen, and indeed economic scholarship itself, who seek to strike a compromise between absolute free trade and a complete ban on imports.

The positive conclusion to be drawn from this dialectic of the tariff system is this: that none of the three aforementioned types of tariffs should be permitted in any national economy, not even in the case of goods where domestic producers seek to compete with foreign producers. Yet, where such a rivalry is impossible, such as the import of tropical fruits into northern countries, one might assume that even a staunchly protectionist government would advocate for unrestricted free trade. However, such a government has become so accustomed to collecting revenue that it imposes a tariff even upon the satisfaction of these particular needs. Since it protects nothing by doing so, it designates such a levy a "revenue tariff", taxing a specific category or kind of good to generate income, yet fails to consider that it is once again redistributing of wealth in an inequitable way, to the extent that funds intended to serve the satisfaction of the community's collective needs are paid for by a narrow selection of inhabitants, specifically those who happen to require the very goods burdened by this revenue tariff. Thus, even here, the enrichment of a few occurs at the expense of others. This is the fundamental contradiction that manifests itself in every form of indirect taxation. Higher taxes, it is argued, stimulates a nation's productivity because greater efforts must be exerted to raise the necessary funds, and the greater a nation's taxable capacity, the wealthier it is said to be. This is true enough, but the more that one takes from individuals through taxation, the poorer they become, and surely one would not want to apply to the wealth of a nation the description of a hole ("what grows larger the more you take away from it?") 

However, since we have now touched upon the relationship between public finance and national wealth, I cannot refrain from also citing the specific contradictions that arise when one attempts to define the various forms of taxation more precisely. A sound national economy, it is argued, must not tax luxury items, for since increasing luxury is a primary source of increasing wealth, taxing it would cause this very source to run dry. Thus, one shifts from luxury taxes to consumption taxes. Everyone eats bread, meat, and so forth. It therefore seems equitable that these items be taxed, because such taxation affects everyone equally. But here, equality in taxation is, in fact, the very opposite of justice. For a poor person requires just as much bread as a rich person, and is compelled to pay just as much in taxes as the rich person who could afford to pay several times more (which is also why poll taxes are unjust). Furthermore, the poor person actually consumes more bread, precisely because he partakes of fewer luxury delicacies, so they ends up paying even more than the rich person. Added to this is the fact that consumption taxes, imposed specifically to avoid burdening the privileged class of luxury consumers, are levied upon the most basic necessities, simply because it is the rich who enact the tax laws. Pork, for instance, is taxed more heavily than game meat, and so on. In short, this tax legislation has consistently made the rich even richer and the poor even poorer. And so it is the very pinnacle of perversity to tax labor, the very source of all wealth, such as it is done through trade taxes, placing obstacles in its path, thereby rendering it less productive.

##### Finance

If exchange constitutes such a great source of wealth, it follows that the very medium that renders all exchange possible, i.e. money, must itself be a great source of wealth, to the extent that it is brought into existence through the labor of the banker. Despite the bitter experience that Spain had with their gold, the individual still cannot be dissuaded from the belief that whoever possesses a lot of money possesses a lot of wealth. Although we have already described the general principles of exchange in our earlier doctrine of property, a deeper examination of these concepts are nonetheless called for here. At this juncture, building upon this foundation, we intend to construct our theory of wealth and analyze its inherent contradictions.

Since possessing a lot of money is not synonymous with being wealthy, the question naturally arises: in what way does money serve as a source of wealth? The primary answer to this is because money facilitates exchange. Money functions as an *object* in the same way that the merchant functions as a *person*. Exchange is simplified by replacing the original activity of barter with the transaction of buying and selling, a "compound exchange involving two factors," as Say puts it, thereby increasing wealth through this very facilitation. Just as the merchant inserts themselves between the consumer and the producer in order to pay the latter the value of the goods they have received from the former, so too is money the *silent* intermediary which, instead of merely comparing two distinct use-values in the abstract realm of the imagination, actually renders their respective values ​​concrete and manifest. If I had boots but needed beer, I would, in a primitive economy, go to my neighbor the brewer and trade my boots for beer. However, if I also needed clothing, I would then have to proceed to the tailor to inquire whether he would be willing to give me clothes in exchange for beer, and so on. However, if I convert my boots into money, my buyers have in turn acquired that money only because they have rendered me a service in exchange for it. Thus, the act of buying always resolves itself into a series of simple exchanges. By obtaining money in exchange for my goods, I come to possess the realized value of all manner of use-values, sparing myself an infinite number of separate transactions that would otherwise consume at least half of my working time. However, the contradiction lies in the fact that, while money is intended merely to facilitate the exchange of goods, it measures the value of all goods while itself fluctuates in value, and that an abundance of money, which depresses its value, is free from adverse consequences only when an abundance of goods increases in equal measure. From this, viewed from yet another angle, it becomes apparent the fallacy of the trade policy that attempts to sell more than one buys in order to tip a nation's balance of trade in its own favor. For the inevitable result of such a strategy would be to make money more plentiful and goods scarcer and more expensive as a result.

From this arises the very question of whether I become richer through buying or through selling. The more I purchase all manner of different goods, so as to satisfy the vast diversity of my needs, the more my absolute wealth increases because I able to procure for myself any conceivable means of satisfaction. This is the economic principle of the spendthrift, considering themselves all the richer the more that they spend. Economists who endorse this proposition therefore view luxury as the greatest promoter of wealth. However, once the spendthrift has run out of money, they will be forced to content themselves only with satisfying the needs for which they have already secured the means. They still suffer from want if they have not made the adequate provision for every contingency, including future needs that have yet to arise, or if their existing treasury is depleted. The miser, conversely, adheres to the exact opposite principle, selling as much as possible so that their treasury always remains full. They keep before their eyes the possibility of procuring for themselves, at any given moment, ever-newer and the very latest pleasures, yet they never grant themselves these indulgences. The difference between the spendthrift and the miser lies in the distinction between possibility and actuality. The spendthrift *actually* enjoys life, whereas the miser only has the *possibility* of doing so. To the extent that the actual, according to Aristotle, ranks so much higher than mere possibility, it would seem that the spendthrift is *more* prudent than the miser, which is another reason why extravagance is the lesser vice. While the spendthrift possesses absolute wealth, deriving actual enjoyment from the goods themselves, the miser revels in relative wealth, constantly conceiving of their labor purely in terms of its monetary value. But just as the spendthrift loses the very possibility of absolute wealth as their relative wealth diminishes, so too does relative wealth, in the hands of the miser, vanish into nothingness as a worthless object, precisely through their disdain for the absolute wealth, i.e. its utilization. As for the utility that both provide to others, the spendthrift distributes money among the people in terms of its substance, while the miser distributes their utility by securing interest through loans. The truth, much as it is for foreign trade, lies in finding the mean between expenditure and income. However, as Aristotle observes, this mean is relative, and in this context, it is defined by the principle that one should spend the larger portion of one’s income to truly satisfy one’s needs, while saving the smaller portion in reserve, so as to be able, whenever necessary, to meet future needs that inevitably arise but could not have been planned for in advance.

If noble metals were chosen to serve as the measure of value, the reason lies in the fact that their value, due to their rarity, was regarded as not only high, but also stable. And broadly speaking, notwithstanding certain major or minor fluctuations (such as when gold mines are discovered here and there from time to time), this is generally the case. However, the amount of labor that was expended in the minting of coins and in their transport from one place to another during the course of trade, is precisely the same amount that is deducted from the overall increase in wealth facilitated by money. Thus, the lower the costs associated with this process, the greater the extent that money fosters the growth of wealth. It was the consideration of this very circumstance that led to the introduction of paper money, a modern invention in which Goethe’s Mephistopheles claims to discern the entire political wisdom and economic salvation of our modern society. In the production and circulation of paper money, a considerable amount of labor and time is evidently saved, resources that can then be redirected toward the creation of new wealth. The counterargument that arises here, however, is that the security of paper money suffers as a result. In the noble metals, I possess actual value of the coin itself, serving as an inviolable, tangible pledge. Conversely, paper money represents only a conceptual, agreed-upon value, one that rises and falls in tandem with public opinion, and therein lies the inherent danger of this medium of exchange, for this element threatens to undermine the very wealth it purports to facilitate. The fact that its production, much like the minting of noble metals, is elevated to the status of a so-called "Crown Right" (as advocated, for instance, by Lassalle) offers no genuine remedy. The state now assumes the role of guarantor for this value. It is claimed that, at the state’s own coffers, paper money can, at any given moment, be redeemed and converted into noble metals. First of all, this is a complete delusion. If one were to attempt to redeem all paper money at once, the state would be compelled to suspend its payments immediately. And even in the absence of a run on their coffers, states have frequently been forced to impose a compulsory circulation upon their paper currency or have suffered financial collapse, the immense debt incurred by which becomes endless. If individuals or associations are granted the right to issue their own paper money as drafts upon their own treasuries, then where lies the guarantee? The principle of liberty, which we hold as fundamental, demands that such a practice be permitted. If an individual commands the confidence of others, why should he not be free to issue such promissory notes? It is all the better that the worker creates their own currency, puts it to productive use, and thereby augments the general wealth of society. It is impossible to prevent them from doing so, provided his notes and bills of exchange command public trust. However, the assurance that he will not equally jeopardize that general wealth is lacking, and so alongside the growth of wealth, there arises once again the heightened risk of impoverishment.

In both ancient and modern times, attempts have been made to address this new contradiction. In mentioning here a Greek and Roman institution of great wisdom, one may say that through it, the difficulties associated with the circulation of metal currency were avoided without recourse having to be taken to paper money. With the public banker (*τραπεζίτης*, *argentarius*), everyone maintained an open account, and for purchases and other transactions, people would visit them to have the sum in question transferred as a credit or debit (*expensum*, *acceptum referre*). But in turbulent times, could not the individual suffer losses even here? The public bankruptcies perpetrated by the ruling powers of the day (*σεισάχθειαι*, *tabulæ novæ*) have frequently demonstrated this. The safest course of action, as practiced by the miser or in times of war, is always to hide one's money in a safe, but in such an event, the money serves no useful purpose and generates no wealth. Thus, an institution arose that combined all the advantages of paper money with the security of metallic currency. These are the so-called deposit banks, such as the Bank of Hamburg, established in 1688. Anyone may deposit a sum of cash into the bank and hold a corresponding credit balance in the bank's ledgers, which can then be used to settle payments. The Bank of Venice, founded in 1171, was the first institution of this kind and served as the model for the Bank of Amsterdam, established in 1609. While such banks are rendered secure by the coins or bullion stored in their vaults, they contribute to the growth of wealth to a limited extent, for they only offer the advantages of accelerating the circulation of money, saving on transport costs, and preventing errors in calculation. Yet trade and turnover soon rose to a volume far exceeding the banks' actual cash reserves. Before long, the further step was taken of regarding these banks solely as institutions for the issuance of paper money. As soon as the deposited cash ceased to provide sufficient security, that security could be found only in the bank directors.

As a result, deposit banks evolved into banks of circulation. In them, the concept of commerce finds its purest realization, for their aim is to bring all goods into circulation, and to do so without requiring the aid or mediation of money. The first experiment of this kind took place in Lyon, utilizing *sconto notes* (*virements*). The foundation of this system is the bill of exchange (*lettre de change*), an invention which, according to Ganilh, served as a beacon of light for commerce, and which, according to David Macpherson, is not mentioned in historical records until the year 1255, although other writers claim that Athenians and Arabs were already familiar with it. It is said that Jews, having been expelled from various European states, collected the claims outstanding to them in those regions by issuing written orders addressed to their debtors, and upon presentation of these orders, the debtors would make payment to the bearer. Thus arose the mechanism by which a creditor could transfer their credit balance, a process enabling them to discharge their own debts to their creditors, or to acquire in exchange those goods he desired to possess. Fundamentally, a bill of exchange is nothing more than an assignment of claims, and it is primarily from this perspective that we must view its contribution to the augmentation of wealth. To the extent that bills of exchange displace and substitute for metallic currency in intermediate commercial transactions, with the wholesaler issuing bills to the farmer and the manufacturer, and receiving bills from the retailer, they offer all the aforementioned advantages of deposit banks, while simultaneously extending the settlement of mutual commercial debts across various cities and distant nations. The loss incurred on the exchange rate is offset by the gain that arises from the fact that money is at one moment dearer or cheaper in one place, and at another in another, a phenomenon for which the alternating preponderance of imports and exports may be one cause, though it is not the only one. 

What the bill of exchange accomplished in a highly isolated and fragmented manner was achieved in a thoroughly general manner through the aforementioned bills of discount, even though it was specific to Lyon, as it proved most advantageous to do so there. To be precise, individual bills of exchange were not only drawn and endorsed, but a general exchange of drafts was conducted every three months. At the fair held each quarter, merchants entered into binding commitments regarding every payment due. Since, at this time, all the merchants of Lyon stood as both creditors and debtors to one another, they mutually settled their debts through the actual exchange of their bills, without the mediation of money, or at least in such a way that only insignificant sums of cash were required to settle the remaining balances.

Where such uniformity of commercial relations did not exist, nor could the same three-month term of credit, as was customary in Lyon, more comprehensive and grander institutions had to be established. Since the bill of exchange rests upon trust, upon the honesty and integrity of the parties engaged in trade, and since personal liability provided it with sufficient security, banks were then established for the settlement of commercial debts. These banks issued paper currency that could exceed their actual cash reserves, as this paper required no new collateral to back it, making it entirely immaterial whether the medium of exchange consisted of silver or paper. This step was first taken by the Bank of London. The bills of exchange that London issued and received, acting as both debtor and creditor in the course of buying and selling, had various maturity dates. Thus, a new medium of payment had to be interposed between these bills. One would receive banknotes in exchange for a bill that had fallen due, using this medium to redeem one's own bills that were set to mature at a later date. In this manner, all of London's commercial transactions were ultimately settled using this paper currency. From London, this method of payment spread to all the provinces and abroad, transforming London into the capital of world trade. These banknotes served as a medium of exchange for bills of exchange. By rendering payment entirely abstract and ideal, this process was effected in the simplest, swiftest, and most economical manner possible. 

However greatly these purely circulation-based banks may have enhanced the national wealth, they were also fraught with great danger. For instance, if a sum owed to foreign creditors exceeded the amount that can be settled through the exchange of bills, the bank is compelled to redeem its notes for hard cash, running the very real risk of suspending payments should its cash reserves prove insufficient. As a result, all those holding such banknotes are put into jeopardy as the notes lose their value. Furthermore, in times of national crisis, holders of banknotes demand their redemption, and once again, the bank is forced to cease operations. Likewise, should merchants have, through a miscalculation, accumulated a stock of goods too vast for them to pay for, be compelled to redeem their paper holdings at the bank on an excessive scale, then the bank's monetary reserves, being insufficient to match the total volume of outstanding notes, will prove inadequate to avert a commercial crisis. Therefore, the inherent contradiction here is this: the greater the wealth that is augmented by the operations of banks, the greater the peril to which that wealth is exposed through bank failures and commercial crises.

Having recognized the theory of exchange and its ever-increasing facilitation as the innermost core of the national economy and the principal source of national wealth, we must now describe the limitations of its endless expansion. Due to the contradictions it harbors within itself, it threatens to destroy all wealth. But if the spendthrift risked their *relative* wealth through excessive exchange, and the miser risked their *absolute* wealth by severely restricting exchange, then this does not necessarily mean that the wealthiest individual is the one who sought to steer a middle course. For if the individual produces only as much value as they are compelled to consume in order to satisfy their needs, they live, as the saying goes, from hand to mouth, and since they put their entire income to use, their situation can even be characterized as highly precarious. However, if the individual accumulates surplus value through their own saved labor, such that they are able to subsist even during periods when he is not working, then Adam Smith was indeed correct in identifying the progress of wealth with the ever-increasing surplus of production over consumption. Yet even this form of wealth is of a highly dubious nature and inaugurates the most cruel of contradictions - the creation of surplus value becomes excessive, remaining dormant and unutilized. For if wealth increases alongside rising savings, which stimulates ever-greater needs, which in turn causes the expansion of production, which in turn proliferates an overproduction of goods that exacerbates poverty, then one might be tempted to refrain from saving any labor on the grounds that such savings would inevitably becomes useless, attempting to escape poverty by refusing to acquire wealth.

The resolution to this contradiction, which transforms the possibility of value back into an actual use-value by ensuring that the *dormant value* of saved labor is utilized, is the concept of *capital*, which we must now examine in greater detail. As such a surplus of income—as such an accumulation of the products of labor achieved through saving, we are presented first and foremost with the stock of goods currently on hand, the goods that a producer has manufactured but has not yet sold. the money he has realized from their sale but not yet spent to satisfy his own needs; and the warehouses of merchants, which they have filled with goods in anticipation of future consumer demand. Thus, capital consists neither solely of ready-made money, as the mercantilist system asserts, nor merely of agricultural outlays, as the agrarian system maintains, but that it encompasses the outlays and raw materials required for the operation of *all* trades and industries, including all goods that require labor power to produce, that is, everything that has value, whether in the form of *use-value* or *exchange-value*. The saved labor that a landowner has invested in the soil constitutes their capital, and since this investment is what enables the increase and improvement of agricultural yields, thereby providing greater satisfaction to everyone, it likewise becomes evident here how capital contributes to the general wealth. Capital is the expenditure of labor for the purpose of saving labor. It is this saved labor that, by facilitating and shortening the necessary labor, harnesses the forces of nature and enables new activities for surplus labor, producing goods more quickly, cheaply, and of higher quality. In short, only the foregoing allows us to fully recognize the advantages the capitalist enjoys over the laborer, who remains confined to the daily work of his own hands (and who, moreover, must remain healthy to do so).

The owner of capital uses this saved labor for their own benefit or for their family, whether by improving their land, expanding their operating capital, acquiring tools and machinery, stockpiling provisions for the winter, and so forth. However, they may also lend it to another person who does not yet possess any saved labor with which to acquire greater wealth. By granting another the use of this saved labor, the individual gains all the advantages of a capitalist without depriving the owner. In exchange for the use of capital, the debtor pays the creditor a return, an interest, yet even through the debt they have assumesd, they still gain just as much as the other party. By virtue of their own industriousness, they may as well gain even more than the creditor, who passively allows their capital to do the work. Through the instrument of debt, the original wealth is effectively doubled, as the same outstanding capital operates simultaneously under the sign of the plus (+) and the sign of the minus (–), thereby yielding double interest.

Here, however, arises the great question: wherein lies the justification for such a return? Proudhon and Lassalle famously denied the capitalist’s right to such a return, and as a consequence, they demanded that loans be granted free of charge, or sought to reduce interest rates to a bare minimum, perhaps 1/12 of one percent, justifying this as a fee for the security of repayment or on the basis of other incidental considerations. In such a scenario, it is highly doubtful that anyone could be found who would be willing to grant such a loan. If one is entitled to claim a corresponding value in exchange for the service rendered by a use-value, then why should one not be entitled to do the same for the service that is rendered by saved labor? The fact that a testator saved the funds makes no difference whatsoever, owing to the unity of persons within the family. Were the gratuitous nature of a loan to be mandated, as it is in Canon Law, the law would be circumvented through the purchase of annuities or rents, just as it was done in the Middle Ages. Alternatively, everyone would prefer to consume their own capital rather than lend it out unprofitably and at the risk of losing it entirely. However, the prospect that such a source of wealth as the monetary loan might dry up cannot constitute the true rationale for interest, rather the basis for this must be sought at a deeper level.

To harness the gratuitous utility of nature for the enhancement of wealth, one requires tools. The production of tools in turn requires the production of the tools and materials necessary for its production, all of which presupposes an original process of labor undertaken not to satisfy present needs, but to save the expentiture of future labor. However, in order for this original labor to even be undertaken at all, the daily necessities of the worker must be satisfied throughout the labor process. If we have gradually accumulated tools, materials, and supplies, we are now able to work with greater productivity and satisfy our needs more effectively, precisely because we have expended a surplus of labor in advance. The allocation of this advance across the totality of the products for which the worker has utilized constitutes the true basis of interest. For if a worker does not require this labor for their own immediate use, but instead lends it to another, this constitutes a service rendered to that individual, a service for which they are entitled to claim a return, the nature of which must be established through the free agreement of both parties. Such is the demand of the reciprocity of services, though in the case of capital it is only the *value* that is compensated and not the gratuitous utility provided by nature. It makes no difference in this regard whether the capital appears in the form of tools, materials, supplies, or whether it appears directly in the form of money, since it can be used to purchase all other goods. Given that the value of capital resides in its *use*, the debtor must cede to the creditor an equivalent value for this use as long as they continue to utilize their capital, and once this is no longer the case, they must return the principal stock itself. This use-value either takes the form of an interest or rent. We term the payment for the use of property "rent", the payment for the use of farmland "leasehold rent," and the payment for the use of capital "interest." Were I to attempt to create solely through my own labor everything for which I now pay interest, I would face a hundred times the work.

However, if the doctrine regarding the non-interest-bearing nature of capital were to bring the accumulation of wealth to a standstill, the question arises whether an excessively high interest rate would not produce the very same result. Economists advocate for usury laws to prevent harsh creditors from bleeding the borrowing populace dry, and to ensure that wealth and poverty do not drift into ever-sharper opposition. The immense severity of the Roman aristocracy toward its debtors, a severity that extended even to their imprisonment and enslavement, is held by some to have been the very source of wealth for the ancient Romans, for the populace was compelled to be led from victory to victory, ultimately to the conquest of the known world, in order to pay off its debts with the spoils of war. But as the populace gained power and wealth through this world-historical role, as they were admitted into the Centuriate Assemblies and successfully pressed for agrarian laws, secured political rights, and attained full civil equality, maximum interest rates were successively imposed for the sake of preserving their newfound prosperity. Conversely, under the emperors, with the aim of progressively curbing the power of the nobility, debtors were afforded protection against their creditors, giving rise to the principle that the former merited greater solicitude than the latter. While the Middle Ages carried this doctrine to its utmost extreme, likely with the underlying political motive of breaking the power of the feudal nobility, the interest rate of 5%, customary in the purchase of perpetual rents, persisted as the standard maximum limit right up to modern times.

Here as well, as is the case everywhere, liberty must be proclaimed as the most rational and beneficial principle. Capital is a good like any other that has its own price determined by supply and demand. In the early stages of a society, when capital is scarce, money is naturally more expensive, yet the more wealth a people accumulates, the lower the interest rate falls, for every good (including money) becomes cheaper through abundance. This price can never fall to zero, for if that were to happen, the owner of capital would, if not consume their savings entirely, at the very least hoard them solely to cover their own needs. But even here, we observe how the rising wealth of the community as a whole simultaneously benefits the individual, enabling them to procure the use of money at a lower cost, for private and public wealth invariably advance in tandem. Free agreement, as in any contractual arrangement, must therefore be permitted to determine the interest rate that is agreeable to both parties. And even in instances where the parties have failed to reach such an agreement, judicial intervention cannot prescribe the interest rate to be awarded to the creditor. To do so would be tantamount to nothing less than price fixing, such as was done historically for the price of grain, in the context of a specific legal dispute. However, if no interest rate can be prescribed, a new paradox arises. Amidst the general abundance and cheapness of money, it is precisely the most destitute, the least able to offer collateral, who are charged the highest interest rates (serving, in a sense, as a safeguard against the loss of the loan principal), and thus the poorest among us grow ever poorer. Likewise, if they were unable to find creditors willing to lend to them at the statutory maximum interest rate, they would find themselves in an even worse predicament. In such a case, every possible avenue of assistance would be cut off from them.

Therefore, out of the absolute arbitrariness of interest rates, a new opposition, that of credit, makes its appearance. The more confidence a debtor inspires in the creditor, the lower the interest rate at which money is lent to them. If borrowing money, and doing so at a low interest rate, increases wealth, then the security the debtor offers and the trust the creditor places in them constitutes a preeminent source of wealth. Thus, credit was invented to break the power of money wielded by the usurer. Credit mediates money in the same way that money mediates the exchange of goods, for it in turn serves as a proxy for money, much like the good itself, and thereby generalizes the very essence of money. It is not solely because a good has been deposited that a worker receives an advance from a capitalist, thereby obviating the need for them to concern themselves with finding channels for its sale. Thus, credit is not restricted to the past, that is, exclusive to the goods that have already been produced. Instead, the creditor’s confidence in the debtor’s solvency is grounded primarily in the future, specifically in the expectation that the loan will enable the debtor to repay the interest as well as the principal. In other words, the creditor will have accumulated beyond the necessary labor of their own to render themselves no longer dependent upon the accumulated labor of another. When a carpenter secures capital to equip their workshop, the future labor that this capital enables constitutes the creditor’s guarantee for the repayment of the principal, the capital that realizes the future value. 

In contrast to commercial credit, wherein the merchant acts as an intermediary between producer and consumer to facilitate the sale of goods, if we designate the credit provided by the banker as private credit (a sphere in which owners and seekers of capital meet one another), then it becomes clear just how much time and effort this saves. It also reveals the immense source of new wealth inherent in the fact that individuals need not seek out their own creditors or debtors, but rather that the banker—through the division of labor—has assumed this task. In other words, in addition to their established roles as deposit banks and banks of issue (or note-issuing banks), banks have acquired a third characteristic of lending money, of procuring funds for debtors. This function would be impossible were creditors not willing to supply the money; thus, the bank effectively acts as a savings institution as well. After a bank of issue, modeled after the Bank of London, was established in Paris in 1716 at the instigation of the Scotsman Law, the merchant Panchaud opened a lending bank (*caisse d'escompte*) sixty years later. Later on, Parisian bankers established a similar institution (*caisse des comptes courants*). Operating under various names, these institutions proliferated, and after undergoing various vicissitudes, during which they competed with one another, they were ultimately absorbed into the Bank of France. With a capital stock of 51 million francs, the Bank discounted 630 million francs' worth of commercial bills only five years after it was founded. Thus, we observe many banks simultaneously functioning as deposit banks, banks of issue, and lending banks.

Since the volume of turnover far exceeds the capital on deposit, the very same dangers we have already mentioned also arise whenever crises occur. Private credit negates itself and turns into its very opposite. Its purpose is to facilitate labor by providing an advance of capital, yet this advance must actually be secured. Since the worker is unable to provide this security in the form of collateral, and since the security derived from the personal liability of bills of exchange is utterly insufficient for the creditor or the banker, credit exists solely for the wealthy, remaining inaccessible to the poor because they lack the necessary collateral. Therefore, credit, as Proudhon observes, is the antithesis of trust. If banks were to lend to the poor without collateral, they would inevitably drive themselves into ruin. Deposit and discount banks serve no other purpose than to make the rich ever richer, with credit perpetually augmenting the power of money, thereby cementing its absolute dominion. The privilege of money conceals itself behind a façade of liberty and progress. Instead of liberating and realizing the value of every movable and immovable asset by representing that value as collateral in the form of banknotes, coinage actually subjects these assets even more firmly to its own rule, as their value inevitably depreciates relative to it. And if someone actually possessed collateral in the wake of the collapse of personal credit (trust devoid of collateral), then real credit is met with the same fate. For while money is made the immutable standard of value, the goods pledged as collateral either plummet in price or become entirely unsellable. In the latter case, no credit can be received for it whatsoever, while in the former case, one must submit to a deduction of about one-third. Through interest, Proudhon adds, the borrowed capital ultimately returns from the debtor to the creditor. In short, because credit fails to fulfill what it promises, taking more than it gives and benefiting only the capital owner, it spreads and perpetuates misery, suppresses labor, and ends in bankruptcy as its last word.

However, what increased the risks faced by banks above all else is the fact that they did not limit themselves to commercial and private credit, but that they involved themselves with state credit (and if they were state institutions, they had no choice in the matter). Often, this was the very reason for their inception—a dependency from which they only more or less emancipated themselves as they grew. At first glance, the state loan presents itself as an infinitely more advantageous instrument, compared to taxation, for increasing wealth. Through taxation, income is directly withdrawn from individuals, thereby diminishing their productive capacity in order to satisfy the needs of the state. Through loans, however, a number of citizens are transformed into new capitalists while state expenditures are simultaneously met. Even those who have satisfied the needs for the sake of which the loan was raised have become wealthier through the payment they received by means of the state loan. In the form of the government bond, the capital disbursed as a loan remains preserved as an asset. Indeed, the state even gains enough to eventually amortize the debt through a sinking fund. However, viewed from the opposite perspective, this effectively means that future generations are paying for the needs of the present generation, as they are forced to provide the funds for the interest payments and the repayment of the principal that benefits the present generation. Such funds can only be raised only through increased taxation, which ultimately hinders productivity. Furthermore, should the state default on its debts, a significant portion of the private wealth held by individuals is simultaneously lost. Finally, if the state, due to the multitude of debts it has already incurred, fails to inspire confidence, it is compelled to contract new loans on unfavorable terms, thereby progressively eroding the general wealth of the nation as its populace is forced to shoulder an ever-increasing burden of interest payments, quite apart from the fact that the state violates its own laws against usury in doing so. The savings put into circulation through private credit contributes to the increase of productivity in as much as they are raised for the purpose of business ventures and the like. State loans, however, as the savings put into circulation through state credit, results in an overall excess of expenditure, and the capital vanishes without any replacement upon its consumption, a process which the loans serve only to facilitate and amplify. Furthermore, under increasingly poor state administration, these loans generally flow into the pockets of those in power, while the remainder falls into the hands of government contractors. Thus, for the broad mass of people, the benefit that might otherwise accrue from increased expenditure and the more plentiful satisfaction of state requirements is severely diminished. To distill this contradiction into a single, final formula: if it is said that only wealthy nations can take on loans, then it is precisely these loans that, above all else, render them poor. 

We have now reached a point where the very summit of wealth appears to have been scaled, and thus it is fitting to cast a retrospective glance, either to pause at this height, or be impelled by the vortex of a new contradiction to ascend to even loftier summit of wealth. If we survey the various sources of wealth that have arisen thus far, we shall see that the yield of annual labor is distributed between land rent, wages, and interest on capital. From these three sources income necessarily flows, and the surplus of this income, over and above the expenditures necessary to satisfy our needs, represents the magnitude of our wealth. Adam Smith claims to have discovered the laws governing the distribution of the products of labor among these three categories of income during periods of declining, stationary, and advancing national wealth. Should wealth decline, the share of production allocated to wages falls so low that the worker can scarcely sustain themselves, the yield from land rent also diminishes, and amidst this general impoverishment, only the return on capital increases, for money becomes ever more valuable the scarcer that it is. Should wealth remain stationary, the interest on capital also stagnates, while the other two branches of income continue to incur losses. Finally, in times of increasing wealth, the products of labor are distributed with equal activity between wages, interest on capital, and land rent. Even if the absolute value of any one of these branches should happen to fall, the sheer abundance and affordability of the products more than compensates for any such shortfall.

### Innovation

Now arises the seemingly irresolvable contradiction mentioned earlier, namely, that as a result of this surplus of products, causing goods that have become even cheaper to remain unsold, all professions suffer a diminution of their income, since they are all producers just as much as they are consumers. Of what avail is circulation, intended to foster credit, if there is a lack of consumers to utilize the products? Credit presupposes sales, and if no sales are made, then the surplus of products, unable to be converted into capital, becomes a burden that hinders the growth of wealth, both for society as a whole and for the individual holding them in their possession, whether producer or creditor. The resolution of this contradiction, which will admittedly tie new knots, falls within the province of the scholars. It is this realm that we must now examine, to see how its labor, representing the very pinnacle of luxury and education, also proves to be productive, thereby elevating the wealth of the people to its highest possible summit.

#### Healthcare and Population

The key to resolving the aforementioned contradiction lies in the ever-increasing population, a phenomenon in which the physician plays such an essential role as a practitioner of the science of health, even if their efforts only serve to aid the natural laws governing population growth. These laws are rooted in the fact that an abundance and affordability of goods eases the conditions of existence, encouraging the formation of marriages and the procreation of children. A wise economy will foster marriage and prevent infanticide. However, the greater the number of children born, the greater the number that may also die, unless the physician knows how to preserve health through preventive measures and know how to maintain the health of the public. Now, as the number of consumers grows, the wealth of the nation increases. This occurs not only because all surplus goods are absorbed by the market, but also because of an ever-growing workforce that is capable of producing even more goods. With regard to agriculture in particular (and in contrast to Ricardo) the American economist Carey observes that population growth enables the cultivation of increasingly fertile soil and lush lowlands, thereby augmenting prosperity, whereas a smaller population, conversely, possesses only the sufficient labor-power to cultivate the lighter soil found on the slopes. Through this reciprocal interaction between the growth of wealth and the increase in population, a process of infinite progress is set in motion. Everything that augments wealth also augments the population, for instance, improved connections between various localities facilitate greater interaction, foster marriage, and so forth, by offering a wider range of choices. Thus, a growing population stands one of the surest signs of a nation's flourishing. As a consequence of Spain's impoverishment by gold imports and other causes, its population has fell drastically. And the fewer the workers, the fewer the goods are that produced, such that even a reduced population can no longer be provided for.

But even here, the weed of misery entwines itself once again, with astonishing speed, around the plant of wealth. If there are even slightly more mouths to feed than there is food available, then population growth and marriage serves only to exacerbate misery ever further. Using England as an example, one can observe this phenomenon on a grand scale. Amidst England’s colossal wealth, whose immense industrial activity has caused its population to grow ceaselessly, we have witnessed thousands of workers, particularly in Ireland, literally starving to death, or otherwise going hungry and barely scraping out a meager existence. The highest stage of technical progress produces the largest proletariat. Yet precisely among the proletariat the drive to reproduce is stronger, for in the absence of the pleasures afforded by luxury, natural drives must serve as their substitute, whereas the wealthy, distracted by the other pleasures available to them, are comparatively less preoccupied with the propagation of their lineage. Thus, given the very seed of misery sown within a growing population, the proliferation of this misery becomes an unstoppable force. And so the human race, as Proudhon puts it, heads toward its ruin through the very exercise of its vital faculties, through labor and through love. But love moves too fast and labor moves too slowly. And if were one able to curb the ardor of procreation, the productive power of labor itself would be curbed as well since, according to Helvétius, that very ardor constitutes its primary spur. Added to this is the fact that the poor man has no provision for the future and survives for the day, begetting children even without the slightest prospect of being able to feed and raise them.

With this, the pinnacle of contradiction in political economy appears to have been reached. The greatest wealth, that of human labor-power, has itself become the greatest misery. Proudhon has therefore depicted this summit with particular vigor, employing the sharp strokes of his pen. "Has Eternal Reason," he exclaims, "run itself into a dead end with the human race?" It almost seems so. For all the remedies that science has proposed to alleviate this plight are equal parts inadequate and appalling. Even in ancient times, simply abandoning children was a favored remedy. Malthus’s proposal, to employ death as an antidote to misery, is the most exemplary case. He does not seek to exterminate the adults among the surplus population, rather if mothers have many children, those children are to be taken from them early on and their tender lives stifled in the bud before they have ever felt the harshness of deprivation. A beautiful garden is to enclose the early graves of these newborns, gathering the grieving mothers there and offering them solace in the thought that their loved ones have escaped such immense misery. One shudders at the sheer audacity of this proposal, one that surely no political economist would ever take seriously. And what then, if the declining population ultimately suffers the very same misery, this time caused by a shortage, just as the previously growing population suffered it through an excess? How, in such a scenario, is Malthus’ strangling henchman to strike a proper balance?

We seem to have reached the end of the cycle of our contradictions. We began with the premise that labor is the source of all enjoyment and all wealth. Yet labor is, at the very same time, a contradiction in itself. It accumulates in the hands of the frugal individual, who hands down their savings to their children who are, in essence, one and the same person with them. As they subsist upon this accumulated labor, they enjoy its fruits without having to work themselves. The antithesis between the rich and the poor, between the capitalist and the worker, is a schism inherent within labor itself. The entire task of political economy is to resolve this contradiction to achieve economic harmony, that everyone should be a saver and an owner, to save humanity from its struggle to the death. The only question is how to go about this. Here, all the forms of labor we have enumerated have only heaped up ever-greater difficulties. The division of labor has resulted in the degradation of the worker, and for this reason, labor had to be reintegrated within the machine and the factory. The machine has begotten only slaves, and the workshop only wage-servants. The fierce competition ignited by manufacturing that has only given birth to monopoly. Taxation renders the monopoly subject to interest and imposes a levy upon capital in order to provide aid. Yet it is precisely these taxes that are placed most heavily upon the poor worker. While in the sphere of international trade, the workers of all nations extend a brotherly hand to one another, the capitalists of all lands, those who exploit the people, do exactly the same. The advantage of credit invariably redounds to the benefit of the financiers, while remaining a disadvantage for the propertyless who continue to grow in number, receiving the final death blow. Our own labor renders our destitution ever more abject. From formula to formula, from institution to institution, society seeks an equilibrium that forever eludes it, and which, with every new attempt, serves only to increase its luxury and its misery in equal measure. Political economy has ended in a complete contradiction, and the "necessity of misery" on this earth has become fully apparent, according to Proudhon, who even appended these very words as a subtitle to his "Philosophy of Political Economy" to be beyond all doubt.

#### Religion and Salvation

Up to this point, the servants of religion have only observed the progress of humanity, they now step into the place of the economists to proclaim the solution to the problem, situated in a higher realm, given by faith, as the promise of a hereafter. Since in antiquity the slave was sustained by his master, and thus even the day laborer fared well on earth, misery at that time was relegated to the afterlife. As Homer sang, even kings in the underworld envied the lot of the earthly day laborer. Likewise, in the Middle Ages, when the concept of otherworldly bliss first took on a truly widespread form, the proletariat of this world had not yet formed, partly through the strictures of the guilds and partly through the monasteries (which always offered a sanctuary), and at the very least, society knew how to ward off hunger, recognizing it as the inveterate enemy of humanity. It was only in more recent times, once the free laborer had been left to rely solely upon themselves and their own resources at their disposal, that misery appeared in the wake of emancipation, and with it arose the view that the poor, in their lack of earthly goods, would receive ample compensation in heaven. The rich, according to an old tradition, have already received their reward in full here on earth, whereas the unrewarded labor of the poor, even their tears, would be repaid to them in the hereafter for all eternity in exchange for their brief period of suffering, i.e. with usurious interest. With this, the impasse appears to vanish, for if divine justice and absolute equality are restored, no values ​​are lost.

Let us first consider the positive aspects inherent in this perspective. The religious solace that the cleric offers to the poor, languishing soul is evidently, interpreted as labor, of value to the giver, and interpreted as a need, of value to the recipient. Man lives by heavenly sustenance as well, Christ adds to the words he cited earlier. That inner contentment and joyful confidence which Stoicism provided during an era of spiritual and physical decay is bestowed here as well. The strength that this faith offers amidst deprivation sustains the worker's courage, they are spurred on to renewed efforts and rouse themselves out of the state of physical and moral degradation into which they have fallen. Thus, the cleric's social work can directly augment the economic wealth of a people. Furthermore, the blessings of religion are available not only to the unfortunate, but also to the fortunate. Will it not ennoble their sentiments and imprint upon their actions the stamp of a higher sense of duty, thereby rendering them more willing to work for the well-being of their fellow human beings? The cheerfulness engendered by religious consciousness will provide a higher impetus to all workers, thereby contributing to the general wealth of the nation. Finally, it is self-evident that religion can also serve to restrain crime, preventing the damage that might otherwise be inflicted upon material goods.

In opposition to this proposition, a contrary force will seek to assert itself with ever-increasing power. In the childlike, undeveloped stages of a people, faith exerts an all-powerful influence upon the human spirit. Religion acts as the nursemaid standing at the cradle of the nation, offering her breast to the infant. But once weaned from this initial sustenance, having outgrown the leading-strings that once guided it, the populace, precisely through the advancement of education, comes to the realization that it owes its well-being to itself alone, and not to any external, alien entity. It comes to prefer the tangible consequences of labor to those imaginary, future, and highly uncertain rewards. Doubt, as the offspring of progressive enlightenment, has begun to gnaw at faith. But we cannot examine here, in terms of its inherent plausibility, the promise of a retributive existence in the hereafter. This would be a matter for the philosophy of religion. Political economy contents itself with proceeding from the empirical fact that the solace derived from the promise of an afterlife becomes a chimera the moment that faith is disturbed ("Thy faith was the happiness allotted unto thee"). Happiness vanishes with the advent of doubt, and so every anchor or every means of securing prosperity through the medium of faith is lost. Once the collective reflection of the people has reached this critical juncture, the question naturally arises: Does such a system of credit, in which religion issues bills of exchange drawn upon the world to come, rest upon a reliable foundation?

Thus, in the hands of those who govern, religion becomes an instrument of servitude and robbery. Even religion turns the solace of the poor into to the advantage of the rich and powerful. "If robbery," says Bastiat, "arms the strong against the weak, it no less turns the intellect against credulity. What industrious population on earth has escaped robbery by priestly theocracies, whatever habit or name they may bear? In this guise, the evil spirit of robbery establishes its foothold in heaven, paralyzing not only the arm but also the mind, thereby realizing what once seemed impossible: intellectual servitude! It seemed that, at the very least, freedom must find an inviolable sanctuary within the realm of thought. If it succumbs even here, what then becomes of the hopes of the ages and the intrinsic worth of human nature?" Even Bastiat, in this instance, did not escape the inherent contradiction. The worst of it is that these oppressors within the state go so far as to vaunt themselves as instruments of Providence, as the very scourges of God, portraying the calluses on the worker's hands and the emptiness of their stomach as a disciplinary rod administered during a time of trial. They extol these hardships to the striving individual as the very first rung of the ladder that they themselves must climb from this earthly vale of misery toward eternal bliss. Yet they forget this teaching when it comes to themselves. For while they seek to justify, and indeed make palatable, the poor man’s deprivation, they themselves revel in an abundance of all earthly goods, without fearing the fulfillment of their master's own words, that they can no more pass through the narrow eye of the heavenly gate than a camel can pass through the eye of a needle. This is the lie, exposed by their own disregard for their own teachings, inherent in the solution they have proposed for the social question.

#### Science and Education

Thus, science finally steps forward to provide a final answer. Yet even here, Bastiat can no longer deny the inherent conflict when, in the course of the passage cited earlier, he adds: “Nevertheless, in the long run, robbery develops, within the very sphere in which it is practiced, a resistance that breaks its power, and an enlightenment that unmasks its fraud. Yet for this reason, it does not surrender, it gives birth to statecraft. It suppresses the liberty of the citizens in order to better exploit their wealth, and allows their wealth to dry up in order to better deal with their liberty. Public activity replaces the industriousness of individuals. Everything is done through officials, an obtuse and vexatious bureaucracy blankets the land. The public treasury becomes a vast reservoir into which workers pour their savings, which are then distributed among office-seekers. Free bargaining is no longer the rule of exchange, and the reciprocity of services is suppressed. The true concept of property vanishes in this state of affairs. One enters the era of privilege. Robbery, growing ever more subtle, takes root within these privileges and hides behind tariff barriers. It drives capital, labor, and population in artificial directions. It causes things to be produced with difficulty in the North that the South could produce with ease, it creates feeble industries and precarious livelihoods, and substitutes the burdensome toil of labor for the gratuitous forces of nature. It favors industries that cannot withstand competition, and calls upon public power to come to its aid against its rivals. It stokes jealousies among nations, and constantly precipitates industrial crises and bankruptcies. It stifles within the citizenry every hope for the future, even the very sense of right. And when, finally, science exposes its misdeeds, it casts its own victims in the teeth of that very science, by decrying the latter as a mere figment of the imagination. It denies the possibility of any science standing in opposition to it, employing the ultimate weapon of skepticism: 'There are no principles.'" Through this self-directed objection, Bastiat finally abandoned his one-sided stance against Proudhon, declaring that this very antagonism, no less than its resolution, lies in the very nature of the matter.

Science remains our last remaining anchor for putting an end to the contradictions of political economy. Let us see whether, and how, it is capable of doing so. All the progress we have hitherto witnessed developing in industrial activity, and in the wealth of the nation by extension, finds its ultimate source in science. Advances in mechanics, physics, and chemistry serve to perfect the various industries. More rational methods of agriculture enhance the fertility of the soil, and advances in medical science increase the population. In short, the cultivation of the science is the original root of all prosperity. Finally, should not a rational approach to political economy itself, the theoretical investigation of the laws and conditions of prosperity, also contribute to its enhancement in the practical sense? Yet it is not only all the empirical sciences, but philosophy itself, the last of the sciences to arise from the realm of luxury, is also the most useful. It storms the fortress of privilege at its very last redoubt, the final stronghold behind which it has retreated. "The priesthood," observes Schopenhauer, "has appropriated for itself the monopoly on satisfying humanity's metaphysical needs." Against this, philosophy, reuniting the division of labor, steps forward with the doctrine that the bridge between heaven and earth must be restored, that the recompense for the struggles of this life is not to be sought in a world hereafter, but in that man's own deeds, here in this world, constitute the true source of both their misery and their prosperity. The struggle between faith and enlightenment is reignited, and because the priests of religion typically have the state on their side as well, they find it no difficult task to impede the progress of philosophy and to confine it to a small circle of initiates. 

Even though the days of the Middle Ages have passed, when the Church raged with fire and sword against the disciples of free inquiry, these disciples nevertheless still find themselves compelled, even now, to heed the maxim of Cicero: "to confine themselves to the few capable of judgment, and deliberately to shun the multitude." However, the opposite impulse has indeed manifested itself, even among philosophers, to universalize their teachings, and to cast knowledge out among the masses. Hence the "philosophy of action" has been propounded. Yet it is precisely here that the struggle between superstition and free thought, between the seekers of light and the seekers of darkness, has truly flared up. The more the entire populace strives toward the light, seeking to attain freedom through knowledge, and thereby to secure the fullest realization for all the drives within human nature, the more resolutely does the priesthood seek to bind them quickly in their old fetters. Since the state holds education in its hands, claiming the right to direct it through administrative channels, such as by means of regulations, it seemed to the late Stahl, that this regression to superseded viewpoints would be an easy task were it not for the family, which knows how to nourish the eternal spark of freedom and self-respect upon the hearth of its household gods, and instills it in the rising generation. But the sorrowful paradox remains: How many children actually find wholesome sustenance within this sanctuary? The poorest children are scarcely able to attend school regularly, for they are left with no other choice but to assist their parents in earning a living from an early age by selling their labor for dirt cheap. Knowledge becomes even less accessible to the proletarian, who continues to sink even deeper into these conditions.

Here, however, the most profound dilemma now confronts us. In the inception of a society, the education of all stands at a generally equal level since, in childlike faith, the people entrusted themselves to the guidance of the priesthood. With the progress of education, alongside the liberation of a few through science, the enslavement of the majority becomes even more stark, driven not only by religion, but also by ignorance itself. This ignorance extends not only to the realm of philosophy (which one might deem relatively harmless), but also to the realm of law, to the very bonds that hold human societies together on earth. Thus, the ultimate outcome of ever-increasing wealth, in both its material and intellectual aspects, is crime, the inevitable result of physical, moral, and intellectual degradation. Crime is a social product and is the final expression that the dialectic of political economy assumes. If vast accumulations of property have devalued labor, then misery exacts its revenge through crime, by striking back at the regime of property itself. The person whose physical well-being is satisfied, and who, through instruction and education, grasps universal moral principles, is far removed from the impulse to commit crime. It is the desperation for survival, combined with the delusion that injustice might serve as the easiest means to satisfy one’s needs, that engenders crime. Nevertheless, moral accountability is not thereby abrogated, since the guilt for their deed still falls upon the criminal. For even if, however powerfully they may have been impelled by evil examples, natural impulses, or deprivation, they still proceeded to commit the crime. Even still, society bears half the guilt for the crime, whereas the wealthy criminal bears double.

However, just as in the economy at large, society must also redress the ill that it has helped to create. Each individual, a member of the social body, bears the duty to eliminate crime, as well as its effects upon both the offender and the victim, as best it can, and under certain circumstances, to prevent it. Thus, society must depart from the blind operation of economic laws in order to consciously, through collective organization, procure the means of well-being that lie beyond the capabilities of the individual. In saying this, I by no means intend to overturn Adam Smith’s proposition that every individual knows better than the government to which type of labor their capital is best suited. I agree with him that it is presumptuous, even ridiculous, for state administrators to interfere with the allocation of the nation’s wealth. Nonetheless, two means can be identified whereby, not by the state and its power, but the free associations of individuals, acting through the authority derived from their own self-governance, must confront the misery engendered by crime. Firstly, the associations that serve to prevent crime directly, reducing its effects as much as possible, and with punishment to ensure that it does not recur, is the justice system. The other, aimed at stemming the source of crime and preventing it indirectly by ensuring that, through free associations and the pooling of individual strengths, work, and in its wake, the prosperity that the individual could not secure on their own, is made available to them with greater ease, we must regard as the proper task of free association as the domain of public welfare. Justice and public welfare are both implemented by freely associating bodies, thus standing as the two complements and fulfillments of political economy. It is solely with their aid that this science may lay claim to achieving its ultimate goal of universal prosperity.
