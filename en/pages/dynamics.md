# Dynamics

Note: This webpage is an adapted translation and commentary of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Encyclopedia Nature* (1876) 

The moment of mechanics now under consideration is what is called dynamics or classical mechanics. As opposed to formal or mathematical mechanics on the one hand, and astronomy or mechanics on the other, this kind of mechanics stands midway between both and mediates between them. For mechanical bodies are, firstly, completely indifferent and external to one another through mutual repulsion, where each body resists being moved by another body from the outside, which is the subject matter of statics. The second is that, acting as others against one another, they place each other in a state of motion, the subject for which is kinematics. But that bodies, thirdly, return from motion to rest by determining and being determined as a system of bodies, passing over from repulsion to attraction, this immanent motion of bodies is elastics.

## Statics

In statics, through the absolute repulsion to which they owe their origin, the motion of mechanical bodies is inertial and persists independently of each other. However, the motion of a body cannot persist indefinetly because its motion is changed the moment it inevitably comes into contact with another body. From collision, the intrinsic motion of the body is restored in that the action of the mechanical body on its other becomes its center of mass.

### Inertia

The inertia of matter consists in the fact that, in being posited by repulsion, it indifferently accepts any influence that reaches it from outside. Since matter here is a relation of discrete units external to one another, it lacks the form of a continuous unity. Rather, since repulsion predominates in inertia, matter disintegrates into many mutually delimiting bodies, which can only be distinguished from one another as different quantities of mass. The inert masses are scattered in space as indifferent units that are distant from one another. Likewise, every distance is only relative, two masses are only distant from each other because there is a third mass that is closer to one of the two masses. If a change occurs in one body, it is indeed communicated to the other precisely because they are connected. But since every state that thus passes from one body to another remains indifferent to it, as coming from outside, it remains at rest or in motion into which it has been placed by another. It is only through the communication of motion that the inertia of masses is momentarily dissolved by their contact.

### Contact

The indifferent separation of masses, which is inherent in inertia, turns into its opposite in contact because the separation of masses posits and presupposes their connection. And because proximity is so absolute that no empty space separates the masses, the nearest masses touch each other directly. Contact, as the abolition of separation, is the beginning of the ideality of matter. Here we see empirically in real matter what, in the case of space, time, and motion, has been proven only as a conceptual necessity, that two points are one, and thus interpenetrate each other. One might indeed think that the outermost point of one body lies very close to the outermost point of the other. But in this way, they would not be in contact with each other. Rather, if they remained two points, they would be separated from each other by a line, however small, which, as such, would have an infinite number of points. But then bodies, however close to each other, would always be separated from each other by an infinite number of points. In the case of spheres, there would be only two points in contact with each each other, and in the case of smooth planes, an infinite number of points can do so. Even the points on the inside of each body, which are only in contact with points of their own body, are in contact with all the points on the inside of the adjacent body.

All bodies will, on the one hand, strive to establish a connection with all others, and on the other hand, because of their impermeability, also strive to resist all others. Impermeability is
only an impermeability of connected bodies, i.e. bodies in contact with each other. Contact, by opposing impermeability, exposes it only one-sided and relative. It is therefore completely right to reject absolute impermeability as a general property of matter and to conceive it only as resistance to motion. The unity of contact and resistance established in permeability means nothing other than each body maintains its independent motion by opposing the motion of its other, that each seeks to position itself in the position of the other and simultaneously strives to assert its position as its own. This is the struggle of both bodies for one place. However, since they must simultaneously remain outside each other, they establish a third, common place in which they abolish their external relationship and establish themselves as one. However, since repulsion still prevails over attraction here, this point of unity is not the absolute center of gravity, but a contingent point of unity in which the two masses can both determine and establish themselves as one. This forms the concept of the center of mass.

### Center of Mass

With the center of mass, the body no longer requires another body to bring it into equilibrium and rest. This action is exerted not externally, but rather on itself as its orientation. It has become a lever, having its point of support and balance in its center of mass. However, since a body as a totality has three dimensions, the equilibrium that its parts have among each other are also threefold. Each of its dimensions forms a plane that intersects it, and where the three planes of division intersect is the point of support that forms the body's center of mass. Thus, the body is not brought to rest by another, but by itself, it is a totality at rest in itself. This inner center of mass can even lie outside the body's matter if its parts are so irregularly connected that the planes of division intersect outside the body. Even if for a moment the body seemed to relate only to its inner totality of parts, this cannot abolish its relationship to other bodies. Rather, it is because of the center of mass that the body is displaced and constantly disturbs other bodies, even if their reaction is unable to disturb it from rest. As soon as equilibrium is disturbed, because the different masses cannot compensate this disturbance, motion immediately replaces rest. But since each force, having its center of mass within itself, has become a totality, it no longer needs to seek a third point of unity in order to come into contact with the other. Instead, the bodies now act directly on each other with their centers of mass, without having completely escaped the control of inertia. This is the subject of kinematics.

## Kinematics

While in statics, motion is contingent and rest is necessary, in kinematics it is the reverse. 
Since rest, due to the balance of attraction and repulsion, appears to be the original state of matter, motion appears to be something derived without actually being so. Here, however, it is imparted to one body by another, and is therefore from the outside still something contingent. Bearing in mind this origin, kinematic motion will be quantitatively different, just as inertial masses, being quantitatively different, press against each other with a different weight. For, depending on the greater or lesser the weight exerted on one mass by another, this other mass will move faster or slower. Its law can only be that of mathematical motion, namely, uniformit: "equal spaces are traversed in equal times." It applies without restriction to every speed considered in isolation, which we can call absolute speed. But if we compare different speeds with each other, i.e. relative speeds: the larger the spaces and the smaller the times, the faster the motion; in the opposite case, the slower. Therefore, the speeds, at the same time, behave like the spaces traversed.

Since the velocity imparted to a body by another also depends on mass, it will, on the one hand, be greater the more the active body exceeds the passive one in mass, for it can only move the passive body through the excess of its weight because the latter resists the action of the former through its counteraction to the extent that it has mass. On the other hand, the passive body can also continue the acquired velocity all the more the more it has mass itself; for this is the weight with which it acts on others. If a feather is thrown with even the greatest velocity, its movement remains powerless because it lacks mass, whereas a lead ball propelled by the same throw flies off into the distance. This already shows that the body is not merely inert, but also determines its movement by itself through its determinacy. In this case, mass forms, in a sense, the spatial aspect, while velocity forms the temporal aspect: both together constitute the magnitude of motion, its momentum. The magnitudes of the motion of two bodies are therefore related as the products of their masses and velocities.

The two elements from which the magnitude of the motion of a body is formed are, on the one hand, the velocity imparted to it from outside, and, on the other, its own mass, with which it absorbs and modifies this velocity, an immanent moment of motion also appears alongside the transcendent moment, although both remain quantitative. Having said this in general terms, what is left is to explain the fundamental concepts of kinematics: the tendency, the persistence of motion as momentum, the change in the persistence of motion via a momentary resistance as impulse, and the return from the motion to rest through the persistence of resistence, friction.

### Momentum

The first effect of a moving body on another, momentum, occurred with the lever and balance, but only as an ideal effect of weight. This was upward momentum, or lifting. The momentum, however, with which we are now dealing, is specifically lateral momentum, the effect of one body on another that lies immediately to its side. The downward momentum, whereby one body is pushed by another and carried as a load, will only have to consider thirdly with the fall of bodies. Two bodies lying at rest next to each other do not press on each other, but rather remain inert relative to each other. But when a body set in motion by another comes into contact with a third, it actually presses on it. This momentum here is not merely the result of weight, as in statics, where the momentum is as great as the mass and motion also occurs with unequal masses or distances. Instead, the magnitude of the momentum is the product of velocity and mass. The same mass will therefore press more, the greater its velocity, and a smaller mass will press just as much as a larger one, or a larger mass will press only as much as a smaller one, if their velocities are proportionally so much greater in the first case and so much smaller in the second. As for the body that gets pushed, a countermovement arises against the initial effect of the momentum. This second momentum, if the pressed body was at rest, has only one factor of mass, whereas the momentum of the moving body arises from two factors. If the resting body wants to successfully resist, its mass must be large enough to paralyze the force of the moving body, which is precisely the product of its mass and velocity. For example, a cannonball will either rebound from a wall or pierce it, depending on whether its velocity is faster or slower and whether the wall is more or less massive. The result is rest if the resistance of the resting body is greater or at least as great as the momentum of the moving body. In the opposite case, the resting body is transformed into motion by changing the momentum of the moving body, and such change in momentum has become an impulse.

### Impulse 

While momentum is what determines movement, the actual change in the movement of the body occurs in the impulse, which arises in three possible cases. Firstly, when a moving body rushes toward a stationary one and, more strongly than the latter, drives it forward by its momentum. The impacting body is paralyzed by the amount by which the impacted body has mass to oppose it. Secondly, if both bodies are in motion, they collide in opposite directions. The stronger body captures the weaker one and drags it along in its direction. Since the momentum of both bodies oppose each other,
the magnitude of the remaining motion will only be the difference of their momenta. And if both momenta are equal, the mutual collision will lead to rest. Or, if both bodies are moving in the same direction, the faster one will have to catch up with the slower one if collision is to occur, in which case the magnitude of the motion will be the sum of their momenta. Thirdly, if two moving bodies collide with a third at an angle, the difference with which they impart their motion to the third can be visualized as two lines of greater and lesser length, in the same ratio, that meet at a right angle. In this way, they form the sides parallelogram of forces, and the body pushed by both then moves in the diagonal of this parallelogram with the combined force imparted to it by the pushing bodies. 

Lifting, pulling, and swinging are only different modifications of the push. Lifting is a push that does not take its direction away from, but toward, the pushing body, in that the lifting body pushes the lifted one upwards toward itself. Pulling is a push in which only a part of the pushed body is in front of the pushing body, but the rest is behind it, i.e. the horse pushes the cart rolling behind it. And swinging is a tangential push around a pivot, whereby the pushed body remains under the power of the pushing body. whereas, in a hurling, the latter releases the former from its power. If we were to say that the active body, by overcoming the countermovement of the passive one, sets it in motion, then this remaining quantity of motion, according to the law of inertia, would last indefinitely because it is not yet foreseeable how a movement imposed from outside could pass into rest by itself. Yet every movement produced by the impact is necessarily finite. For the pushed body does not rush along in a void, because there is no such thing as empty space, it always encounters other bodies on its path. Each body it encounters offers resistance being pushed by the same body, and the impact weakens more and more due to the counterpressure the longer it acts, until it transitions to rest. This transition from motion to rest is friction.

### Friction

Friction, is often regarded as something accidental, as if, if only friction could be removed, momentum would continue ad infinitum by means of inertia. But one must not seek infinite motion where it cannot be found. For since the impulse is finite by nature, and because everything that has a beginning must also have an end, friction is only the manifestation of this finitude. Friction, like impact in general, is a lateral impulse, except that now the impulse emanates from the impacted body, not from the impacting body. It is irrelevant whether the restraining body has such a strong connection that the moving body cannot divide it, or whether it encounters bodies which, due to their weaker cohesion, it divides in order to clear a path through them by pushing aside the parts of the mass lying in its path, rather than the entire body. It is difficult to decide in which case the greater friction occurs, however in both cases the entire impacted mass paralyzes the movement of the impacting body.

Up to this point, we have still not been able to deny the law of inertia a certain legitimacy. It is subject to the intellectual law of identity, insofar as the externally introduced movement and the externally created rest are supposed to remain only movement and rest for all time. Friction already puts a complete end to the finite motion, expanded into endless motion by the law of inertia. Yet while friction brings about a transition from motion to rest, the cause of this transition is still external. At the same time, however, friction also represents the beginning of the intrinsic nature of this transition. Since friction reduces and ultimately abolishes motion, it is the activity of one body not to let go of the other, but to take the contact seriously so that it remains attached. Thus, the reverse tendency of that which we saw when we entered finite mechanics begins again. The infinite mass, through abstract repulsion, disintegrated into a multitude of bodies, each of which had its center of gravity within itself and now excluded the other. As the bodies cling to one another through friction, they seek to return from their multiplicity to unity, from repulsion to attraction, that is, they seek their common center of gravity. 

## Elastics

It is still only a one-sided concept of Nature to allow masses to exist only in their separateness, inertia, and external relationship to one another. The ideality of matter that breaks into this externality, demands, on the contrary, that they all related to one another through the oneness of the center of gravity. This center, however, is located in the body with the greatest mass, because only through this center of gravity can it attract all other masses. Since this is also their own impulse, they will incessantly move of themselves toward it and will not rest until they have come as close to it as possible. If repulsion was previously the predominant and essential factor, now attraction takes its place and becomes the immanent determination of bodies, whereas repulsion becomes only contingent, external factor.

However, instead of attraction not having previously appeared at all in analytical mechanics, now a struggle occurs between the two moments of elastics, in that attraction strives to abolish the indifference to the general center, which lies precisely in the one-sided effectiveness of repulsion. It therefore must first have to consider the case in its own, exclusive activity as movement toward the center in the direction of the radius, compression. The second is that the transverse direction seeks to divert the compressed body from its inherent motion and expose it to the impact, tension. Finally, both motions will come to apparent equilibrium with each other in the oscillator.

### Compression

Elasticity as such is the first positive realization of the immanent motion in the material in that it restores itself from any external compression by its energy density alone. The first effect of the impression produced is the displacement of the body from its place, at least partially. It yields to the impression made on it, the capacity for which can be called flexibility. But this external quantitative impression is at the same time an internal qualitative determination, the pressing body seeks to transfer its density to that of the pressed one. As the soft body, through the impression, occupies a smaller space than before, it becomes specifically denser, and several of its qualitative spatial points become one. But flexibility is not an absolute quality, no elastic body can be compressed to a mathematical point.

### Tension

The immanent form, which maintains itself in this condition, reacts accordingly against this compression, which can only be continued up to a certain limit. Once this limit is reached, the soft body offers resistance, persisting in the now smaller space it occupies: i.e., it becomes hard. Stiffness is just as little an absolute property as flexibility. No body is absolutely hard; it always yields slightly if it encounters an even harder opponent. Since flexibility has transformed into stiffness through its own activity, which is its own negation, the elastic body now itself becomes the compressive one, and the other the compressed one. The elastic body wants to reconquer the space it has lost. This transformation of flexibility and stiffness into one another, as merely relative concepts, this interpenetration of them is elasticity itself. Compression and expansion are a play of forces established by the inner activity of the form. When the negativity of position, as an inner change of position, is abolished, and position is restored, movement returns to rest. Since the two states of greater compression or greater extension are no longer merely distributed between two bodies, it is the inner structure of one and the same body that reciprocally negates its material position through compression in order to regain it through expansion. The external appearance, the being-for-other of this ideality of matter, is, however, as the negation of the filling of space, as the transformation of material space into material time, a trembling of the entire materiality, an oscillation.

### Oscillation

Because attraction and repulsion are identical in oscillation, not only must both moments always be determined by gravity, but also the masses set in motion by gravity must maintain their independence and dependence, their distance and proximity to the barycenter, in uniformity. However, since impulse, fall, and the other mechanical relationships are descriptively limited because they only partially capture the full relationship of attraction and repulsion, it must be noted, on the one hand, that they are all simultaneously swept along and sublated by celestial motion. But on the other hand, the mechanisms also maintain an appearance of independence from it, because it is precisely the equalization and balance arises through the distortions produced by oscillation, even if in Nature these are opposites that diverge only spatially, not temporally.
