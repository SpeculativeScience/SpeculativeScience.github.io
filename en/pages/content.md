# The Content of Philosophy

Note: This webpage is a direct translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Encyclopedia Logic* (1876) 

If the purpose of philosophy is knowledge, then the question arises as to *what* knowledge it is, and what is to be known. To put it another way: the examination of philosophy’s purpose leads us to the nature of its knowledge as well as its object. In this twofold respect, philosophy distinguishes itself from the empirical sciences on the one hand, and from theology on the other.

For the empirical sciences, knowledge itself is not the ultimate end, but is a means to another end. To be sure, this knowledge also originates from a contradiction—a question that demands an answer. But the empirical question is a human *need* and the answer is the drive that *satisfies* it. Physics, chemistry, and other natural sciences originally took shape in response as useful means to satisfy man’s sensuous needs, whereas jurisprudence and other social sciences took shape in response to man's higher spiritual needs. These sciences therefore serve a purpose external to themselves, and as a result, they stand, to a greater or lesser extent, in the service of society. For instance, the purpose of medicine is health, and the purpose of jurisprudence is the security of persons and property. Even geometry originally served a specific purpose—in Egypt, where it was invented—to settle boundary disputes regarding agricultural fields following the Nile’s recession within its banks, as the annual flooding had obliterated the demarcation lines.

Admittedly, philosophy also satisfies a human need, i.e. the need for knowledge. But in this instance, the drive is no longer distinct from knowledge, no longer something alien to it, because the drive for knowledge takes itself as its own object. The activity of the drive toward knowledge finds its fulfillment within itself, even if the activity yields no other utility whatsoever. All human beings—as Aristotle begins his *Metaphysics*—are driven by nature to seek knowledge. The indomitable curiosity of children is the first stirring of this philosophical drive. In philosophy, knowledge has no end other than itself, it is knowledge for the sake of knowledge. Having originated—as Aristotle observes—from the desire to escape the wonder and astonishment of ignorance, it transforms, in its progression, into its very opposite. The ignorant person marvels that the side of a square and its diagonal are incommensurable as lengths, but the geometer does not, for they know that the roots of commensurable squares are never themselves commensurable. Hence, Horace’s maxim *nil admirari* (let nothing astonish you) constitutes the essential characteristic of the one who has knowledge—the philosopher. But if, throughout history, people have engaged in philosophy precisely to escape ignorance, then the proposition of Cicero that *inscientiam esse principium philosophiae* (ignorance is the beginning of philosophy) applies only in the sense that ignorance is certainly the beginning (*initium*) of philosophy, and is destined, in the end, to transform into its opposite.

However, if the end goal of knowledge is truth, then philosophy makes the knowledge of truth its content and object—the original principle of all things. Truth is the ground for the truth of all things that follow from this principle, just as fire warms everything that surrounds it. Now, if people designate this primal principle—this truth-diffusing presence—this truly existent Being that imparts its own existence to all things, as God, then philosophy, with regard to the content of knowledge, is in general one with religion, which likewise aims at knowledge of God. However, a difference in form still persists between the two. For what religion apprehends through feeling and faith, philosophy comprehends through thought and thinking. This formal distinction admittedly ceases where religion is elevated to the status of a science, i.e. theology. But even here, a certain difference in content still remains, since religion and theology are narrower in scope than philosophy. By viewing the Divine as a truth manifesting itself within the human spirit and imagination, the inner life of the spirit is revealed—a realm in which God is apprehended as in opposition to the world, and wherein the secular is understood as striving to absorb the Divine essence into itself. Philosophy, by contrast, contemplates God as fully realized within reality. Philosophy does not rest content with the mere conception of God as a distinct, particular entity, but to recognize in the entire world (as His works) the adequate manifestation and realization of His being. Thus, in all finite things, philosophy rediscovers the Infinite. If one were to accuse philosophy of pantheism, then what exactly is so wrong with asserting, in the spirit of the Bible, that God is "all in all"? This very conception of philosophy is also the reason why it was given the nickname "worldly wisdom"—in contrast to "divine wisdom" (theology)—even though Friedrich von Schlegel, and Hegel after him, applied the term "worldly wisdom" to philosophy in an earnest sense.

In its active engagement with the world, philosophy does not fall into the one-sided limitations of the empirical sciences. Instead, philosophy distinguishes itself from them in precisely this respect: that by extending its scope across the entire domain of being—as it unfolds within the empirical sciences—it relates everything back to God as the ultimate source of this being. It demonstrates how God reveals Himself not only within religious consciousness, but equally in nature, in the state, in art, and so forth. In sphere of religion, even if this truth is verbally affirmed, it often remains more or less a figure of speech. The empirical sciences, however, do not even wish to employ such a figure of speech, for they expressly disdain to speak of God. Only the discussion of proximate causes is permissible, whereas any talk of final causes is refused. If they were to attempt to do so—for instance, by citing God as the cause of electricity—they would make themselves a laughingstock, even though such a statement would be correct by definition. For while it is true that God is the cause of all things, it is precisely for that reason is this cause is too remote, too indeterminate, and what is actually demanded is the immediate, proximate cause. Hence, the empirical sciences are atheistic by nature. When Napoleon I asked the astronomer Laplace what he thought of God, the latter replied: "Sire, I have had no need of that hypothesis." Lalande similarly remarked that he had searched the entire heavens and found no God. In this way, physics regards the objects of nature solely as natural entities, politics views the state solely as a human creation, and so on.

Philosophy therefore distinguishes itself from theology and empirical sciences in that, whereas the latter two subjects apprehend their respective objects in isolation as distinct from the objects of all other sciences, philosophy recognizes them in their interconnection, by seeking out the divine archetype that manifests itself within each sphere. All the sciences enter into relation and contact with one another through philosophy. For the sciences, as Cicero observes in his defense of the poet Archias, a common bond held together in mutual cohesion. Philosophy is conscious of this unity in that it apprehends the objects of the particular sciences as the successive stages in the development of the One Divine Being, rendered as an encyclopedia of the sciences. Within the common bond that philosophy weaves around all the sciences, it includes theology as well. If the empirical sciences—those concerned with the finite conditions of life—can be likened, in Hegel’s phrase, to the "workdays" of life, then religion constitutes the "Sunday" of life. Yet, this Sunday merely alternates with the other days, as much as religion keeps God and the finite distinct from one another. In philosophy, by contrast, even the workdays of life are subsumed into the Sunday and are transformed through their relation to it. Thus, in the philosopher’s activity, the earthly and the heavenly do not merely alternate with one another, they comprehend both in a state of living interconnection. The philosopher is Sunday's child—one who lives in a state of eternal festivity.

While it might appear that philosophy itself—much like the poet who arrived too late in Schiller’s poem *The Division of the Earth*—has been left empty-handed, the reality is quite the opposite. Unlike that poet, who must be content to accept Zeus’s offer of a dwelling place in the heavens, philosophy does not exit from the division of the earth empty-handed. Instead, philosophy only reclaims possession of what has already been occupied. Given this vast scope, one might accuse philosophy of meddling, being a "jack-of-all-trades," or a "pot-gazer" poking its nose into every scientific dish. As the argument goes, the philosopher apparently cannot penetrate as deeply into any one science as the specialist who has devoted themselves exclusively to that specific field. While philosophy may indeed possess knowledge of everything, it does so only in a superficial manner. According to Plato, just as the pentathlete may surpass specialists in individual disciplines within those areas that the latter did not practice themselves, having being trained in all five forms of athletic contest, so too will they inevitably be defeated by each specialist in that specialist’s own field. This circumstance must also fare for the philosopher, by knowing less than the physicist knows of physics, or the jurist of law, and so forth, nonetheless presumes to have a say in these matters. Nevertheless, the philosopher has an absolute right to this form of "meddling"—for as Novalis observes, philosophy is "the capacity to be oriented in all things", even if this very proposition serves only to antagonize others against the philosopher. It can be readily conceded that, in a quantitative sense, the philosopher knows less within any given specialized science than the scholar who confines themselves exclusively to that specific field. But the crucial factor is not *how much* is known, but the *nature* of the knowledge itself. The philosopher knows *multum*—much in substance—not merely *multa*—many separate things. For what is known is either the particular, an infinite multitude of instances that constantly multiply, all differing from one another, and which can never all be known, or it is the universal that underlies them all, the simple principle that, as the truth that remains constant to itself, appears in all particulars and is reiterated within them. Although, whoever knows the particular—as Aristotle very rightly observes—does not thereby necessarily know the universal, but whoever knows the universal also recognizes all the particulars that can comprise it. That philosophy is this knowledge of the universal is not to say that the empirical sciences do not also arrive at universals, for all scientific knowledge is knowledge of a universal. However, there two particular types of universals. In the empirical sciences, the particular remains the foundation and never becomes the result. From a multitude of particulars, the general law is abstracted, but newly discovered particulars may subsequently alter this law. Such a universal is, therefore, only a relative or comparative universal—a universal that is induced from the particulars themselves. In philosophy, conversely, the universal is the objectively and absolutely universal—the foundation and determining principle through which the particular is comprehended.

The most universal Being—from which every individual thing is generated—religion calls God, and philosophy is the habituation to such a universal, which as the primordial source, constitutes the genesis of the world. According to Aristotle, this universal is also the most knowable, because it firstly operates within the purest realm of knowledge—pure thinking—without being dependent upon either the infinite multiplicity of individual objects or the contingency of their discovery. Knowledge of such particulars is merely a sum of information, not complete knowledge. For this reason, this universal is all the more knowable because it stands furthest removed from the senses, which present only particulars and can be deceptive. Although sense perceptions are—chronologically speaking—the first things we recognize, they are not as truly *knowable* as the absolutely universal recognized at the end. Since philosophy is the science of that which is most knowable, it is—as Aristotle asserts—the *most* scientific. Since Hume, knowledge has been held to be all the more certain and infallible the more it concerns itself solely with the particular and the sensuous. Conversely, the more that knowledge aspires toward the absolutely universal and the Divine, the more it is deemed tenuous and unattainable. The ancients, in their divine wisdom, maintained the exact opposite. Although these two forms of universality—the one derived from sensuous particulars and the other recognized independently of the senses—must ultimately converge and coincide. It is precisely through this convergence that the system of philosophy simultaneously becomes the system of the sciences.

What, then, is this universal that philosophy discerns within things and through what activity does it generate this universal within itself? The activity that discovers the universal—in and for itself—as the true principle of things is *thinking*. However, just as there are two particular forms of universality, so too are there two particular forms of thinking. The first form is subjective thinking—what we call *representation*—which derives relative or comparative universals from individual things, of which they are, in Hume’s sense, faint reflections of them. Locke termed such comparative universals *ideas*—such as "elephant," "army," or "drunkenness." The crucial point in philosophy, however, is not to assume that these universals exist merely within our heads, even though that is how they first present themselves to us. The second form of thinking—*objective thinking*—consists of comprehending the universal as the very essence of the thing, as the thought inherent to the matter itself, and it is this alone that we designate as *concepts*. What first presents itself to human perception—sensible things in their individuality—reveals to us a colorful interplay of coming-to-be and passing-away. This particular rose withers, and in its place, another sprouts forth another rose from its seed. What persists amidst the disappearance of all these roses is *the* rose—the concept that underlies all these individual instances and encompasses them all within itself. We cannot imagine this act of synthesis merely as our own doing, as if we were generating the concept of the rose within our minds without the concept already having existed beforehand. We designate such an objective concept as the *genus*, the principle inherent in every individual instance, serving as the inner, indwelling force that brings the individual forth from the seed, rendering it a mirror and exemplar of the universal. The individual is *real*—and indeed, all the more real—the more purely it gives expression to its genus. Yet the creative power of the universal exists only through the *medium* of the individual, as Aristotle rightly observes: "Peleus is the father of Achilles, and you are your father’s son; but no one is the son of the universal."

The objective thought, however, does not merely maintain a peaceful, positive, and creative relationship to the things we have observed thus far, for the individual is not entirely commensurate with the universal. The genus is active not only at the moment of generation, but persists throughout the entire life of the individual. The universal reveals itself ever more fully within the individual, embedding itself ever more deeply. This growing ascendancy of the universal constitutes a destruction of the individual. In this act of creation and destruction lies the activity of divine thought. Everything flows ceaselessly from thought and is drawn back into it. The Creation and the Last Judgment are thus not sundered apart, assigned to the beginning and the end of days, but are connected with one another at every single moment. Scarcely has the individual rose passed into judgment through its fading away, when the creative power of the genus within the seed already begins to germinate once more. The individual does so as one of the manifold possibilities of the universal, arising from that singular reality just as that reality in turn had originated from that potentiality.

What objective thought performs is now, in the same manner, replicated by subjective thought. When I think of an object, I do not leave it simply as it is. I eliminate the aspects of its sensuous appearance and retain the enduring, common essence shared by all individuals of that kind. Yet through this act of destruction, only the finite has vanished, whereas that which truly *is*—the essential reality—is preserved and retained in thought.

Therefore, the Truth is neither the universal nor the individual, but the universal realizing itself within the individual. This universal, conceived of and simultaneously held to be that which truly *is*—has, since the time of Plato, been designated by the term *Idea*. Thus, knowledge of the Idea constitutes the subject matter of philosophy eternal and imperishable elements within them. To the extent that philosophy identifies thought with being, it is *Idealism*—though not *subjective* idealism (such as Berkeley's idealism, who asserted that the existence of things depended on our perception of them, as if our own mental representations were the things themselves), but that *Idealism* is simultaneously *Realism*, as much as the Idea is reality itself. Objective thought is the hook to which Zeus fastens the world—the eternal principles, as Aristotle puts it, upon which the heavens and all of nature depend. Hence the world of thought is constructed not alongside, outside, or beyond the world of appearances, but right within the one and only world. It is thus an inaccurate expression when Schiller, singing of the eternal principle of all things, writes:

>High above time and space there hovers,

>All-powerful, the highest Thought.

Likewise, we find ourselves drawn more closely to Goethe when he has Faust declare:

>The realm of spirits is not barred;

>Arise! Bathe, student—undaunted—

>Your earthly breast in the morning glow!

The philosopher—like the sorcerer in a fairy tale—sees straight through the stony shell to behold the pure diamond of thought hidden deep within the mountain’s core. This thought, as the formative activity at work within all things, unfolds into a system of Ideas. By recognizing this intellectual realm, philosophy does not lose itself in empty abstractions—in "shadow-beings," as Schiller initially termed these forms—but that through this very act, begins to move within reality, for everything that is truly real in things is their thought, their substantial form. The masses of the world, however, believe precisely the opposite. Like the inhabitants of a cave where a light burns—to borrow a Platonic simile—who see only the shadows cast upon the back wall by objects moving past the entrance behind their backs, and mistake these shadows for the things themselves. But if were they to be turned around and led out into the light of day, having been blinded by the sun's glare, they would mistake the archetypes for the shadows. Resembling them, non-philosophers regard sensible things—which, according to Giordano Bruno, are the shadows of ideas—as the true things. However, if they were they compelled to gaze into the sun that enlightens the world, they would mistake the ideas or the archetypes for the shadows, and their sensible images for the real things.

If we designated God as the object of philosophy earlier on, this designation has now been clarified for us to mean that philosophy makes system of Ideas intelligble to us, conceived as the various aspects and moments of the One original Idea that permeates all existence. The many Ideas are referred back to the One Idea—to the Absolute Idea—that encompasses within itself the system of species and genera of all things. Thus, God ceases to be a mere name, a term under which one might conceive of the most disparate notions, as various religions have certainly done. In philosophy—where we substitute the reality itself for its name—we explicitly declare the Absolute Idea to be our object, and only then do truly we know *what* God is, rather than merely knowing *that* He is. However, the indeterminate nature of "God" within mental representation, it is preferable in philosophical discourse to avoid using this name, opting instead for the terms "Idea" or "Absolute Idea", refraining from this until God has been adequately defined. Therefore, philosophy, conceived as the doctrine of Ideas, transforms the simple mental representation of God into a form of actual knowledge into the Divine, by allowing the One, universal, divine Idea to take shape—within the context of each specific science—as a distinct, concrete Idea within the realm of reality.
