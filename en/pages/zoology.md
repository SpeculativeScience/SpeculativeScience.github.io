First of all, regarding the concept of the animal organism, only in the animal has life itself attained its highest conceptual form. The principle of unity is no longer a multiplication of individuals, for the growth of the animal is complete and its size is delimited. The multiplicity of forms has been reduced to a series of dependent moments that have significance only within this unified organism. To the extent that the animal possesses this unity within its central organs, its *Self* is completely identical with its entire body. Even less are the animal’s limbs independent entities related to it solely through space and time, like the parts of a machine, nor are they like the geological formations of the Earth that rest side by side in space, after having been piled upon one another in time, such that their unity is posited solely through its concept. In the animal, the center is a *realized* center—one that constantly generates its parts, in terms of their content, out of its own being. The peripheral limbs of the animal—acting as the bodies serving its center—are necessarily united with the organism itself. Thus, the peripheral structures have, through the agency of the center, fused their physical nature—as qualitatively distinct members of the whole—into a single organic form.

In this way, the animal organism is the very microcosm in which all of nature’s forms exist in an ideal state. "The more developed the parts of a whole are," says Müller, "the more dependent they must be upon one another." Thus, the animal organism is the Idea of Nature, to the extent that it is capable of existing within the material realm. As much as the Idea of ​​Nature attains its highest reality in the animal, form has become the absolute master of matter, for matter is posited only through form, and form exists solely within matter. As the form that sustains itself within matter, animal life constitutes an *end*. However, the end of the animal does not succumb to the impotence of letting its *means* develop into independent *ends* of their own (as is the case with the plant), rather it retains all of them under its control. Even if these means do develop into ends, they remain *intermediate ends* that exist entirely in service of the *ultimate end*: the self-preservation of the animal.

It must be acknowledged that, even among empirical physiologists such as Müller, the organism is conceived (following Kant’s guidance) as an activity that constantly operates in accordance with an immanent, necessary, and non-intentional *purposiveness*. We will not dwell upon the fact that he leaves the matter as an open question—suggesting, as he does, "as if organic force and organic matter were merely different modes of viewing the same object." He readily concedes that the resolution of such problems "is not, in general, the task of empirical physiology, but rather that of philosophy." Let it be said that this conjecture is entirely correct—that the purpose of the organism (qua organic form) is nothing other than the active functioning of organic matter (qua potentiality), and that as a result, it does not stand opposed to a external substance or "vital force," but is inherent to the organism itself. Such a form will subsequently reveal itself to be the soul. For the soul is the omnipresent, primordially animating power of the organism—the very power that shapes all the organism's constituent elements, developing them from the initial germ into distinct, articulated members.

From the concept of animal life follows, secondly, the further determinations and predicates thereof, which we ascribe to it as a system of positive judgments:

1. The first of these—the *differentia specifica* distinguishing the animal from the plant—is *sensation*. The animal moves itself by moving its limbs, whereas the plant cannot find itself within its parts, for each of its parts *is* a distinct individual. The animal, however, constitutes the *ideality* of all its parts, as much as the limbs of its body are united with its soul through sensation.

2. Although the animal is aware of the external world, it does not distinguish itself from the external world as an "I" distinguishes itself from a "Not-I." Nevertheless, the external world does become for the animal an *Other* distinct from itself, such that the animal is able to sense itself in opposition to that world. By contrast, the plant, in its multiplicity of individual parts, is always *outside* of itself, being completely unable to detach itself from the external world, remaining perpetually bound up with it. Hence, only the animal is capable of asserting itself against external inorganic nature, and of interrupting its processes in relation to that nature (even if not all of them). The animal eats and drinks only at specific times, whereas the plant is perpetually absorbing nutrients. Breathing, for instance, is such a vital process for life that even animals cannot interrupt it (the exception being animals that can swim).

3) This apprehension of the "Self"—which the animal succeeds in attaining—exerts, thirdly, an influence upon its relationship to space and time. ...to the material realm in general. The animal posits itself within its own space; it is no longer rooted in the earth. And even if the polyp—as a hybrid between animal and plant—remains rooted, its movements, by means of which it captures its food, are nonetheless voluntary. Just as the animal, through free change of location, posits the ideality of abstract space, so too does it posit—within warmth—the ideality of concrete matter. The animal possesses the power to soften matter and to maintain its own organic warmth against the influences of external temperature—influences capable of altering that organic warmth (28–30°) by only a few degrees. Connected with this ideality of the material is the very vibration of the animal’s own corporeality—manifesting as spatiality becoming temporal—as its voice. Through this voice, the animal’s sensation becomes objective to it, as much as the animal senses itself in its speech and thereby gives expression to this sensation. Sensation—which initially merely existed *within itself* (and which subsequently, in the form of interrupted *intussusception*, merely defended itself against the external)—has now completely overcome that externality.

Thirdly, we must consider the classification of the animal organism, a realm in which the various processes of Nature are no longer external to one another, but constitute truly distinct functions of animal life. For here, unlike in the plant, the entire individual is not invariably active in the same manner across its superficially differentiated parts. Every function of the plant—such as growth—is simultaneously a genus process (such that the individual plant never becomes an independent subject *vis-à-vis* its genus). By contrast, the animal first asserts itself as an individual subject—distinct from and independent of its universal genus—through its own internal activity, constituting its internal process of formation. The second function of the animal is its activity directed outward—against the inorganic world—with the aim of subduing and assimilating it. The third element is the individual subject’s relation to its universality, i.e. its genus process. If we may designate the science of the first process as Anatomy—though Physiology invariably plays a role within it, and indeed the second process belongs completely to Physiology—then this latter science extends its scope into the third process as well, before ultimately being taken up by Phylogeny and Medicine. An even better—and certainly more in accord with empirical observation—would be to structure this classification as follows:

1. Anatomy and Physiology, representing the totality of the animal's structures and functions—encompassing formation, assimilation, and reproduction.

2. Phylogeny, wherein the archetype of the animal is now realized in its species, first presenting its abstract moments in isolation, and then uniting them into a totality.

3. Medicine, wherein the living individual finally relates to its genus—not as a single individual, nor merely to its species, but to its genus in its complete universality. Ultimately, the inadequacy of the individual in relation to this genus comes to light and manifests itself as sickness and death.

## Anatomy and Physiology

### Animal Anatomy

#### Systems of Sensitivity

##### Skeletal System

It might seem unusual to attribute sensitivity to bone since it has the appearance of being the most insensitive type of organ, similar to the protective role that bark has for trees. Hence with age, the ossification of bone becomes more and more widespread, whereas babies and children have soft cartilage that has not yet ossified. However we cannot forget that bone indeed has a form of sensation in and through the marrow that it encloses. On the one hand, bone is the insensitive precondition of sensation within, and on the other hand, the marrow enclosed by bone is a self-limited sensitivity. As the inanimate precondition of life, bones are mineral in nature and belong, in a sense, to the calcareous formation in animal life. Because of the inward-looking nature of sensitivity in the skeletal system, its contact with the outside world is still absent, which is why it is also less accessible to sensation. However, some animals have their bone structure on the outside in the same way that trees enclose themselves in layers of wood. 

The skeletal system, as in the geological and vegetative organism, represents its totality as a series of spatial configurations. The bone begins in the child with a bone nucleus, the germ which contains the marrow. These nuclei elongate into lines and the bone becomes the columnar growth of the vertebra, a kind of linear trunk of the animal, and expands into new lines or lateral shoots, such as the ribs. The vertebra is the center of the bone system, just as the leaf forms that of a plant. All bones are merely metamorphoses of the vertebra. It is directly connected to the brain through the medulla oblongata, and through this, the brain and spinal cord interact. The defined surfaces of the shoulder blades then lie posteriorly on the rib lines of the vertebrae, which merge into indeterminate surfaces by curving inwards. This surface finally rounds itself off, on the one hand, into the enclosing surface of the skull, which contains the highest flower of animal life, the brain, and on the other hand, into the pelvis, the shell that encloses the viscera. Thus, the bone, like the layers of wood in trees, has emerged from its interiority into external existence, both to protect the internal parts from external injuries and to support the external activities of the organism by providing internal support.

##### Central Nervous System

Outward-directed sensitivity, appearing in the form of reactivity, is the central nervous system. The sensation that arises from a stimulus in the external world, to which the subject reacts, is reactive sensitivity, which represents the totality of the three moments of the concept, just as inward-directed sensitivity did previously. 

The first moment of reactive sensitivity, has its organ in the nerves of sensation, which are carried along a common nerve. However, since the original fibers in the nerve bundles remain separate from the brain or spinal cord to the periphery, each individual sensation is also perceived separately in the general point of unity. If one crosses the index and middle fingers, and both touch one and the same sphere, one has the sensation of feeling two spheres, because each nerve senses the roundness in the direction it originally had. Where consciousness takes place in the brain, only one point receives the external impression, so only this one reacts to the outside world. 

This reactive side of reactive sensitivity is mediated, secondly, by the nerves of movement. Here, too, the communication originates from the common trunk, only setting individual fibers in motion. Now there are indeed nerves that serve not only sensation or movement, but both functions, in which case they have a double root: nevertheless, sensation is located solely in the anterior roots, and movement in the posterior ones, which alone have ganglia. The activity of the motor fibers, however, must be imagined as if the brain were striking them individually, like the keys of a piano, with the soul as the pianist. 

Thirdly, the point at which the nerves of movement and those of sensation meet is the cerebral system, in which the sensorium commune is the exciter of all nerves that conduct activity. It is then said that the nerves of movement, representing the practical side, originate more from the spinal cord, while the nerves of sensation, representing the theoretical side, originate more from the brain. The nerves thus return from their relationship to the external world, where they have their organs everywhere on the extremities, to the innermost core of the organism in the brain, in order to reproduce their sensations and desires there as at the center. The cerebral system is therefore the organ of reactive sensitivity, and therefore it is intimately connected with the peripheral system, in which sensitivity becomes reproductive in the true sense.

##### Peripheral Nervous System

The sensation of the organism, which no longer senses itself but also the outside world, now has its seat in the peripheral nervous system. These nerves are both sensory and motor: however, the sensations are weaker and more obscure than in the other organs, and its movements tend to be involuntary. The contractions or reactions to stimuli are not temporary and momentary here, but last much longer than the stimuli themselves, precisely because the ganglia reproduce the nervous system as a whole. The cellular tissue that is peculiar to sustainability connects to the ganglia preferentially, while the nerves, as the reactive side of sensitivity, form fibers similar to the muscular system. As the mediator of reproductive sensation, the ganglia are also found in the brain. While the nerve fibers of the brain are white, the ganglia form the gray cellular brain substance, which is said to be the actual vehicle for the conscious perception of sensations in the brain. The nerve fibers are thicker, denser, and exhibit little variety in their structure. The ganglia, in contrast, are extremely thin, soft, and very different from one individual to another, whereas nerve sensations are more uniform among individuals.

#### Systems of Reactivity

##### Muscular System

The muscle is therefore the medium through which both the nerves of sensation and those of movement are active. The stimulated nerve excites contraction in the muscle in which it is distributed. However, the movements in the muscles can also be merely an indirect consequence of sensations, not direct excitations, which are the reflex movements. The two vital qualities of the muscle are the sensitive and contractile responses. The former belongs only to the sensory fibers distributed within it, and not to the muscle itself. Whereas the latter is the essential power of the muscle, which it expresses in response to any kind of stimulation. The stimulus only evokes the original self-movement of the muscle. The effect of the stimulus in the muscle is a vital activity of the muscle itself, an organic reaction, which is directed against the stimulus, as an external influence. Connected with this is the fact that the reaction of the muscle does not always follow the stimulus immediately and directly, but rather a considerable time elapses after the stimulus is applied, before the stimulation occurs. We can also see that the reaction often only grows or spreads after the stimulus is removed — it can even become greater than the stimulus because it results from an internal drive, which would be impossible if the reaction were not different from the effect of the stimulus.

The muscle's reaction to external stimulus is twofold: to contract against its own inner self and from there to expand again, to enable movements, whose fixed point it carries within itself. We can therefore compare this movement to elasticity, except that it now appears in an organic form. On the one hand, the muscle has the flexibility to yield to the mechanical stimulus, to withdraw into itself against it, but on the other hand, it also cancels out this flexibility from within, hardening and bracing itself against it in order to preserve itself. Thus the animal retains control over its own body. However, if the stimulus is to be overcome, it must not be too strong, because otherwise it can result in the paralysis and death of the muscle, as well as its nerves. The double movement of the muscles, as contraction and expansion, is then also manifested in two different organs, the flexor and the extensor muscles. Even if one claims to have found a real increase in cohesion during the contraction by the flexor muscle, it is certainly not a very noticeable one. It would be nothing remarkable at all, since the organic activity of the muscle, in its relationship to the inorganic, must also result in mechanical changes in the muscle.

##### Respiratory System

If blood appears in the muscular system as sensitive reactivity, as the means by which the animal responds to the world through sensation, then blood in the respiratory system denotes the aspect of assimilation in reactivity. Therefore, it does not appear coagulated as in flesh, rather, in its more fluid state it reacts against the outside world and enters into a process with the air, specifically with oxygen, in order to renew itself through inhalation. Hence, the lungs are to the animal what the leaf is to the plant. Blood appears as pure activity, as the process of inflow and outflow, the more detailed nature of which will then appear in animal adaptations where we will have to consider the actual physiological nature of the activity, rather than only anatomically.

##### Circulatory System

As blood is continually renewed and refreshed through this outward-directed activity, its reactivity automatically transforms into sustainability. This sustainable reactivity now fully manifests itself in the circulatory system. As the return of the blood to and from the heart, which moves spontaneously throughout life, this is the contractile muscle of the system that automatically pulsates. Through this internal activity of circulation, the reproduction of the organism as a whole is enabled as every organ is constantly renewed by the reoxigenated blood.

The blood coming from the lungs through the pulmonary veins and, by means of the left ventricle through the body artery (aorta) and branches, driven to the body, restored to its integrity, is bright red; the blood returning through the body veins and, by means of the right ventricle through the pulmonary artery and branches, driven back to the lungs to be refreshed there by breathing, is dark red. Because the blood, as a result of its interaction with all organs, makes them capable of life, it itself becomes incapable of continuing to exert this stimulus necessary for life. The constant transition of arterial blood into venous blood and vice versa—the true life of the blood, whereby the arterial blood generally flows more rapidly—occurs through very fine channels, the capillaries, which can only be seen microscopically. This continuous movement of the blood cannot be explained by mechanical causes, such as impact, since every impact presupposes an external cause. The movement occurs from its own drive, partly in the direction opposite to gravity since the movement of the blood does not cease when the body is completely horizontal. The circulation of blood is thus a periodic contraction (systole) and expansion (diastole), an oscillatory self-movement, the inner trembling of the entire individual life: the temporality of the animal in which the arterial system manifests itself as the pulsation of the blood. The pulse is a pressure of the blood against the elastic arterial walls resulting from the contraction of the ventricle and synchronous with it, and the resulting expansion of the arteries. The regularity of the pulse and a certain duration of its beating are therefore also features of a healthy organism.

#### Systems of Sustainability

##### Integumentary System

The first system of sustainability is the integumentary system, which includes the skin, lymph, and glandular systems. The animal, having been nourished by its blood, reproduces itself in an inconspicuous manner, as the cellular tissue beneath the skin constantly renews itself. The skin covers the entire organism, including its internal organs, and the flesh shelters the nerves from being damaged. In this way, this system of sensitive sustainability is directly connected to pure sensitivity in the bone, as well as sensory reactivity in the muscle. Only where the skeletal system extends entirely into the extremities, as horns, claws, nails, and teeth, is the skin missing and the bone exposed. Lymph is connected to this, which is excreted by every viscera and organ, is obtained everywhere by the skin and is always already prepared by the lymphatic system. The skin, like the glands, are also secretory organs. When a cut has disrupted its continuity, and blood oozes from the wound, a new, abnormal cellular tissue forms, the scab, beneath which the skin regenerates and heals itself, not without revealing the abnormal fusion as a scar.

##### Hepatic System

The hepatic system, also known as the liver system, is the second system of sustainability, the reactive, outward-directed process of sustainability, of which we will have to develop in more detail in the assimilation process. The fluid that the liver uses for the purpose of metabolic processing as its means is bile. 

##### Digestive System

The return of reactive reproduction to true sustainability, or reproductive sustainability, is represented thirdly in the digestive system. The organs of this self-generating system are are the stomach, the small intestine and the large intestine. With their process, the organism's struggle against the outside world is ended, and its triumph over it is sealed. 

The inseparable unity of the various functions and systems that permeate and differentiate one another everywhere. But to understand how the totality of anatomical systems now presents itself in reality, we must consider its physiology.

### Animal Physiology

With the study of animal behavior, we have taken the first step out of the domain of morphology into that of physiology. Yet, just as the latter has often played a role in the former, so the latter will now resonate in the former. It goes without saying that all three functions of the animal organism will be at work here again because they all relate to the external world in their own way. Thus the life of the animal predominates here, but not without also passing over into organic life as a result of its processes. Furthermore, the function of reactivity predominates because assimilation is the animal's process of relating to the external world that stimulates it. As in the formative process, in which the the sensory system sets the tone, so now reactivity predominates in physiology, even though all three functions are present here. Physiology in the sensitivity is the receptivity of the senses, as reactivity it is adaptation to the environment, and as reproduction it is the process of digestion.

### Receptivity

The animal organism, which has come to a sense of self in the process of formation, firstly senses its self as all being, it becomes the microcosm in which all of nature is reflected. While this is the infinite multiplicity of existence, the subjectivity of the animal, on the other hand, is the simplicity into which this multiplicity withdraws itself. The modes of this return are a specific multiplicity, the number of the concept, the trinity according to the qualities of nature that were previously considered in inorganic physics. But since in nature the moments of the concept appear in their differentiated independence, the opposition, as the middle that unites the extremes with one another, is thus itself a duality, we have here a quaternity. Because the opposition is precisely the opposition of identity and opposition, as opposition it contains two terms, as identity one, and thus itself decomposes into three moments. However, there cannot be more senses than these five, because their object, in the properties of nature, represents the complete totality of the concept within it. An animal, however, can have fewer than these five if it is at a lower stage of development. Firstly, the senses are the immediate apprehension of the external world through the sense of sight, which leaves things as they are. Secondly, the mediated senses of hearing, smell and taste, which directly interact with the thing in order to determine the animal's relationship to it. And thirdly, the sense of touch, which unlike the other four, does not deal with abstract properties of nature, but actively shapes the concrete materiality of the thing both in its existence and in its dissolution.

#### Immediate Sense (Sight)

In the sense of sight, the self and its object are the furthest apart. The eye faces infinite space and grasps it through light and its colors. Assimilation here is entirely ideal, light and color are not a part of the seen object, which the eye absorbs. Only the entirely ideal is absorbed: but in doing so, the eye, because the organism itself is the ideal unity, nevertheless absorbs its object entirely into itself, while at the same time leaving it outside completely untouched.

#### Mediating Senses (Hearing - Smell - Taste)

Hearing is no longer a spatial sense, but a sense that negates spatiality by interpreting the vibrations of objects in space as sound. In this respect, the concept of hearing is already a mediated sense. However, hearing does not yet play a definitive role in the practical interests of the animal, in the sense that hearing is only receptive and not interactive. As the sense of spatial vibrations, hearing is the sense of time since sound necessarily takes place in time as well as space. The sounds that are interpreted by the ear do not clearly resolve the properties of the object perceived, the animal generally only distinguishes between what is heard as something loud or quiet.

If the vibrations in the sense of hearing transitions from the negation of the material back to the positive, of time that returns to place, then now sense begins with smell as the real dissolution of the material object. Thus, even if hearing is still immediate in its apprehension of the object, the relationship of sense to the real, total assimilation of the object that occurs with digestion begins with smell. Smell is the precursor to taste, in that it tests the food in advance through the nose before dissolving it through taste. However, smell still retains some immediacy as it does not, unlike taste, bring about the total, sudden dissolution of its food by eating it. The process of smell starts with the air, which is the basis of the sense of smell, and it is simply the slow, inconspicuous decomposition of the object in the sense of smell, whereby the object thus decomposed is conveyed to the olfactory nerve through the mucous membranes of the nose. If the eye did not yet perceive anything from the rose, we cannot at all claim that the nose does not smell anything from it. It is a rose dissolved in air that enters the nose and communicates with the nerves located there. This air is sucked in through breathing and passes into the blood of the organism.

The immediate assimilation that relates entirely to the mediated nutritional process is taste. Taste is the immediate assessment of food to determine whether it is pleasing to the senses. The more pleasing the food is to taste, the better the senses will contribute to nutrition, since the organism will embrace the foods that are more palatable with greater readiness. While in sight and hearing the objective existence of the object is not shaken, or at least ultimately emerges untouched from the process, we cannot say the same of the two mediated senses. In taste, this identification is so great that the tongue muscle sends saliva through the salivary glands toward the dry object of its desire in order to dissolve it and convey it to the nerves of the tongue. For the dryness of the tongue eliminates the possibility of tasting Since dissolution in water is the route of communication for taste, the taste buds are particularly susceptible to "chemical influences."

#### Self-Mediating Sense (Touch)

The sense of touch constitutes the totality of sensory assimilation, and as this general sense, it is also called feeling in general. In it, sensitivity reveals itself not as bound to a single point in space, not as limited to a single natural quality. Matter as a whole, primarily its quantitative determination, comes to sensation in every part of the organism. Yet here the theoretical and practical sides are united. On the one hand, we perceive matter in its mechanical totality as existing, in its cohesion and shape. But the other hand we also perceive it in its dissolution, in the loss of cohesion or deformation of shape. The inorganic warmth, which the animal always has within itself, absorbs external warmth and always measures it against the latter. We always judge external warmth in inverse proportion to internal warmth, so that the heat appears less intense to us the hotter we ourselves are until both degrees of temperature have somewhat equalized. But even here, warmth is increasingly to be regarded as simply theoretically perceived as it is absorbed by the organism.

### Adaptivity

In adaptation, the qualities of nature do not appear simply as dependent moments of inorganic nature that are absorbed into the organism, but rather as the inorganic forces that confront the organism and force it to a. These forces are the same ones that we have already seen determining cellular life, i.e. light, air, and water. The process with them is the irritative side of assimilation. If the qualities of nature could only be assimilated by the organism through theoretical processes because it carries their elemental roots within its senses, now through the stimulus that this relatedness arouses in it, it will also awaken within itself the capacity to simultaneously transform these elements practically by reducing them to specific moments of itself.

#### Adaptation to Light

The organism does not relate to light merely as seeing, rather it absorbs the specificity of light in its entire external form. The influence of light is initially limited to the surface of the body. For instance, if you fluff up a bird's feathers, the covered ones are paler than the uncovered ones. But light not only affects specific parts of the organism, but also its entire being when the absence of light at night causes the animal to fall asleep. There are of course exceptions to the rule, such as bats and owls that sleep during the day. In general, however, animals are active and express their nature during the day. Since the lengthening and shortening of the day is seasonal, animals are affected by the seasons as well. During the winter, the lack of light and warmth causes some animals to hibernate, the highest degree of which progresses to insensitivity. Whereas in the aestivation of reptiles and amphibians it is the exact opposite, an excess of warmth during the summer triggers a state of dormancy. For the excess in either directions nullifies nervous reactivity. If the process with light belongs more to sensitive reactivity, the one with air belongs to the purely reactive.

#### Adaptation to Air

Since air, as potential plasma (fire), can be awakened by compression, we can designate it as the seat of elementary reactivity, which stimulates the organic element, the blood, so that it struggles against it in order to assimilate it and thereby renew itself. This is, secondly, the respiratory process, whose organ we have already recognized in the lungs. In fish, these are the gills. Insects have tracheas distributed throughout their bodies, which are also said to be the organs of humming. The mechanical aspect of the process is the expansion of the chest cavity and the lungs during inhalation through the activity of the muscles, with the diaphragm in particular contracting. During exhalation, when the chest cavity is reduced, the respiratory muscles relax and the lungs are compressed, with the abdominal muscles now contracting.

Chemical beginnings also occur in this process, which are always taken back into organic circulation and dissolved. Nevertheless, respiration is also chemical process. The lungs actively filter the air they inhale, retaining the oxygen, and expelling the carbon dioxide and nitrogen. The exhaled air is no longer respirable, no longer suitable for the animal. However, the absorption of oxygen purifies the blood. The arterial blood, which flows freshly into the body, therefore has more oxygen, while the used-up, darker, venous blood, which flows back to be refreshed, contains more carbon dioxide. Therefore in corpses, one finds not red blood, but venous blood, as is the case with strokes in the brain. For the same reason, arterial blood is said to be somewhat warmer and lighter than venous blood, the lungs are the source of warmth, the heart is warmer than the extremities, and in animals hibernating, the lower warmth comes from less breathing. It must be noted that breathing, however, is not a chemical process, at least not a complete one. It does not result in combustion, not even oxidation, it only prepares the reactants that cellular respiration requires. And yet, metabolism is still much more than a chemical process. In the latter, the two chemical substances act against each other such that a third (or fourth) can arise. In the organic process, on the other hand, the organism maintains itself at the expense of the inorganic. 

#### Adaptation to Water

The adaptation to water is the dissolution of the tension described in the second process, it is the process of neutralization. The animal desires water one of its essential metabolic needs, as well as purposing it as a means to eliminate excess internal heat thus. The animal neutralizes and brings itself to homeostasis by absorbing water and releasing it as sweat through the pores of its skin. Through both aspects of the process, through drinking and sweating, the water process manifests itself as reproducible reactivity. For the organism, by drinking water, restores itself by satisfying this external excitation, and sweat, like mucus, is itself a result of the productive activity of the cutaneous system, in which the organism maintains its health by expelling what cannot be purposed by its body.

### Digestion

True reproductive assimilation, however, only occurs in the digestive process, where the animal no longer relates to environment but to individual substances in order to appropriate them and maintain itself through their absorption. The organic is completely different in content from the inorganic it has assimilated such that nothing inorganic remains of it. Because the inorganic is shaped and purposed the organic, it cannot resist the organic's power. Conversely, however, a chemical process can never produce the higher nature of the organic from itself, only the organic can produce the organic.

The beginning of the process is the mechanical grasping of food by claws, hands, mouth, and teeth. The stimulus for this activity is hunger, which causes it to grasp that which is inherently organic. An animal has the innate instinct to grasp only what is suitable for its food and it is rarely mistaken in this. What is thus grasped, chewed in the mouth and mixed with saliva, then passes through swallowing between the surface of the tongue and the palate into the esophagus – thereby closing the larynx and glottis – and from there to the stomach, where it is partially dissolved as gastric pulp.

While we can call direct digestion assimilation through sensitive reproduction, as the organs of sensitive reproduction, indirect digestion belongs to the organs of reactivity and the system of irritative reproduction. For it occurs through the mediation of the liver, spleen and gallbladder, until the digested substances are actually delivered to the entire organism. Mediated digestion belongs only to the mammals in general, especially those which have more than one stomach. The necessity of mediated digestion lies in the fact that the organic does not merely react, as in immediate digestion, as a subjective end apart from the external world in general, but that in the place of the merely external object, it now sets its own object in order to make the already assimilated its means. The already transformed food once again becomes the object of organic activity, which now no longer processes something foreign to it, but as something homogeneous to it.

Towards the end of mediated digestion, while actually adding nothing to digestion, only represents the completion of the process precisely in that the organic subject has now completely returned to itself. This return is also evident in the fact that lymph, arising from nutrition, also passes into the blood. This is in turn supplemented by the lymphatic system, communicating itself to all limbs, enlivening them as it absorbs them, directly and without the mediation of the lymphatic vessels. However, even without digestion, the skin secretes this lymph during the formative process, since all limbs participate in the general life of the organism in order to sustain themselves. It also goes without saying that organic heat is generated through this constant dissolution of matter during digestion. Through regeneration, however, reproduction returns to formative processes, and the cycle of the individual's processes, as such, is completed. The negative result is then this: as the organism has become involved with itself, has overcome its own determination, it has also worn out the means it used to fulfill its purpose, and thus expels it as waste. 

Since the organic has thus found the inorganic within itself, itself in the external, it is related to itself in the other and with itself. It thus begins to rise into universality: to step out of the confines of its individuality and to relate itself as an individual to the species.

### Animal Reproduction

In the genus process, the more theoretical process of formation—as well as the more practical process of assimilation—finds the opposition between the theoretical and the practical processes completely resolved. For in the genus process, since the inorganic realm to which the organic relates has itself become organic, the organic has practically assimilated the inorganic while allowing it to persist theoretically as its "Other." Thus, while the individual—by overcoming its "Other" in this manner—elevates itself beyond its own individuality toward the universal, it nevertheless remains, despite this elevation, confined within its individuality. The relationship remains a relationship between individuals, even though the *species* is the result of the process. Thus, the genus process serves only to complete the archetype of the individual.

The genus process is structured as follows: the individual first appropriates the inorganic nature surrounding it and establishes itself within it as a universal, as the *species*—this constitutes the *instinct*. It is no longer merely excrement that the individual repels with disgust, but it is inorganic nature itself, which the individual reshapes to form its environment. This represents, once again, the more theoretical aspect—grounded in sensibility—which springs from animal instinct. As a result, the individual practically transforms *itself* into its own inorganic nature by reshaping itself—as such an entity—and unfolding through various stages of its development. However, in doing so, however, it becomes the *species*—the metamorphosis of the animal. Finally, the
individual arrives at the point of combining this second, more practical process—just
as it did with the theoretical one—in that the other
individual with which it enters into relation is truly
another of the same species, which it both theoretically contemplates
and with which it practically unites in order to
bring forth the species; – the sexual relationship, which here
first truly emerges as a species-process—as reproduction—
whereas the first two processes are merely the surrogates of the sexual relationship.

### Instinct

With instinct, the animal manifests itself as acting purposefully in that it renders its inorganic environment conformable to its ends. The animal reshapes this environment, thereby employing it as a means for its own existence. Foremost among the phenomena here is the practical application of the fundamental characteristic of the animal organism: to establish its own place for itself. Instead, instinct impels animals to seek out the specific place, terrain, and climate that are most conducive to their well-being during the various seasons, i.e. the migratory drive. Fish vanish from certain seas only to reappear elsewhere, and in particular, they flee from steamships on rivers. Migratory birds, such as swallows and larks, migrate in vast flocks toward more southerly regions at the onset of winter, only to return again in the spring. Storks gather at a designated rendezvous point to set forth together, and then return to the very nests they had abandoned.

The second point, however, is that animals do not leave the place they have voluntarily chosen exactly as it is, but give it a specific form, preparing a resting place for themselves, and gathering provisions for the winter. Birds build nests, beavers build their lodges upon the water, and even insects have their own nests. We may term this the true *artistic* instinct—an instinct that stands in such a relationship to the sexual instinct that it actually precedes it. Birds do not copulate until they have constructed their nests.

Thirdly, the artistic instinct serves, to some extent, as a substitute for copulation. For in this instance, the animal does not merely seek out or reshape a location, but generates it from within its body. To this category belongs bees that secrete honey and construct their honeycomb cells from it. These worker bees are said to be asexual, or according to Müller, to possess only "imperfect ovaries," and are thus to be regarded as "infertile females, or at least females whose sexual development has been arrested." Among ants, the corresponding role is played by "the wingless individuals, who are charged with the care and feeding of the larvae." This specific occupation—this exercise of artistic skill—renders the sexual instinct superfluous for such insects. The male bees—as well as the reproductive female, the queen, with her longer body—do not, by contrast, engage in labor. As a result, once their reproductive task is complete, the drones are either killed or starved to death by the worker bees—much like the Amazons of legend. Other animals—those that are not asexual—produce from their reproductive organs not only their weapons, but also their dwellings—just as the spider produces its web—employing these means to defend themselves and ensnare their prey.

If we can recognize in the migratory instinct an expression of the animal's *sensibility*, whereby it senses out what place is beneficial to it, then the construction of a dwelling is a matter of *irritability*, as much as it constitutes a practical activity directed towards the world. But when the animal finally generates its environment out of its very self, therein lies its productive return to itself, and simultaneously, the highest stage in the development of instinct.

### Metamorphosis

If we may already regard instinct as a preliminary step toward sexual reproduction, then the metamorphosis of animals constitutes the second. It is a remarkable phenomenon that certain animals, insects in particular, do not generate something inorganic, such as a dwelling, out of themselves, but completely transform *themselves* into a different form of organism—such as when a caterpillar pupates and later emerges as winged insect. The first two forms are asexual, as much as they—like plants—grow and develop without copulation. The caterpillar first feeds and secretes during the summer, and for the winter, it then spins itself into a pupa. Finally, in the spring, the pupa emerges as a butterfly, and it is only this form that engages in copulation and lays eggs, from which caterpillars once again emerge. The same applies to May beetles, flies, ants, and so forth. Here, it is no longer an external material to which an inorganic form is imparted, but the organism's own individuality that is reshaped and transformed into a different form possessing entirely different characteristics. Taken collectively, these characteristics belong to the animal as a species, but this relationship does not yet constitute a free interaction between one individual and another. In insects, pupation is evidently a kind of compensation—an analogy of the sexual instinct—just as we have already observed in the case of plants.

### Sexual Reproduction

If the reproductive relationship of one individual to another is at once *sensible*—as the self-awareness of each within the other—and *irritable*—as the stimulus of desire of the one toward the other—then we have the *sexual reproduction*: the highest process within the archetype of the individual, for here individuals truly generate the *species* through their offspring. Reproduction now becomes the *production* of a *new* individual, within which the old individual recognizes itself as identical with itself in the new individual. However, the deficiency of the animal, and of Nature in general by extension, lies in this: that the species is not produced *as* a species—that the universal does not become an *object* for the individual. This individual would be Spirit, and its universal object would be Thought. Since the species only manifests itself as the self-awareness of the individual *within* the individual, it relapses into mere individuality, i.e. the offspring. Prior to copulation, the species exists merely as self-awareness, and after copulation, it exists as a state of exhaustion within the outcome of this supreme process. Aristotle, therefore, observes in numerous passages that the consequences typically ensuing after copulation include dejection, melancholy, physical weakness, and various other infirmities. Thus, the old individual reveals its inadequacy vis-à-vis the species in that it is enervated by the very act of engaging with it, as is especially the case with certain insects, where the male perishes immediately after copulation, and the female shortly after laying her eggs. This inadequacy will manifest itself both in the organs involved in the process and in the various phases of its activity, as well as in the ultimate logical culmination it embodies.

#### Sex Organs

Firstly, regarding the organs of the sexual reproduction, the individual—precisely because it is not the species—is a one-sided entity. Only when taken together with its other—the individual finding itself in the other, and the other finding itself in it—does the individual constitute the totality of the species. Thus, the individuals that engage in copulation are of different sexes, a fact manifest in the formation of the sexual organs and the rest of the body. The process does not take place within a single individual, but is distributed between two individuals, who, precisely through their union, attains self-awareness of the species. The moments of this process are, however, the active and the passive, the universal and the particular, the form and the material. This manifests itself as the opposition of the masculine and the feminine. Initially, however, prior to the development of the fetus, the external sex organs are identical in both sexes, and only later does sexual differentiation take place.

Furthermore, Oken has demonstrated a complete analogy between the generative organs of both sexes even *after* differentiation—subject only to the aforementioned difference in functions, from which it follows that the active, male organ is more prominent and external, whereas the passive, female organ is, conversely, less prominent and recessed inward. Finally, that in one sex, according to its nature, one aspect is more essential, while in the other, the other aspect is more essential. Thus, the ovary corresponds to the testis, and the penis corresponds to the clitoris, which, by contrast, the sensation remains passive. The uterus corresponds—according to Ackermann—to the prostate, and the labia to the scrotum. The sole difference in the latter case, for instance, is that the raphe—the median line of the scrotum—has opened up within the female labia to form the vagina.

#### Procreation

Secondly, if we consider the process of sexual reproduction itself—procreation—it constitutes the highest pinnacle of the animal life, wherein the active and the passive elements unite to form a unity in which the sexes vanish as distinct entities, and the species comes into being. According to Aristotle (Metaph. VIII, 4), the male sperm is the activating form of the process and the female egg is held to be the matter. Although, this matter essentially includes the solid ovum itself, as it is detached by the activation and fuses with the sperm, it falls from the ovary into the womb and develops there. Yet both sexes contribute both matter and form, just as the child bears a resemblance to both parents.

The act of the procreation must not be understood as merely material. It is the union of two beings as the species itself, undertaking the actual creation of a new individual as sperm and egg—"that which is already organized and that which strives toward organization"—merge with one another in terms of form and matter. Among the animals that—like plants, are composed of multiple individuals (such as planarians, polyps, hydras, and naids)—young individuals detach themselves, either spontaneously or artificially, and become autonomous entities, or alternatively, the detached limbs, buds, or fragments develop—through mere growth—into new individuals. Some animals, such as insects, spiders, and crustaceans, are capable only of regenerating their detached limbs, without these limbs ever developing into new, distinct individuals. The most developed animals are those in which also form embryos, but whose development occurs solely through the fertilization of the egg by sperm, mediated by two individuals, which constitutes the very first act of generation.

The second stage concerns the development of the fetus—a process already recognized in certain respects by Aristotle—whether this occurs within the womb, through incubation mediated by the mother's organic warmth, or even through the sun, as when eggs are laid in hot sand. Here, the physiologist asks whether the germs, limbs, and so forth are newly formed, or whether everything was already present from the very beginning: the former represents the doctrine of Epigenesis, while the latter corresponds to Bonnet’s theory of *emboîtement* (encasement) (*Considerations sur les corps organisés*, Vol. I, pp. 2, 87–89; Vol. II, p. 230). Both are true: *Insofar* as everything is involved [present in potential], it is *also* newly formed, in that everything takes shape from cells—cells which, in part, constitute the whole through their explicit union, and in part, as germ cells, contain the whole *in potentia* (i.e., possess the power to generate all the specifically organized cells), and whose germ layer differentiates into an inner organic lamina and an outer animal lamina.

The genesis of all embryonic structures from the yolk begins with the central component of the animal nervous system and concludes with the mucous membrane of the intestine. In the bird egg, the auxiliary systems of the nervous system—the integumentary system and the vertebral system—are developed first, then the mediating circulatory system develops, and finally, the intestinal system, which supports the mucous membrane, is formed—though the first indications of the extremities become perceptible *before* the closure of the intestinal tube has commenced. In mammals and humans, the initial formation of the embryo proceeds in a manner similar to embryo formation observed in birds. Specifically regarding humans, at the beginning of the second month, the embryo has a length ranging from a few lines to half an inch. The villi of the outer membrane of the ovum already draws nourishment from within, even before the embryo's blood vessels have formed and penetrated the outer membrane of the ovum: serving to supply the mother's blood directly into the blood of the fetus. The reciprocal interaction between these two elements takes the place of respiration, which is also why, in the fetal circulatory system, a portion of the blood is diverted away from the lungs.

The third stage, the conclusion of the procreation, is birth. Here, the mother, as Schultz-Schultzenstein wittily observes, gives birth to her child in the same manner that a snake sheds its skin. He argues that it is not the mother who gives birth, but the child that presses toward the light of day, casting off the mother as if she were an old skin. Against this view, one need only note that the process involves not the efforts of the child—straining through contractions to liberate itself by advancing head-first with knees drawn up behind—but equally the corresponding efforts of the mother to facilitate her own relief by bracing her limbs, holding her breath, etc., such that birth only ever results from this reciprocal interaction. But by means of a qualitative leap, the child now exchanges its plant-like mode of nutrition within the womb for an animal-like one: it draws atmospheric air into its lungs, begins to breathe, and cries out.

While Müller deems it improbable that "the stimulus of atmospheric air upon the cutaneous nerves, being transmitted to the spinal cord, should trigger respiration as a reflex movement", and while Müller himself asserts that "the cause of respiration is arterial blood"—blood "which is formed upon the initial influx of air into the respiratory organs" and which, "saturated with oxygen," stimulates the respiratory nerves—one fails to perceive any genuine contradiction between these two views. For in both instances (and in the latter case, indirectly) it is ultimately the air that, through the oxygenation of the blood, stimulates the respiratory nerves. The resulting offspring—initially a neutral product, the child—lastly reverts to individuality, belonging once again to one sex or the other.

#### Lineage

The complete process of sexual reproduction appears as a complete inference in which the species itself—as that which unites the sexes—forms the *medius terminus*. This is an inference of the third figure (P–U–I), since different sexes (P) of the same species (U) have produced a new individual (I) of that species. It is surely beyond all doubt that the species must be placed in the middle, but the remaining question is how to characterize the extremes. It is equally beyond doubt that the sperm also denotes particularity because it encompasses the species (U) within itself in the form of individuality, and the egg denotes individuality because it contributes the individual matter to be formed. Since the masculine is the universal form—according to Aristotle (*Metaphysics* I, 7)—it imparts this form, through the act of insemination, to the individual instance of matter. The egg, however—as individuality as such—is fertilized by an individual act of copulation. Therefore, the egg, by encountering the universality of the species within the sperm, invariably reduces that very universality back down to the level of individuality in the child, such that the species presents itself only as an infinite series of new individuals in Nature.

With this, however, the transition into phylogeny is simultaneously established. The concluding proposition of the third figure reads P=I, meaning that the species, taking the form of a particular universal, constitutes the individual. Thus, the individual is no longer immediately related to its species, but only mediately, through a particularity. The individual, as we observed, is not commensurate with the genus, it does not represent the archetype of the animal in its full purity and perfection, but is simply a particular manifestation of this archetype, one that corresponds to it more or less closely. Thus, within the animal kingdom, we find a system of species that, ascending in stages from the lowest to the highest, progressively approximate the archetype of the animal, and to trace the development of this sequence is precisely the subject matter of phylogeny. In the realm of plants, to be sure, we also distinguished between lower and higher classes—monocotyledons and dicotyledons, cryptogams and phanerogams, apical growth and columnar growth. But even this distinction was superfluous—much like the process of generic differentiation itself—because in the plant, the totality is contained within every part. This is not the case with the animal, for since its various members perform distinct functions, these functions are realized—more or less developed, more or less fully present—in one animal compared to another. Thus, the graduated sequence of species acquires a genuine significance here, and so phylogeny also falls under the third figure of the inference (P–U–I). In truth, however, there are three genus processes at work here—

In sexual reproduction, the species still bears the significance of individuality since the individual produces the species by producing another individual of its kind. 

In phylogeny, the universal assumes the significance of particularity by disjoining itself into particulars, constituting a disjunctive inference in which the individual is linked to the genus through its species, precisely because the genus unfolds itself as the totality of its species. 

In medicine, it is only in sickness and death that the universal truly acquires the significance of the genus—a significance, however, against which the individual perishes (since it cannot become a positive object *for* the individual), thereby effecting the transition to Spirit.

## Phylogeny



## Medicine

Nature’s striving to transcend itself stems from the fact that we have observed it arriving at a contradiction. This contradiction lies in the fact that the organism—as a natural entity—always remains an individual while coming into contact with its universal—the species—while simultaneously perishing in the process since it cannot become fully adequate to it. For the individual’s initial relation to its species was such that the latter appeared to it only as a another individual of the same species. To the extent that both individuals, in and of themselves, represent the species, they negate themselves while simultaneously positing another individual. The species remains something external to them, yet they also possess it within themselves, such that they—in their individuality—are themselves also species, that is, a particularity in which the genus is posited not in and for itself, but only in one of its specific determinations. The first contradiction—that of the sexual relation—was resolved within the feeling of the genus. However, since this feeling—in its individuality—could not attain to true universality, a second contradiction arose therefrom: namely, that the individual represented the genus solely in a particularized form. The dialectic of zoology finally reached a stage where this contradiction, too, was resolved—specifically, when the individual elevated itself to the level of the *species*, which is adequate to the *genus*. The individual has realized, within its own form, the archetype of the organism—an archetype that was initially merely an idea residing within its individuality. Yet, precisely because it has realized this archetype solely within the confines of its empirical individuality, a third contradiction has thereby emerged—one at which we now stand. As an individual entity, the animal no longer has the genus external to itself; rather, the genus resides entirely within the animal itself. Yet, by this very fact, the contradiction becomes all the more immanent, for it lies in the very nature of the individual not to correspond perfectly to the genus—which it nevertheless contains within itself. Thus, the individual perishes in relation to the genus manifesting within it. The Judgment—the primordial division of the Concept—takes place within the individual itself. The essence of the individual is the genus, but is simultaneously merely a particular instance of the genus. The individual qua subject stands in relation to the predicate—its own universality—by virtue of its own process of particularization.

To the extent that all the particularities of the organism correspond to the species, we ascribe to it the attribute of health. This represents the *telos*—the ultimate goal—of medical science, yet strictly speaking, this goal falls outside the scope of medicine proper, having already been treated within the domain of physiology. The existential ground of medicine lies, rather, in the *disturbance* of health—in the very contradiction and failure of the individual, in all its particularities, to correspond to the genus. Medical science is therefore the endeavor to resolve this contradiction—to preserve or restore health. With this, the internal division of this science is also established. First, the phenomenon of a particularity detaching itself from the harmony of the whole organism constitutes *disease*—the subject matter of pathology or nosology. Second, the struggle of this particularity against the universality and its subsequent return to that universality—constitutes *healing*—the subject matter of therapeutics. However, since health constantly relapses into illness—as much as particularity repeatedly separates itself from universality—the complete negation of all illness is, thirdly, the complete dissolution of all particularities into the universal, wherein the genus appears as that which now alone asserts itself within the individual in death. While this is the end of the individual, it is also the end of Nature, as well as the beginning of the genus, as such, within Spirit.
