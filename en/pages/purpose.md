# The Purpose of Philosophy

Note: This webpage is a direct translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Encyclopedia Logic* (1876) 

The essence of philosophy is best discerned from its purpose. For purpose is, after all, the concept brought into application—the concept realized. Thus, when Cicero and Boethius speak of the consolation that philosophy affords, or when, in everyday life, people speak of a "philosophical demeanor" in the face of pain or misfortune, we can say that philosophy first reveals itself to us by the fact that it repairs and heals a rift or fragmentation, much as Friar Laurence, in *Romeo and Juliet*, reminds the hero, anguished by his banishment, of the "sweet milk of philosophy." What Schiller, in his poem *Melancholy to Laura*, says of love applies equally to philosophy. If he conceives of himself and his beloved as the shattered fragments of the Divine, then love seeks to rejoin these fragments into a whole:

>Hence this burning yearning

>To sink—dying—into your very being,

>When our glances beckon.

But this restoration cannot be an immediate one, as when Romeo, casting himself upon the ground in Friar Laurence’s cell, clumsily demands:

>If she cannot create a Juliet,

>Nor alter a Prince’s decree,

>Then philosophy is worthless.

Philosophy proceeds from a state of incoherence and incongruity to which it seeks to resolve. Philosophy is the coherence of this incoherence, and the need for such coherence constitutes its very premise. However, since the original natural unity is not restored, the resolution of incoherence, being one posited and mediated by the spirit, assumes a new and distinct form, constituting a step beyond that immediacy.

Yet if we examine more closely the nature of this dichotomy, its most general form, the widest rift within humanity, is the rift between the outer world and our inner self, the rift between the world of the object and the world of the subject. Philosophy is the arrow that inflicts this wound, and yet, like that fabled arrow of legend, it is the same arrow that heals it. This rift erupts within entire nations just as it does within individuals. Religion stands at the cradle of a people, and faith serves as its nurse. Once the faculty of reflection has matured, however, this trust in tradition is shaken. Enlightenment breeds doubt, such as it did among the Greeks during the Peloponnesian War, and among the Germans during second half of the eighteenth century. However, philosophy ultimately seeks to bring forth a higher harmony within the inner chambers of thought. While Hegel observed that the owl of philosophy, having once painted its "grey upon grey," cannot rejuvenate the world’s antiquated form, I would nonetheless supplement this proposition by asserting that through this inner reconciliation, much like the crowing of a new dawn, rises a higher form of reality. As for the individual, however, we know how rarely our aspirations correspond to our actual achievements, or our intentions to their outcomes. How seldom are our ideals realized! How often does reality fall far short of our hopes! This fragmentation is no less prevalent in the realm of theory than it is in the realm of practice. Who does not encounter epistemic contradictions that they yearn to resolve? How frequently does a burning thirst for knowledge remain unquenched! How often does even the most unwavering diligence fail to yield the solution to a difficult problem! "Art is long, and life is short," Faust’s Famulus cites from Hippocrates. The truly tragic element of Goethe’s *Faust* lies precisely in the portrayal, imbued with the highest pathos, of the attempt to resolve these contradictions. *Faust* is the truly philosophical tragedy, it is the tragedy of philosophy.

If we ask what it is that we actually seek when we approach philosophy, it is surely the highest demands that we place upon it, demands identical to those that it, in turn, places upon the philosopher who completely entrusts themselves to it. We expect philosophy to solve all the riddles of the the universe surrounding us, that it will lift the veil shrouding the mysterious workshop of Nature. Likewise, we expect philosophy to solve the riddles of our own hearts, and the riddles of our history, that it will reveal to us the plan of Providence and the ultimate purpose of the world, allowing us to ascend into the Divine. Conversely, the individual is entitled to such hopes only if they suspend all their other notions, opinions, and interests to this Supreme Ideal. If we fix our gaze steadfastly upon that realm where all finite things dissolve, and where the eye apprehends only the One, the Eternal, and the Unconditioned, then from this higher vantage point, the finite will be illuminated by the light of the Infinite.

Broadly speaking, this is the "lofty" conception of philosophy held by everyone, however varied their other views regarding it may be. Admittedly, we are immediately confronted here by the objection that this would indeed be the true aim of philosophy, were its attainment only possible, and were it truly within man's power to arrive at a knowledge of eternal truth. 

1. Since man is a finite being in the first place, he cannot, as Kant maintains, transcend the limits of his own cognitive faculties. Critical philosophy teaches us to acknowledge these limits, so that the philosophically educated individual may henceforth be cured of the madness of fanaticism, that urge to reach all the way to the heavens with the edifice of philosophy, much like Schiller’s Hans Metaphysikus. The roofer may instead attain a sober prudence, contenting himself with the dwelling-house of experience here on earth, so as to be able to survey its corridors. 

2. Precisely because man is a finite being, it follows—secondly— that he can only recognize that which is finite. This finite knowledge attainable to man would merely be knowledge of the transient and untrue. 

3. While Aristotle cites the words of Simonides, that it is unworthy of man to strive after that which is not meant for him, others conversely assert—thirdly—that while philosophy strives for knowledge of the eternal and the true, one is never actually capable of attaining it. *In magnis,* they add, *voluisse sat est* (in great matters, the small will suffice). This modesty is then carried to such an extreme that, following Cicero, ignorance is elevated to the ultimate goal, or even the supreme principle, of philosophy.

4. In this state of "not-knowing," however—fourthly—one may still love the truth: just as Pythagoras, when asked by the tyrant Leon of Phlius what he was, replied that he was a *philosophos*, that is, a person who loves wisdom, whether or not they actually possess it. Such contentment then indulges in a Pharisaical gaze, eyes turned heavenward and declaring: "Lord, I thank Thee that I am not like those others, who seek to storm the heavens like Titans." 

5. Thus, the fifth and final outcome of the Kantian standpoint is Jacobi's doctrine: that Truth cannot be known, but only believed, that philosophy is a positive science (as the Neo-Schellingians assert), and that it must begin with the presupposition of faith, denying that reason cannot, by itself, arrive at reality.

This ignorance that has spread itself into philosophy, puffs itself up, and brazenly flaunts itself as truth is *unphilosophy*. The greatest joy and jubilation arose among men at this discovery, for they were now, in a most convenient manner, relieved of the burden of thinking. Thus, incoherence has infiltrated philosophy itself, the very discipline that was destined to resolve it. This despair is a sign of the dissolution and disintegration of our age, but at the same time, our age has become a parched soil that is ripe for philosophy. Indeed, philosophy could find a no more receptive ground than this one, for philosophy arises precisely from the need to resolve contradictions. In human affairs, people have now reached the point of abandoning everything firm and steadfast. Man wanders along the dark path of life, and with every step he attempts to take forward he stumbles over a new knot. To the extent that this very viewpoint has become a philosophical assertion, seeking to establish itself as science, signifies nothing other than that philosophy is effecting its own self-destruction. It is no wonder that philosophy, as the highest of disciplines, has forfeited all trust and respect from the public, seeing as it has thereby ceased to trust or respect itself. Nevertheless, philosophy cannot let go of its purpose, and criticism must ultimately arrive at the point of at least *believing* in the Truth, having despaired of ever *knowing* it.

However, even within this view regarding the unknowability of Truth, two distinct forms of it must be distinguished and recognized. In one manifestation of skepticism, one can find traces of the true purpose of philosophy, a trace that has vanished entirely in the other manifestation. To the extent that this first viewpoint still aspires to be philosophy, it also seeks to calm and satisfy the mind. The mind refrains from striving for a knowledge that it recognizes as being denied to it. The despair of unknowability can engender this spiritual certitude, the assurance that nothing can disrupt your unshakable equanimity. According to this skeptic, everything that surrounds the individual is not the Truth, only semblances that vanish. The only steadfast element amidst this flux of all things is the individual themselves—the I—that has arrived at this conclusion. This conclusion is the Truth and the Unconditioned itself, and thus the individual is blissful and satisfied with themselves. With the annihilation of the Truth—conceived as a world of this side—it is simultaneously not posited as a distant beyond. This is standpoint of ancient skepticism, for which the beyond does not exist at all.

Any inkling of philosophy, however, vanishes when romantic feeling courses through it. The Truth, which man does not encounter in this world, is generated anew in a distant, otherworldly realm. Therefore, the Truth exists in itself, but does not exist *for* the individual. The individual is divided between two worlds: one true, supersensible world that exists in itself, and one untrue, sensible world, this earthly vale of tears. In the place of the repose and certainty that resignation granted the ancient skeptic, the modern skeptic mercilessly yearning for that distant beyond and for knowledge thereof. The subject’s predicament is that of the doubter in Tiedge’s *Urania*. Faith expects the solution to those riddles, a solution impossible here, to come from a better, imaginary state of existence. Thus, philosophy destroys itself a first time only to lose itself a second time to revelation, as witnessed in Schelling’s "Philosophy of Revelation." If such modesty, a modesty of ignorance that hides behind religion, reproaches philosophy as an act of presumption, then we have also seen how these pious souls, with their parted hair and humbly bowed heads, are characterized by the greatest arrogance and utmost presumption.

The firmer, the more widespread, and the more deeply rooted this view is, the more zealously we must oppose it, specifically by refuting its five main arguments in reverse order:

1. Truth cannot be known, only believed
2. If we cannot know truth, then we must love it 
3. We must love the truth so that we may strive for it
4. In striving for truth, we recognize only the finite
5. We recognize the finite because we ourselves are finite and limited.

"A known God," the Jacobian philosophy of faith asserts as its first point, "would cease to be God, as much as He would be reduced to something finite; for to know means to derive the finite from the finite." According to the philosophy of faith, a philosophy that seeks to know God is godless, elevating Nature—and itself—to the status of God. Thus, it would appear to be in the very interest of science that no God should exist. All of these accusations can be turned back upon their accusers. To the charge of atheism, Spinoza gave an excellent reply: *Pro dolor! res eo jam pervenit, ut qui Dei nullam ideam habere fateantur, non erubescant, philosophos atheismi accusare.* (Those who wish to know nothing of the Deity—not those who champion the knowability of God—are the true atheists). Whoever denies the knowability of God, according to their own logic, cannot speak of divine matters. But to speak of such matters is precisely the most essential task of philosophy. There can be no philosophy without God, seeing as He is its sole object. Since philosophy generates all things out of itself, whatever it cannot generate from within itself, whatever it cannot attest to, does not exist for it, and the God whom it cannot know, it is bound to deny. Thus, if it is the philosophers of faith, not the philosophers of thought, who deny God, they are nonetheless are better than they appear. Even if they cannot pass off faith as a form of *cognition*, they still call it *knowledge*. They wish to *know* God in a firsthand, immediate manner. However, such knowledge is not knowledge of the *what* God is, but merely knowledge *that* God is (*quod, non quid*). Immediate knowledge, or knowing *that*, amounts to knowing nothing at all. For if I know only *that* God is, but not *what* He is, then He could be an ape, an ibis, a cat, or whatever else. What actually matters is the *content*, the specific manner in which the nature of God is to be defined. For it is the *what* that first defines God as the true God, and to discover this is precisely the task of philosophy. That this is actually the case is something we have yet to prove and can only presuppose at this point, but we can still counter Jacobi’s undisguised faith in his own ignorance with philosophy’s faith in the knowability of God.

Secondly, if such credulous skepticism seeks to hide behind etymology and the venerable authority of Pythagoras by claiming that it has not yet *devoured* wisdom, but merely *loves* it, then we must respond with the same weapons of etymology. After all, the word devour (φιλόσοφος) is formed in the same way as love (φίλοινος). Will a lover of wine stand idly before a full bottle, or will he not rather lift it to his lips and drain it to the very dregs? “Desire and love for the thing makes all labor seem slight,” as the proverb goes. Whoever loves wisdom will also drink its cup to the very last drop.

Thirdly, as concerns that sluggish, inactive love that manifests as a longing, futile striving toward a goal that can never be attained, such a state would constitute the Tantalic torment of reaching for the fruit of knowledge only to have it snap back at the very instant. It would be to desire to drink the water of life, only to have it vanish immediately, like filling a leaking barrel with water. But to conceive of this love and striving as a gradual approach toward the goal (as Schleiermacher does) is of no greater help. For if I merely approach the Infinite, approaching it *ad infinitum*, without ever actually reaching it, then I still remain an entire infinity away from it. To borrow Haller’s metaphor: I subtract the distance I have already traversed, and the infinity that still remains to be traversed lies entirely before me. Regarding that great object with which one is content to settle, even while maintaining a feeble attitude toward it, Hamlet, on the contrary, rightly demands an active, resolute stance even in the most trivial matter, and how much more so in the case of the most worthy object:

>Truly to be great

>Is not to stir without a mighty cause

>But rather, to fight a straw with mighty zeal

>When honor is at stake.

But whoever grasps for straw instead of the grain, whoever contents themselves, in the manner of Kant and Jacobi, with appearances or the conditioned instead of with the *in-itself* and the unconditioned, then such a philosophy that asserts that it cannot recognize the truth refutes itself. It commits the contradiction of simultaneously affirming and denying the possibility of knowledge. If the outcome of its inquiries is not the Truth, but rather falsehood—the temporal, vanishing world—then it is evident that not the slightest value can be attached to such a result, and it is not worth the effort to strive for illusions instead of knowledge. But this view is not only useless, it is also pernicious. For by deeming the object of such knowledge worthy of being known, it elevates the finite—mere sense certainty—to the status of Truth, and this constitutes an atheism of the worst kind, one into which even modern skepticism has fallen, degenerating into a dogmatic empiricism of the worst kind.

Furthermore, under the premise that we are capable of knowing only the untrue, human reason—the natural light of knowledge—would be nothing more than a *will-o'-the-wisp* that drags us down into the quagmire of falsehood. If truth—as the saying goes—is a light, then its very nature consists in illuminating both itself and other things. It does the light no harm if other things are kindled by it, it does not become any poorer thereby. In Athens, there was even a penalty for anyone who refused to allow another person to light their own lamp from his. In the same way, God does not become poorer when He shares from the fullness of His truth and reveals Himself. Truth is therefore absolutely communicable in the same way that light is. If God wished to keep the truth solely for Himself, He would be envious. According to Aristotle and Plato, however, envy is banished from the ranks of the heavenly beings, for a being can only be envious if they lack something. God thereby ceases to be the hidden, secluded, and unknown One. When Paul was in Athens, according to the account in the Acts of the Apostles, and was brought before the Areopagus on account of his new teachings, he said: "Men of Athens, I see that you are very religious. On my way here, I noticed an altar bearing the inscription: 'To the Unknown God.' Him whom you worship in this manner, without knowing Him—Him will I reveal to you." In short, by the very fact that God became man, the depths of the Godhead have been unveiled. By descending to man, He has drawn man up toward Himself. We can apply Schiller’s words regarding the Gods of Greece, with some modification, to God:

>As the Deity became more human

>Humanity became more divine

Whoever believes in or recognizes the unity of the divine and human can therefore—fifthly—no longer speak of the limits of the human cognitive faculty. They know that God is the innermost essence of the human spirit and that He brings Himself to consciousness within it. What, after all, does this opposition of the finite and the infinite, of the limited and the unlimited, actually signify? For anyone who asserts that the limits of the human cognitive faculty prevent man from recognizing the Truth fails to realize that, by imposing this limit upon man, they simultaneously impose it upon the eternal Truth itself. If man’s limit is so insurmountable that Truth cannot reach him, if it is unable to soften this hard shell and husk of finitude, then Truth remains something limited and finite to him. Logically speaking, limitation necessarily implies two entities. A boundary is possible only between two adjacent fields, where the one ends precisely where the other begins. However, Truth encounters no limit in the finite, it permeates and dissolves finitude. In communicating itself, it loses nothing of its own fullness, for it rediscovers itself within the very entity to which it communicates itself. But this also implies that this communication is conditioned by the receptive capacity of the finite entity. Sensuous nature is that finite entity which, being merely bounded, does not perceive its own limitation, and thus feels perfectly at ease within its finitude. The reason for this restless striving, however, is that man is conscious of this boundary and seeks to transcend it, but in so doing so, he thereby transforms it into a *limitation*, for a limitation is a boundary that is meant to be transcended.

Here one might ask why the boundary exists at all if it is ultimately to be transcended? It is precisely the central point of philosophy that the immediate is insufficient, that struggle and incoherence must precede it so that perfected harmony may result from it. God’s boundlessness is established only through His overcoming of man’s limitation, not through the absence of that limitation. On the contrary, the limitation is necessary, for it is only only by knowing the limitation that man comes to feel the need for infinity. "There is more joy in heaven," says Scripture, "over one sinner who repents than over ten righteous men!" The limitation of man is not a fixed barrier in his relationship to the Truth, but by uniting himself with the Truth, he transcends that limitation. This act of man, this transcending of his own limitation, is itself the divine activity within him, what theologians call *grace*. However, just as grace is effective only through human action, so too is it the act of man, through philosophy, that resolves the dichotomy between the finite and the infinite. Therefore, we must not, as Aristotle argues in opposition to the proverb, to strive solely for the finite and the human simply because we are human and finite beings, rather, we must attain immortality for ourselves. Precisely by recognizing the finite, we must simultaneously grasp the infinite within and alongside it. Only then does this striving attain its goal, and the love of the finite for the infinite is, as Spinoza puts it, the "intellectual love of God" through which God loves Himself within man. Yet if man, by uniting himself as one with his object, thereby sublates all those mediations through which he passed during the period of dichotomy, then his act of knowing becomes simultaneously that immediate knowledge which Jacobi termed "faith." Therfore, when rightly understood, these five objections are resolved within this true perspective, yet are simultaneously still discernible therein.
