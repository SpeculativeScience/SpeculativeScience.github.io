# Free Will

> This webpage is a direct translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Anthropology and Psychology, Vol. III* (1840) 

In order for formal self-determination to achieve the universal purpose of happiness through the infinite particularity of its drives, it must become the content of the will not beyond this particular content, but in the midst of this heteronomy. Since the will itself is the infinite form of universality, it remains, whatever its particular content may otherwise be, a self-determining universality. Therein lies the concept of the will's freedom, it has only itself as the content of its self-determination. The free will becomes an object to itself, and the existence of this object is right. Thus, we have to present, firstly, the pure concept of right, secondly, the existence of right, and thirdly, the unity of the pure concept and existence of right as the realized concept of law.

## The Concept of Right

### Rational Will

Since the will is no longer fragmented into the manifold content of its drives, but has elevated each of them to the form of universality, its purpose is achieved in every individual expression of freedom. The infinite series of individual satisfactions, which never terminates beyond an endless progression, does not require the will to be infinite because each limitation of the will is determined by itself. The will that knows itself in the content of what it wills is thus the rational will. Pure reason is what privails in the will, all other drives are illuminated and transformed by the drive for knowledge, and it becomes clear that only as practical reason is the will actually free. Thus, the intellect reappears into the will for the last time, and both have now become absolutely one, so that only now do we truly understand Spinoza's proposition that the intellect and the will are one and the same thing. The will contemplates its freedom in the satisfaction of every drive. It therefore desires the drive not in its natural immediacy, but as a universal determination of reason, that is, not merely for their own satisfaction, but for the satisfaction of all. In this way, it was Kant who properly conceived the concept of right. The drive for love becomes marriage, the drive for possession becomes property, and so on. Drives become moral relationships in which the freedom of each individual is recognized. Since the satisfaction of the drive takes place only in each individual, it itself becomes universal and rational. Thus, pure reason is the true origin of right and morality.

### Objective Will

The will that relates to itself as object because it knows itself in it can be called the objective will, since the free will has become an object to itself. This is what it means for the free will to be objective, that it makes itself the goal of its actions. But this objectivity of freedom does not only refer to the natural drives since the rational will refers to itself in its drives, it must be completely at peace with itself in the other, and this other must therefore also be a rational will. My freedom is only objective to me when I recognize my freedom in an other, i.e. in another free will like myself. The expression "objective will" can then also refer to the formal sense in which freedom exists as an action, as in the case of imputation. Finally, the will can be called objective in the historical and pedagogical sense, as in a person who only recognizes the freedom of others, but does not yet recognize their own freedom. The context must subsequently determine in which sense we take "objective" to mean. Here, however, we contrast the objective will with the subjective will only to the extent that the latter purely thinks the content its drives as purposes that belong entirely to themselves. However, the expression "subjective" also stands in contrast to the two other meanings of "objective," so that the subjective will is partly an unrealized purpose and partly a pure form of self-consciousness in which the content of the will is exclusive to itself. This form is by no means lacking in the objective will, since it is also a thinking will.

### Autonomous Will

Because the will, regardless of its practical activity, does not cease to theoretically contemplate itself as an object, it is the form of freedom in which the will wills itself. We can call this, following Fichte, the original drive of the spirit. Here, heteronomy is completely dissolved into autonomy. For while the spirit is only partially present in each individual drive, recognizing itself only partially and inadequately, it now recognizes its own essence as the object by which it is driven, is driven in this way only by itself, and is therefore free as self-determining. That the will wills itself means that its freedom, the systemization of rational drives, is its object, and thus the objective and subjective are identical in it. When the will acts, it transposes its freedom from the form of subjectivity into the form of objectivity. But since this freedom already has its origin in the object that is the rational drives, the will also comprehends its actions theoretically. The free will as a subject wills the free will as an object, and this purpose of the free will is right.

## The Existence of Right

The freedom that wills freedom contains, firstly, as its subject, the free will. The object of this free will, secondly, is another free will, in that one is equal to the other. From this equality lies, thirdly, the awareness of their oneness, their fraternity. Liberty, equality, and fraternity, of which Rousseau, Aristotle, and socialism each established one aspect as the complete definition of right, are thus the three inseparable and necessarily interdependent aspects of the complete manifestation of the concept of right.

### Liberty

By willing itself as an objective will, freedom comes into being and perceives itself as the existence of liberty. Freedom that wills its own existence is liberty. Here, it is no longer about satisfying this or that drive, but the pure existence of freedom. The owner rejoices in the existence of their liberty even if they are not currently using what they own to satisfy their drives. Hence we are not dealing with the individuality of the will, as the manifold of drives and inclinations that exist, but with the universality the will, which exists in the individual and not beyond them. Since Rousseau exclusively identified right with liberty, he began with the individual will, for liberty is the individual's being-for-itself. Had he stopped here, his doctrine could rightly be accused of making arbitrariness the basis of right. However, because he defined right as the liberty that remains constant in all individual wills, he also comprehended the universality of the will and made the rational will its foundation. Everyone in Rousseau's state desires liberty in general, and that means liberty for all. If liberty in general, that is, the encounter of the free will with another free will, is recognized in this way, then liberty cannot be defined as the capacity to do and refrain from doing whatever one wants, i.e. arbitrary. What is arbitrary must be restricted so that liberty can actually exist. Kant's definition of right as a restriction of liberty therefore only applies to arbitrariness. It is only when arbitrariness of each is restricted that liberty exists for all. Arbitrariness is precisely the slavery of man, and liberty arises only from its restriction.

### Equality

In saying that liberty is the freedom of this free will, the question arises as to how the various free wills relate to one another? Liberty in general is to exist as a free person, so it seems irrelevant as to which person is free. But if one is free and another is unfree, then the free person and the unfree person are unrecognizable to each other. If everyone is to be free, then everyone must be equal to everyone else. However, equality as the mere demand for sameness and homogeneity turns into its opposite. For if liberty demands individuality, which presupposes that individuals are distinct from each other as individuals, then their equality would make every difference in content disappear as if everyone was one and the same person. This abstract equality is impossible by the mere fact that no two people are the same, and much less is it just, since people can only be seen this way from the perspective of a higher authority that is beyond the standard it prescribes. Equality and liberty must therefore be connected.

Equality is, firstly, the possibility for everyone to freely use and apply the powers bestowed upon them by nature to realize and expand their freedom. This capacity finds its limit only in the freedom expressed by others. In other words, this is the equal entitlement of anyone to anything. Equal entitlement is not an entitlement to homogeneity because it allows people to be different from each other. That nature distributes its gifts unequally is not an injustice of nature, for nature does not have knowledge of justice or injustice, let alone knowledge of itself. Moral inequality as a difference in happiness or unhappiness must be brought back to equality through right and ethicality, as Rousseau beautifully put it. Whether people are more or less alike are merely possibilities, that is, contingent upon external conditions, whereas equality of entitlement is their necessity. 

Everyone must have the same opportunity to acquire as much as they are able. This equal right is the first condition of popular sovereignty, whereas hereditary rule violates this right and favors certain people over others simply by virtue of their birth. To make ancestry the basis of right is an absolute injustice. Voltaire therefore says very well: "Men are equal, it is not birth, but virtue that makes the difference." All privileges of birth must be abolished without exception, and this is the precise meaning of this fundamental right. Enough advantages remain for nature bestow upon one person over another certain talents that precludes the existence of privileges on the basis of class. Through the qualities of their character, a person is already at an advantage over another. The privileges of talent and skill cannot be eradicated. For if no one was recognized by the talents and skills that make them distinct as individuals, this would be an injustice that creates the conditions for the abstract conformity of populism or ochlocracy. 

Since true equality, which is demanded as a fundamental right, does not exclude inequality, but the existence inequality is unjust as such, it must be reduced to equality, and this is precisely what distributive justice does. If everyone receives as much as they contribute, then no one can complain. One person can accomplish this kind of work by virtue of their unique qualities, just as another person can accomplish another kind of work for the same reason. Non omnia possumus omnes (Not all of us can do everything), as Virgil already said. That a person is unique in one way and not another is not a valid complaint, for otherwise they would have to give up their very being. Everyone will therefore be satisfied and consider their freedom preserved when they receive the full product of their labor. They will then find their inner being, their abilities and talents, recognized and rewarded in the external world. Their freedom has thus become tangible, and they enjoys their right to it. If something undeserved happens to them, they suffer injustice. Reward and punishment according to merit is therefore the second of humanity's fundamental rights.

But if, in this way, the external existence of freedom varies in scope, equality must nevertheless be restored by ensuring that the freedom of eachc is recognized equally in its respective scope. Everyone must be equal before the law and receive the same protections from it, and this is the third fundamental right. The nobility of merit, which does not preclude popular equality, must not become an inequality before the law so that someone with greater merit has more rights than another. That would mean replacing an oligarchy of birth with an oligarchy of money, as is the case in voting during the Roman centuries and in any election that is decided on basis of funding and not votes.

### Fraternity

Up to this point, we have understood liberty and equality primarily from the perspective of the individual. Liberty was indeed the universal purpose, but only as it manifested itself in the limitation of the individual's drives. Even equality, though it presupposes a relationship between individuals, refers back to the individual as such, as well as to their merit, which differs from others. However, since freedom is the universal essence of individuals, the spirit that comes to being in all, individuals must also become consciousness of this freedom through their interaction with each other. They must know they are kindred spirits, an I that is We, members of a common body, in short, that they are brothers. This insight is the pinnacle of the concept of right in social life. Right is the universal freedom that gives itself existence, it is the general will, the ethical union of many wills united as one will. For if everyone recognizes themselves in the other, and everyone is an end in themselves to the other, then everyone pursues a common end. The first fundamental right to be mentioned here is the right of assembly, which recognizes that the spirit of all is one spirit. The spark expressed by the spirit of one person ignites all other spirits, and this becomes the bright flame of enthusiasm for the assembly as a whole. They make decisions in common and set a common goal for themselves. The will of many becomes one will without ceasing to be the will of the many. That the union of spirits maintains a lasting existence is guaranteed by the second of the fundamental rights belonging here, the right of association. Everyone contributes according to their ability to carry out the common purpose established by the assembly, which is achieved more easily and effectively than if they had each acted individually. The pooling and combination of resources doubles, even tenfolds, their effectiveness. Even small contributions, when taken together, can make a big difference. "Concordia res parvæ crescunt" (Small things flourish by concord) as the Latin proverb says. In common work, everyone recognizes their freedom as an inseparable part of the whole. The highest expression of fraternity is, finally, the mutual guarantee of all for all. Because all people know themselves as brothers, as members of one universal being, they make common arrangements so that everyone receives according to their contribution, that they are able to exercise their abilities unhindered, and that the adversities of nature and fortune are compensated for through mutual support and insurance. This reciprocity completes the concept of freedom in equality. The freedom of each is the freedom of all, and the freedom of all is the freedom of each. Having fully brought the concept of freedom into existence, what remains is to examine more closely the existence of freedom accords with its concept, i.e. the realization of the concept of law.

## Law

Since the free will, which wills the existence of freedom, is the unity of the subjective and the objective, we can call the subjective side the person and the objective side the thing. The connection of person and thing, of concept and existence, is the first idea of ​​law, and whose last moment, ownership, is the beginning of the philosophy of law or practical philosophy.

### Person

The unconditional equality of all individuals, which we last saw in fraternity, comes from the fact that each person is identified by universal thought of personhood. A person is a will that wills freedom and is also recognized by all as this free will. Because it is recognized as free by all other persons, it is the subject of a right. This legal capacity is the universal characteristic of a person. The first principle of law is therefore to be a person and treat all others as persons. Personhood is thus the highest legal principle there is, because it is the characteristic of freedom that is the same in all. At the same time, however, personhood is also the lowest and most abstract legal principle because the expression only denotes legal capacity, which is necessarily abstract since personality cannot be denied to any human being. Every human being has legal capacity because every human being has reason. To the extent that a person is unable use their reason to exercise their legal capacity themselves, they can still exercise it through another person who represents them.

#### The Illegality of Slavery

Since freedom is the universal characteristic of every individual, as the stamp of humanity within each, the first consequence of the concept of legal capacity is the illegality of slavery. Every human being is free as a human being. The distinction between a freeborn person (ingenuus), one born into slavery, or freed from slavery (libertus, libertinus), as Roman law establishes it, must therefore cease to exist. The equality of human beings describes prior must preclude the immense privilege of a free birth over an unfree one. Historically, however, the universality of this freedom was not recognized. In the Asiatic empires, everyone was a slave except for the ruler, and since the ruler did not recognize freedom as the free will of another, neither could they realize freedom or justice for themselves. By taking the place of justice, the tyrant becomes a slave to themselves in that they are servile to selfish and arbitrary whims that can never be satisfied, while the slave, in fear of master, practices the renunciation of selfishness and thus takes the first step toward freedom. 

In Greece and Rome, the citizens are born free, whereas the barbarians are born slaves. Legal capacity was therefore not the general essence of every person in Rome, but was tied to the particular and contingent condition of birth, and the status of servitude stood in opposition to the status of liberty. Antiquity went so far as to not even consider not even privilege of birth sufficient for legal capacity, but one also had to be a citizen of the same free city. This is the basis of the Roman concept of status civitatis, in relation to which the stranger, the peregrinus, was without rights. Even between the estates of one and the same people against each other were initially not legally capable in Rome, possessing only commercium, not suffragium and connubium in relation to one another. In Rome, legal capacity was limited to an elite estate in Rome, it was the exception instead of the general rule.

Not only did the existing legislation of antiquity protect slavery, but even its philosophers defended it, such as Aristotle. But every philosophy is only its time elevated to thought. Aristotle says that people who cannot obey reason, but can only follow their impulses, must obey someone else who has reason, just as the drives are subject to reason. But this presupposes the existence of people who lack reason and are eternally condemned obeying whatever their impulses tell them to do, which is simply not the case. Aristotle begins by contrasting barbarians and Hellenes, and asserts that the barbarians can never free themselves from being ruled by their impulses, whereas reason has come to power for the Hellenes. However, no one can be denied the capacity for education. Rationality is the universal inheritance of all human beings, which means they are all capable of reason and have legal capacity by extension. 

With Christianity, the true concept of legal capacity has thus become a historical fact. Only in Christianity is man no longer considered merely a citizen of a state, but a human being. In Christianity, the individual, as an individual, and therefore every single person, has infinite value, is the object of the divine purpose, and thus has an unconditional existence destined for eternal blessedness. Therefore, Aristotle's proposition that those born with inferior natures are merely animated tools, as he calls slaves, means to the ends of others, is an impossibility. Every individual is an end in themselves, and without this universal equality, freedom and right are impossible. All educated peoples of the world, therefore strive to eradicate slavery off the face of the earth.

#### The Freedom of Labor

The first fundamental right of the person was, in its general scope, to not be made a means to another. Just as the work I prescribe to a tool or a machine is entirely within my power, so too is the work of the slave prescribed by the master. The full extent of their lived existence, the uninterrupted series of their actions, is determined by another, and so nothing remains of the person's freedom. However, slavery need not take the form a direct relationship of person to person, but can be mediated through land ownership. This is the relationship of serfdom, a milder form of slavery, which persisted well into Christianity because serfdom did not abolish personality as directly as slavery did. Montesquieu seems to have had this in mind when he justifies slavery in the case where the weaker party places themselves under the protection of a powerful person, i.e. the client-vassal relationship. The serf was the steward of a piece of land that was part of a larger estate. With the acquisition of this estate, the owner also acquired the right to use its inhabitants for certain services called corvée labor. These services did not constitute the entire scope of the serf's labor power, nor the similar forms under which this more material form of slavery appeared. The serf was also allowed to cultivate the fields on which they worked, but their was subject to glebæ adscriptus and could not leave the land without the lord's permission. Personal freedom and slavery were intertwined because the serf was dependent only in part of their labor power. In all cases, the freedom of the individual was not fully recognized. This includes, above all, the Indian caste system. A born Brahmin must be a priest, and whether one was a warrior, farmer, or craftsman was determined by birth. This can also be found throughout the Middle Ages in Europe. The nobility were warriors, the townspeople were tradesmen, the peasants were farmers passed from father to son, and the priesthood was supplemented from the other estates. Finally, the guild system of the Middle Ages restricted only a certain number of people to practice a certain trade. But just as no one may be excluded from a branch of labor, so too may they not be forced into a specific vocation, even if only for a few years. The choice of vocation must be entirely free. Compulsory military service certainly does not correspond to the complete freedom of labor. When we see the effects of military rule, it becomes clear to us how the concept of personal freedom is severely compromised by military conscription, and neither can the state withstand it. 

With the complete freedom of the worker, i.e. their right not to be forced into any particular work, we now see his right to work deduced from it. However, the state only provides work as a last resort, when the work provided by families, associations or communities are insufficient. However, this right cannot be extended to a specific job, because otherwise it would contradict the aforementioned freedom of work. For if no one has the right to demand a specific job from me, then I can no more demand that a specific job be assigned to me. Indeed,  right of only to some, this would deprive the freedom of work for others. Everyone must be able to pursue their choice of activity without restriction as long as they do not infringe upon the freedom of another. The type of work must be freely chosen, though the choice is limited by the available work. If there is no more room to perform this activity in this place, the worker performs it elsewhere, or they take up another type of work. As long as there is work available, the right to work exists undiminished. However, this is can only be the right to seek work in general, by no means a right to a particular type of work. This is the basis of the right to emigrate, to go wherever I hope to find work. 

#### The Right to Exist

The right to work necessarily implies the right to exist. For I am only the sum of my activities, and through my work I sustain myself as a living being. Work must therefore provide me with the sufficient resources that I need to live. I thus have the right to go anywhere to find work that is best suited for me and securely earn my living in the best way possible. Thus, the right to relocate and freedom of movement are fundamental, that is, the right to change one's residence and to remain undisturbed wherever one wishes. This right to exist, summarized in its highest point, is the right to live. Personality not as the free expression of activity in the full breadth of its scope, and not partially, as in labor, but as the undivided existence of itself at this point in the present. When a French writer, for whom he was imprisoned in the Bastille, excused his satirical poems against Richelieu by saying, "Monseigneur, il me faut vivre," (My lord, I must live), the Cardinal replied, "Je n'en vois pas la nécessité." (I don't see the necessity). This is a cruel answer that violates freedom to the highest degree. This right to exist is the foundation of all rights, the return of all forms of legal capacity to their original essence, and is the most general expression of the right to be a person. Since this universal right must be defended, it is even permissible to violate the particular right of another in order to defend it, and such a collision of rights is what jurisprudence calls the right of necessity. If someone is in danger of starving, they have the right to steal food to save their life, i.e. theft in the case of emergency. But this does not mean that theft is justified in preparation for a possible or future emergency since the life of the person is not currently in danger, only the present existence of an actual emergency justifies such an act. If my life were endangered by another, it would be well within my right to resist them by whatever means possible in order to defend myself. And by threatening the life of another, the attacker forfeits their own right to live. Furthermore, the beneficium competentiæ follow from the right of necessity: that a debtor is allowed to keep enough of their assets, the creditors' property, to provide for themselves in a manner befitting their station. The moment of life is extended again to the duration of life. Finally, from the right to existence follows the right to sustenance when I can no longer work, mutual insurance mediated by free agreement, as in pension funds for retirement and the like.

### Things

Having declared the person sacred and inviolable both in its simple being and in the manifold aspects of its own existence, the question now arises as to how it behaves in its external existence. We saw that the person was only a possibility, a capacity, and so was, apart from its content, the emptiest. We can now call the external content of person the thing. A thing is always only an object, never a subject of rights, just as a person can never be an object. The legal subject is, in the first case, the current owner of the property, and in the second, its future inheritor whose right is calculated retroactively to the testator's death. If the person is simultaneously the highest and the lowest concept of right, then this also applies to the thing. The thing is the highest when it provides the content of the person's freedom, for it is the real existence of freedom. But since as only the person and their freedom are valid in the thing, the thing is also the lowest in that it is subservient to the person. The thing is the objectification of freedom, nature that has been given the mark of spirit by spirit itself. Since the person is free, i.e. objective, only in the thing, it must be added to the idea of ​​the person as the other, necessary side for its realization. The question now arises as to what the true division of things are. In this respect, it is only a matter of knowing how each thing, as object and representative of the right, relates to the subject of the right.

#### The Body

The immediate transition of person to thing is the living body of the person. The individual, sensuous person has a natural existence in itself and through which it relates to the external world as a connecting intermediary. In the body, the person is immediately and necessarily present. The human being's natural body is so immediately the representative of its person that this externality is precisely designated by the expression personality, as when one says of someone that one knows them personally. Because the person is absolutely present in their body, the person's body must also be recognized by everyone as the real existence of the person. Similarly, because of this immediate unity of person and thing, the body is not at all separated from the subject of law, just as in self-consciousness the knowledge of the self as a subject is equal to knowledge of the self as an object. Violence done to my body is violence that is done to me. The fundamental right we are to establish here is personal liberty, which no one may be deprived of. Because every person possesses a body, every person is a bearer of innate, inalienable rights. In short, right to personal liberty is a fundamental right. This existence of freedom is the most important one because, like life, it is the necessary condition of all other rights. 

#### Products

We can now call the object of freedom, distinct from the person as such, a thing in the proper sense, which is either a product of nature, a product of the mind, or a combination of the two.

The purely external product is anything that exists in the natural environment of man in which his will can be placed. A natural product is thus not the immediate existence of his freedom, but is only made so by a particular act of will on the part of the person, an extra step that was neither necessary nor present in the case of the body. The immovable thing appears as the principal and general thing, in and within which movable things are located. Man inhabits the immovable thing as if in a continuation of his body. From this follows the right of the inviolability of one's residence, the right to housing, according to which I can expel anyone from my dwelling and prohibit any searches without a court order. With regard to movable property, it goes without saying that I must have it if my person is to be recognized in it. Any movable thing can again attain the form of generality and be the subject of law. This is the fungible thing, in contrast to the non-fungible one, e.g. a bushel of grain. The fungible thing remains limited to particularity because it is only useufl for a particular purpose. This character of particularity disappears in money, which although fungible, it nevertheless possesses the character of general utility because it functions as a general equivalent. 

On the other hand, if we consider the general possibility of products existing in and from which they can arise, like fruits from a field, as the inner world of the person, then we have mental abilities and skills, arts, sciences, etc. These are also objective modes in which freedom exists, and they are always in the person's possession. Products of the mind are external to the person only to the extent that they are distinct from the person themselves, and in that they are taught to the person from the outside through instruction. These are subject matters into which a person can place their freedom, just as they can place their freedom into physical things. However, the existence of a person's freedom in a physical thing naturally excludes the existence of another person from it, whereas in subject matters, one person's immersion in a subject matter promotes the existence of freedom of all others within it. For instance, the more people have acquired skill in an art, the easier it is for others to learn the different skills they have acquired from each other. The subject matter thus becomes, through appropriation, an acquired skill of the individual and it is only through such appropriation does the person make something of themselves. The subject matter also has the advantage of being all the more irreplaceable than a physical thing. However, no skill has an objective existence until it has manifested itself in a creative work.

Any work of human activity, whether it be a work of art, a craft, a work of science, etc. is therefore the third and final kind of product, the unity of the natural and the mental. In it, the opposition between person and thing has already been dissolved. In the work, the entire scope of it is the person itself, the individuality of the person is perfected through it. By being able to treat each of my works as belonging to me, my person is indeed immediately present in this thing, yet it is no longer something immediate for me, but something that only becomes a thing because I placed my person in it. 

#### Partnerships

If one or more persons do not merely produce external things, but rather unite their common powers in an association to carry out the lasting purpose of freedom, then this spiritual bond that unites the persons is a partnership. As the union and association of several living persons, the partnership initially appears as an entity or thing that is the common and objective existence of each. However, the persons are also free in their mentality, they live for one another through voluntary acts of service, and their shared mentality is an expression of the freedom of all. This is the legal matter elevated to the standpoint of fraternity, where the free association of persons constitutes the existence of freedom by itself. Such embodiments of partnership also appear as agreements between families, businesses, communities, nations, etc. But now that the person and thing are naturally as well as spiritually linked forms the concept of possession, the third aspect of the idea of right.

### Possession

Only in the connection of the two aspects of right previously considered, the person and the thing, is right as such realized. Law can have no other beginning than possession, and the drive for possession, the drive for the means to satisfy my desire, becomes a legal matter the moment it becomes apparent that it is not the individual's desire, but their free personality that makes itself objective in the thing. Considered in this way, possession is not limited to the physical occupation a thing by a person, but extends to any thing that is permeated by the will, for which Roman legal scholars rightly formulated the principle: Adipiscimur possessionem corpore et animo (We acquire possession by body and mind).

#### Possession of the Body

The object that is in the immediate possession of my person is my body since it is precisely the thing with which my person, as one and the same, naturally coincides. It is therefore the natural unity, established by birth itself, of the two aforementioned aspects of right. Because others must recognize in the body of another the objective unity of their inner and outer person, of possessor and possessed, their body is necessarily for them the most immediate manifestation of their freedom. However, it only appears as the manifestation of freedom to the extent that the body is used to take action. Faust's words: "What you have inherited from your fathers, use it to possess it," although generally applicable to all possessions, it especially applies to the possession of the body. If I was not in possession of my body, it would appear as something alien to me. Hence the question posed by Schiller's distich already contains the answer within itself: "For years now I have used my nose to smell, But do I have a demonstrable right to it?" The title of possession lies precisely in use, whereas with alienable things, use is not enough, for it is possible for it to be in the possession of another, which is never the case with my own body.

#### Possession of Products

If, in the possession of my body, the physical aspect of possession is self-evident and sufficient for others, but the will (animus) to possess it is also required for the possessor, then both requirements must be investigated for the acquisition of products. One does not call someone a possessor who is only in physical contact with the thing, that is, who possesses only in body and not mind. In this case, the person is merely the holder of a thing (detentor). For instance, in a deposit agreement (depositum), the depositor does not desire the existence of their freedom in the thing, but the existence of the freedom of another, the depositary. Furthermore, whoever possesses a thing merely to satisfy their desires by means of it is a possessor only in body but not mind. The satisfaction of desire is the interest of their relationship to the thing. In this sense of a merely physical detention, the thief also possesses, he takes possession of something by cunning or force in order to satisfy his needs through it. In this respect, possession is not a right, but a fact. Animals are living proof of this, since they exercise the same activities of physical possession as persons do. The wolf eagerly subjects the lamb to physical force, kills the lamb and eats it. If animals had the right to kill other animals, then this presumes that some animals are persons whereas other animals are not, which refutes the original assumption that animals are persons. The dilemma of the thief is, in this respect, the same dilemma as the wolf. Since the intention of the thief is limited to theft, they cannot assert their possession as a universally recognized existence, for if they could, their act of theft would be discovered.

Since the idea that a right arises only after the fact through injustice is entirely untenable, the matter must be decided in the following way for philosophical reasons. It is true that for animals, possession is a fact and not a right, but for every human being, possession is certainly a right. The reason for this is that physical possession is always connected with the striving to secure an existence for one's own person in the thing itself. Only in children and the insane, to the extent that they can assert no other intention than to satisfy their own desires, is possession limited to being a mere fact. With the presence of reason, however, possession becomes a right. The thief would have a right to possession if they did not violate another's right to possession. However, if the object has been recovered by the thief, and the judge does not have justifiable suspicion to seize it provisionally, the thief can still claim an interdictum possessorium until the dispute is resolved. Even a thief is a possessor in body and mind since the act of stealing and the intent to steal are present, even if they are asserted in secret. This relationship arises with every dishonest possessor (malæ fidei possessor), whether they came into possession through fraud or by other malicious means.

The honest possessor (bonæ fidei possessor), who believes themselves to be the rightful owner, seeks to realize the existence of their freedom in the possession. However, this will must be recognized by all possessors. It is the animus rem sibi habendi (intent to keep the thing) that is always present, whether it serves the satisfaction of desire or not. This is what makes possession a right, and so the depositor, by virtue of their intent to keep the thing, is permitted to retain possession of the property entrusted to them. In every individual case of possession, the question is only how strong the claim to ownership is. Every possessor, whatever the origin of the possession may be, is in the right against every non-possessor and has the right to not be disturbed in their possession. And while the dishonest possessor must yield to the honest posessor, any possessor, whether honest or dishonest, must yield to the rightful owner. However, it goes without saying that the non-possessing owner must relinquish possession to the possessing non-owner until their claim to ownership is proven. Possession is thus presumed, incipient ownership, which becomes full ownership when it is universally recognized by law.

#### Right of Appropriation

Possession and ownership thus only diverge to the extent that the thing that someone takes possession of is already the existence of another's freedom. It is one who first placed the existence of their freedom into the thing that has the prerogative. As the master of nature, spirit has the unconditional right to place its will into every ownerless thing. This is the right of appropriation that all persons have to all ownerless things. But if another person has already placed the existence of their freedom into a thing, then the thing is no longer a mere thing, but the external existence of the person, which all people must recognize to recognize their own freedom as persons. Hence the legal principle: Res nullius prius occupanti fit (Nothing is occupied first). With the completion of the concept of right, we have exhausted the philosophy of practical psychology and have arrived at property as beginning of the philosophy of law.
