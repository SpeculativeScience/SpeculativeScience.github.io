# Thinking

Note: This webpage is a direct translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Anthropology and Psychology, Vol. II* (1840) 

The concept of thinking can only arise from its relationship to sensibility and imagination, to which it is the third moment of the intellect. While sensibility relates to objects in such a way that it absorbs them without extinguishing its capacity for action in this receptivity, imagination, on the other hand, is the subjective preoccupation of the mind with this material supplied to it by sensibility that it processes. The progress to the concept of thinking now lies in the fact that, like sensibility, it passively relates itself to the objects of cognition, but since their true being is proven to be a product of the mind, on the other hand, like the imagination, the truth that is implicit within object must be actively exposed. Only by making things conform to the mind do I arrive at truth since the subjective and the objective completely coincide with each other. The conclusions of sensibility are transient and momentary, they are only true for today, tomorrow, or a couple of weeks. "That leaf is green" is a truth that disappears too soon and cannot be permanent. Sensibility only explains the appearances, never the essence of things. Every step that is taken in sensory cognition contains only one hall of meaning, the core of which we never penetrate if we limit ourselves to our senses, but always only discover new things until we dare to comprehend the true essence in thought.

This intellectual world determined by the mind is alone what is eternally true, as the sole objective and the core of sensible things. In this determination of things by the spirit of reason, the beginning of true freedom can be recognized, which will then develop even more fully in the practical reason. However, even if the mind changes things by thinking about them, in this activity it does not turn to their sensible material in order to generalize it, as the imagination does, while retaining its sensibility in order to elevate it into the realm of representation. Instead, thinking turns completely away from sensible experience and does not consider it at all, but at the same time, it also does not interfere with it, allowing it to be what it is. Thinking only abstracts the intelligible universal from things, which it reclaims for its own conclusions. These universal entities are, as products of thinking, simultaneously the creative forces of nature and the ideas that are innate to the mind. From this perspective, the Platonic theory of ideas can be understood for what it is: the pure thoughts that pervade all reality.

In thinking, we only have to consider the pure concepts under which representations are classified. This first activity of thought belongs to the understanding. The second activity is that, after these concepts have been discovered, they are now related to one another, either as the subject or predicate of another, by the power of judgment. Whereas the understanding establishes general rules, the power of judgment relates particular cases to them. After the power of judgment has subsumed the particular case under the concept, the concept is now bears within itself the entire content of truth. This free and pure movement and connection of concepts through themselves is, thirdly, the power of reason, which generates the ideas it contemplates through its own activity.

## Understanding

Since the understanding builds the system of its concepts in the higher realm of the intelligible than the realm of sensible nature, the question still remains as to where this knowledge originates. Since truth is the correspondence of the concept and the object, the objective is contained in the subjective and vice versa. Accordingly, a twofold path to knowledge can be identified, one that begins with the object and ascends to the concept, and the other that begins with the concept and descends to the object. The former is knowledge a priori, the latter is knowledge a posteriori. Since the understanding, on the one hand, generates concepts a priori, but on the other hand requires the material of representations to give life to its concepts, both the one and the other source of knowledge are found in it. The categories of the understanding are given a priori in it, but it receives its intuitions a posteriori. The a priori element of the understanding requires the material of representation in order to gain content from it, so universality and necessity come to it only through the categories. Kant expresses this very well by saying that the categories of the understanding are empty without intuitions, and intuitions are blind without the categories. This also gives rise to the conceptual division of the understanding. Since intellectual knowledge does not yet have its origin purely a priori, but requires sensibility and imagination, we must first consider pure categories in isolation. Then, these categories are to be applied to the a posteriori given material of sensibility, whereby this material is elevated into concepts. Finally, the result from the connection of these two aspects is experience, as the system of principles and rules of the understanding, which are acquired through the abstraction of thought.

### Categorization

As far as pure categories of understanding are concerned, the spirit in thinking, as generating the universal, strives for unity in the infinite multiplicity of its representations. The "I think," as the indeterminate activity of the understanding, not only accompanies the entire scope of our representations, but the movement of thinking is also the principle by which this abstract unity is again divided into several classes, which are the many particular modes of the unity of our representations. The categories of understanding are nothing other than the necessary unities of the understanding under which all the material of our knowledge must be subsumed. Kant only presented them, and Fichte first attempted a deduction, which we have to complete here. But since the representations, whose existence as unities we are seeking, are contained in the signs of language, in their adequate connection of representations through thought, there is at the same time a connection of the signs of language according to their conceptual affinity. Since we had to deal more with the material and lexical importance of a philosophical grammar in the case of signification, so too is its logical linguistic element. We therefore think only in language, and distinguish ourselves from animals through both. And it is an infinite merit of Aristotle to have at least vaguely felt this connection, since the table of categories he established, with its ten categories, actually corresponds quite accurately to the grammatical parts of speech. Its basic division, however, is as follows: The ego, as the absolute universal, is the subject of which an infinite number of predicates, the entire range of its states and positions, can be expressed. Because the unity of the ego is the foundation of a circle of sensations and perceptions, these, as we have seen, first become an object. Each object is thus the mirror of the ego, in that it is, as the same, a simple foundation on which various states and activities can be distinguished. Every word, according to Plato, is the primary function of a speech, is therefore either a subject or a predicate, and this is the basis of the opposition between noun and verb. The consideration of these two main classes of speech in isolation forms the etymological division of philosophical grammatical theory.

### Observation

What the understanding still lacks in order to experience is that it must include the material obtained from sensibility. The beginning of this was made in the interjection since sensation itself became a pure content of the understanding. In this way, the understanding no longer deals with abstract categories, but now relates the categories directly to the material of sensibility. In this process, however, the understanding does not so much bring it under the unities of the understanding, but rather objectifies these categories, introduces them into things, and thus encompasses the multiplicity of the material into a network of concepts. This is a matter of observation, whose moments are the following:

When understanding begins to consider things and wishes to incorporate them into its consciousness, it must first apply the category of substantiality to them. It is no longer the main nouns, but the main subjects themselves, the substances of phenomena, that must now be singled out so that, secondly, the qualities and properties of things can be recognized. The first activity of the observing understanding is the discovery and classification of substances. While discovery is more concerned with discovering new genera or individuals in order to then designate them by proper names, classification consists in either grouping individuals of one and the same species into genera, or placing the lower species under higher genera according to their similarities. Everything that exists must be, to a certain extent, reproduced and transformed by the consciousness of spirit. The sciences are being divided ever further and are explored ever more deeply. This restlessness of mankind, to constantly discover new beings in nature and to constantly expand the boundaries of his knowledge. The ancients took no pleasure in observation for its own sake, but rather easily returned to speculation. Since the Reformation, mankind has cast a free eye over the earth and has striven to know its treasures from all sides, to learn about them, and to appropriate them for himself. Those who have not been fortunate enough to discover something completely new, which has only been the lot of a fortunate few, tend to focus more on the individual, penetrating into things, dissecting and analysing them, observing their inner workings. This is the aspect in which the understanding relates to sensibility and imagination, the spirit turns everything into representations, but equally it elevates these representations to concepts by instinctively separating substances according to their genera and species.

The second activity is the justification of these differences between genera. Similar substances are grouped together into one species, while dissimilar ones are separated. The basis of this relationship and distinction lies in their properties and qualities. After the observing understanding has discovered the substances, it proceeds to consider their accidents in order to know how they differ from one another and how their classification can be justified. This is the description of nature and the highlighting of the characteristics of substances. The characteristic is the property that is essential how the category is constituted. Thus, in the characteristic, the adjective has once again become a noun. Since each genus contains several species, substances have, on the one hand, essential characteristics of similarity, by which they belong to one genus, but on the other hand, also distinctive characteristics, by which the particular species of the same genus are again separated from one another. These accidental qualities are not only qualitative, but also quantitative. Animals differ from one another by warm or cold blood, by the color of their blood, by their claws, teeth, etc., but also by their number of limbs, plants by the number of their stamens, etc. This characteristic has a double meaning: first, it characterizes the thing itself, and second, it characterizes the purpose of classification, namely, to distinguish objects with it. Thus, not only do we distinguish animals by their claws and teeth, but they are themselves distinguished from one another by and through them. If the characteristic is viewed only as a component of our cognition, it remains doubtful whether it is even essential for the objective differentiation of substances, as if, for instance, having earlobes is what really separates humans from all other animals.

Because of this ambiguity of the characteristic, and the uncertainty as to which characteristic is essential to substance and which is not, the discovery of the subject has not yet reached its climax through mere observation and description. In attempting to answer the question of which characteristics are essential, cognition is often hindered as long as it remains with the categories of the substantiality, for any accidental characteristic has the ambiguity of either elevating itself to substance or remaining indifferent to it. If we allow substance to remain inactive, we cannot know at all which of its features are essential and which are not since they would all seem to have the same significance. Discovery, observation and description can therefore decide absolutely nothing about the difference between the contents of what it discovers, observes and describes. In order to separate the inessential from the essential, we must eavesdrop on substance in its activity, which reveals only that which proves itself to be essential in appearance. The accidents preserved in active substance are, in fact, the essential ones, and the ones that perish are the inessential ones. The experiment is no longer, like observation and description, a passive cognition, since I do not leave the object presented to me as it is. I cause the substance to change with other means, I translate it into the verb and apply the causal relationship to it. In equality, however, several substances are always interacting with one another, therefore I am not considering each substance etymologically in isolation, but rather its syntactical unity. At the same time, in order to recognize the effectiveness of each substance in isolation, I must separate it from the influences of all others. 

Since the effects of other substances on an active substance are, for it, inessential and accidental, the essence of the experiment consists precisely in separating the accidental and thus in tearing apart what is bound together in a material way. The ancients mostly remained at the level of observation and description without advancing to experiment. What is characteristic of the modern standpoint is to abolish the immediate existence of nature in order to arrive at the true interior of nature only through the negation of this immediacy. One might think that if I change the object, I compromise my knowledge of it by no longer leaving it as it is. But through this change, I abolish the false and unfounded in it instead, and grasp its manifestation in its negativity, for it is freed from all accidental conditions that would disturb its logical necessity. This is the goal of every experiment in physics. In order to emphasize the pure authenticity of any natural form, such as electricity, I must remove all inhibiting factors. Among other things, I keep out the moist air, surround the machine with insulators that do not allow the force to dissipate, etc. In order to have the essential phenomenon of the free fall of bodies, I must also subtract the resistance of the air or any other medium in which the fall takes place. However, in this procedure, physicists must be very careful to not insert their own opinions or hypotheticals in the resulting phenomenon. The truth which arises from the stripping away of inessential circumstances has as its content the permanent universal, which is the object of experience.

### Experience

In experience, the material of sensibility is completely balanced with the categories of understanding, in that in the lasting, the ephemeral, the imperishable are maintained, without abandoning the sensuous content. From the categories come the universality and necessity of experience and from intuition comes the content.

The method of experience is induction, i.e. the process of discovering the general from individual cases by analyzing and comparing them. The test corresponds to syntactic sentence formation, while induction corresponds to the quantification of grammatical structure. The qualities of the substances are again connected with each other, and from this the general is deduced. To this end, however, the manifold of phenomena must be considered in many different ways and frequently turned back before a satisfactory result is reached. Experience is therefore only the result of a long history of testing, so its fruits have only become apparent late in humankind, for every people as well as for the individual. 

The content of experience, because it stems from this dual, partly aprioristic, partly aposterior source, is the universal principle on the one hand, and the change of phenomena on the other. The understanding, after having brought their inner essence to light, does not leave either contentless, but rather communicates the differences of the phenomenon in such a way that it reduces their varied diversity to an essential, simple difference. The fall of individual bodies has its contingent side, the composition of the body and the medium through which it moves modifies its velocity in many ways. In all this contingency, however, the essential relationship remains, and that is the difference between space and time, namely that equal spaces are traversed in equal times. The number of planets orbiting the sun is also contingent in that no truly adequate knowledge has been able to demonstrate the necessity of this number. But that the planets orbit in an ellipse around their center, has been found, through many years of experience, to be universally necessary. Thus, the observation has no longer the same content as the appearance, it is the essence of the appearance itself, and this the appearance, as it appears in the guise of contingency. For this reason, however, the two sides do not correspond. The individual case is not an adequate expression of the appearance. Just as there are aberrations in organic nature, so too in inorganic nature the contingency with which every individual case is afflicted can go so far in one or in some cases that the rule cannot be recognized at all, or can only be tenatively recognized. The universality and necessity of experience are thus opposed by the exception, and if the general principle is a product of experience, then it is also a fact of experience that no rule is without an exception.

This contingency of the non-correspondence of both sides is, however, at the same time a necessity that flows a priori from the nature of the content, and this will result in the lack of experience. Because of the infinite number of individual cases, each of which belongs to a general law, experience can never be completed. An infinity of observations and experiments cannot be conducted in a given finite time. We can therefore never know whether the cases that have not been experienced, new ones of which must constantly present themselves, correspond or not to the law abstracted from the observed cases. If we now experience, as we very often do, that newly added cases do not correspond to the law, but the more the exceptions accumulate, the more mistrustful we must become of our law and finally have no other option but to abandon it. The general defect from which experience and its method, induction, suffers is thus the attempt to substitute the multiplicity of cases for the totality, and to transfer what is true of the former to the latter and burden it with. This lack of experience, which clings to all empirical sciences, and demonstrates the inadequacy of their mode of knowledge, since they are in a state of constant change and always establish modified or entirely new laws, proves that the understanding is not the highest form of thinking. As experience, it has arrived at a contradiction that cannot be resolved within its own domain because, despite the apriority of its categories, it nevertheless stands, so to speak, with the strongest of its feet on the ground of sensibility, whose nature is the indefiniteness of spatial and temporal succession. The first step to removing these contradictions of experience is the power of judgment.

## Judgmentation

Just as imagination can only imagine if it has the material of experience provided by sensibility, judgmentation can only judge if it has consistent rules and principles for judgment provided by understanding. For instance, a legislator needs understanding to legislate, that is, to understand the general rule that consistently appears across many individual cases. By contrast, a judge needs judgmentation to judge since they do not, like the legislator, only need to consider cases that have already occurred, but to decide on ones that have recently appeared. Judges cannot freely invent laws that apply to each individual case, but can only select the law from the existing set of laws that is most relevant to a given case. In this way, judgmentation overcomes the deficiency of experience indicated earlier, for it makes the individual case conform to a general law by determining the principle or rule under which the case must be considered. 

Every judgment is a sentence in that it is a connection of subject and predicate, but not every sentence is a judgment. In a sentence, the substance is related to an arbitrary accident or activity, e.g. "A car drives by". In a judgment, however, the predicate is the general principle under which the subject is subsumed. The subject does not have to be a sensory individual, but can be (and often is) a set of complex of ideas and categories, such as a legal case. This subsumption by the faculty of judgment is also by no means carried out mechanically, as if one only needed to compare each case with an existing set of laws to find the corresponding one to which it should be related. Due to the imperfection of experience and the inability of any rule to encompass the totality of cases, the judge has no other option but to derive a new general principle from the existing laws in order to correctly judge the individual case. Applying the lifeless law in its strictness to every single case would often be the greatest injustice, and here the Terentian maxim applies: summum jus, summa injuria (extreme law is extreme injustice). The faculty of judgment is therefore not merely a matter of memory and experience, even if it still depends on them in terms of content. The faculty of judgment relates reality to its concept and decides whether the reality adequate to the concept or not, e.g. whether the law has been violated or not. 

The understanding and its rules can certainly be taught, but the faculty of judgment cannot be taught, for only the general is teachable. The faculty of judgment, however, is this insight that recognizes the general in all individual cases. For this reason, judgment is an immediate, natural gift that can be sharpened and practiced, but cannot be taught through instruction. The unthinking person can, with diligence, impress many facts, rules, and principles upon their memory, but has no ability to make proper use of this wealth. In this sense, judgment is rightly considered a superior and more valuable skill than memory. There may be people with prodigious memories, having learned from the best and most comprehensive experience available to them and genuinely knowing a lot about the topic at hand. But what good does any of this knowledge do them if they cannot apply it? Experience and learning are in this way completely lifeless without the deeper insight and power that judgmentation provides.

Since judgment requires the relationship between the two sides, the case and the rule, there are two paths that can be conceived to realize this relationship. Either the particular case is given and the task of judgment is to make the general rule fully explicit in the particular case. This is the easier of the two approaches since knowledge of rules is a matter of the understanding, so I just need to retrieve the appropriate rule from what I already know. We can call this faculty of judgment the subsuming faculty. Kant calls it the reflective faculty because thinking moves back and forth between fixed determinations of thought without producing anything new. The rule is already given, the particular case is presupposed, and the faculty of judgment is the reflective movement and copula between these two fixed extremes. However, once the rule is given and the case has been selected, the case will, as directly derived from the rule, be adequate to it, and such a case is now called an example. This faculty of judgment is thus the exemplary one, and Kant calls it, because of the creative element contained within it, the determining faculty. Its exercise is more difficult than that of the subsuming faculty because only the rules, as the general, are already comprehensible me in pure thinking, whereas individual cases, on the other hand, first have to exist before they can be thought. For the same reason, that it was easier in memory to find the representation corresponding to the sign than the sign corresponding to the representation, it is also easier to recognize a grammatical rule in the text of a writer when reviewing it than to correctly apply the rule in an extemporaneous speech using an adequate example. Thus, while on the one hand the creation of one's own example demonstrates the highest activity of the faculty of judgment, conversely, the example provided by others provides help to the faculty of judgment, allowing it to strengthen and develop itself.

The exemplary faculty of judgment makes the immediate transition from judgmentation to reason. For now the last imperfection of experience is also overcome, which had still persisted through the subsuming faculty of judgment. Although the relation of the general rule to the individual case is still present in the exemplifying faculty of judgment, the general rule is proven because of the complete adequacy of the example. In understanding and experience, on the other hand, the particular case was the foundation, and the rule only a product of abstraction from many instances, which could be also be modified by newly added ones. Judgment in general already deprives the particular of the value of a principle by subordinating it to a general rule. Especially in the case of an example, however, the particular case is generated by the rule only to the extent that it is evidence of the rule's existence, and whatever else is observed is incidental and has no essential existence to it. The particular no longer obscures the universal in any way since the latter attains reality in the former, it is simultaneously preserved as the universal within it, providing the basis for the standpoint of reason.

## Reason

Rational knowledge no longer begins with experience, but begins from within the subject and descends as a pure conceptual development from the universal to the particular as the one and only reality appropriate to the concept. The universal is no longer, as in the understanding, abstracted from experience, in which case it is not truly universal, but only has relative or comparative universality as something that is common to many things. Only the universality of reason that possesses true universality and necessity. Kant originally emphasized this very distinction between understanding and reason. The understanding, according to him, only provides knowledge of the finite, the appearances. However, while he attributes to reason an endeavor to know the unconditioned, he nevertheless remains skeptical that this endeavor is possible for reason to complete. He argues that if knowledge of the unconditioned were possible, reason would have to apply the categories of the understanding to its objects, thereby rendering them finite. Kant could not transform the concepts of the understanding into categories of reason, although Plato and Aristotle had already done so. Plato and Aristotle had distinguished between the two domains as Kant had done, but neither of them denied the possibility of rational knowledge. If reason had left it doubtful that there is nothing in the intellect that was not first in the senses, then the opposite principle, that there nothing is in the senses that was not first in the intellect, applies unconditionally to reason.

The classical dispute about the source of ideas, which had been fiercely debated by Locke and Leibniz, has an easy answer and explanation. The human mind certainly appears to be completely devoid of content at birth and acquires knowledge only through external impressions. However, this knowledge is only partial knowledge of the world due to the inexhaustibility of individual cases, so the case and the rule, the external and the internal, never perfectly correspond to each other. If truth is to arise in knowledge as the identity of the subjective and the objective, then the subject can neither be empty nor copy the object, but must itself be one and the same totality that the object is. The human mind thus has a method of knowledge independent of experience. For knowing to become active, it certainly requires experience, but this is only the stimulus for knowing, not the source of knowledge. So when Aristotle asserted that one cannot think what one has not felt, he only meant that knowledge does not appear until it is opened from the outside, not that the mind only has knowledge of what reaches it from the outside. In other words, the inherently existing truth does not come to consciousness if the senses do not awaken it. If one understands by innate ideas that consciousness is prior to the actual existence of an external stimulus, then one misunderstands the nature of being-in-itself, that the possibility of an existent precedes its actual existence. Furthermore, Plato's mythical conception of innate ideas must be completely rejected, which claims that since a consciousness in this life does not exist before the stimulus of the senses, then ideas must have pre-existed in the previous life of the soul, and all teaching and instruction would be a matter of remembering what you had forgotten in a previous life. Here Plato also misunderstands the nature of being-in-itself and wants to grant it a priority in time when time is not even relevant to the question. Against such a conception, Aristotle's question is entirely appropriate: "If such knowledge were innate, shouldn't we be surprised that we possessed the noblest of sciences without knowing it?". It is not our knowledge of science that is innate, but the concept from which our knowledge of science develops. 

### Faith

The first mode of this recognition of reason is the immediate apprehension of its object as an inner truth, which requires no mediation through concepts of the understanding. We call this mode of knowledge faith, and since it certainly belongs to a priori rational knowledge, it is not without reason that people have spoken about a rational faith. I believe something not because it is confirmed by
my external senses, but even without and against all experience, because I find the confirmation of it absolutely within myself. "Blessed are," says Christ, "those who believe without seeing." What I believe is so connected with my spirit that I cannot help but accept it if I do not want to deny my own being.

In faith, the object is inseparably connected with me and is by no means given from the outside. As an example, let us consider faith in the true sense, religious faith. We do not know God through sensory experience because He is, as Kant expresses it, not given in the context of sensory appearances. God remains eternally distant and unknowable to the understanding, and if reason were to apply categories of the understanding and intuitions of the senses to Him, God would be completely transcendent or transcendental to them. However, my faith in God is a priori, independent of all experience, existing within myself and interwoven with my being. I believe in Him, and in believing, I relate directly to Him. Faith is the middle ground between opinion and knowledge: opinion is a subjectively and objectively insufficient claim to truth, faith is subjectively sufficient and simultaneously objectively insufficient, and finally, knowledge is the subjectively and objectively sufficient. But it must be remembered that faith does not consider itself less certain than knowledge, on the contrary, it is absolute confidence and unwavering certainty. But it is true, of course, that it also rejects any objective mediation and justification, not because it is built on uncertain foundations, but because it considers any non-subjective validation to be subordinate to it. And if faith is taken subjectively in a less precise sense, so too is its object. One can say that any empirical fact is believed even if its verification through the senses is lacking, or also of a mathematical theorem, if I have not gone through the intermediate steps of the reasoning that are supposed to prove it. 

In a philosophical sense, Jacobi made faith the principle of knowledge: he disdained the understanding as second-hand or derived knowledge and viewed the source of all knowledge and truth only in faith, as this idealistic discovery of all content within oneself, which he calls first-hand or immediate knowledge. In all mediations of the senses, he says, we always see only our own seeing and hear only our own hearing, that an object corresponds to them is given only through belief. We believe, according to Jacobi, that we have a body, and without this belief we cannot go out the door, come to the table or bed. If we did not have this belief, we would not consider any of them as things, but as mental representations. But with this, belief has at the same time transcended itself. For in declaring mediation to be insufficient to verify an object, it thereby renders it unstable, at least where such mediations are indispensable to it, and the acceptance or rejection of such an object is thus regulated by the arbitrary will of the subject.

### Enlightenment 

The discernment between reality and illusion by the subject is the standpoint of Enlightenment, which follows directly from faith just as both were combined in Jacobi's philosophy. Faith that leaps over the mediations of the understanding forcibly maintains itself on the standpoint that immediate is already infected with disbelief and on the verge of transitioning into it. Whoever consciously rejects the testimony of the senses and the reflections of the understanding knows that the firm anchor of faith has vanished for them.

Faith that is conscious of its own groundlessness is now superstition. The original, unreflective faith had its foundation within itself since the believing subject would also have to doubt itself if it were to doubt its object. But if we have already separated from our object and want to be reunified with it, then the bridge, if it is not built from the understanding, is insecure. Despite this, we still cling to this immediate knowledge even though the understanding directly contradicts it. Understanding contradicts faith precisely because the latter refuses to accept any arguments or proofs for its validation. However, the understanding considers everything that is unproven to be superstition. It is therefore the understanding that intrudes into faith and denounces it as superstition. The enlightened understanding finds the immediate knowledge of faith deficient since in its unreflective state it did not possess such insight. The deficiency of faith consists more precisely in the fact that, because it has no objective basis, it cannot be validated for others, nor can it be communicated at all. Those who do not believe cannot be helped to believe, nor can faith be forced upon them. Reason, on the other hand, contains necessity, so it enters into conflict with faith, attacks it, and strives to destroy it.

What faith lacks is insight, and it is precisely this insight that reason seeks to produce in its goal to abolish faith. Insight is the movement of thought that is applied to the fixed presuppositions of faith. Reason rightly asserts that one should accept nothing that has not been elevated to the clarity of insight through a series of mediations. Therefore, faith is an obstacle to reason because it is an unproven conviction. This marble, this bread dough, etc. is immediately regarded as divine by uncritical faith. Insight reveals the contradiction that the absolute being is a feelingless piece of stone, a piece of bread assimilated by humans. Insight likewise demonstrates the contradictions in theological exegesis that lie in the external historical testimonies of faith. It further demands mediations of reason to understand this, which reason cannot provide. God is just, and yet, so many good people are unhappy and so many wicked people are happy. God is wise, and yet, there is so much in nature that fails to achieve its purpose. Thus, insight comes to overthrow faith by dissecting it, for in its eyes, remaining unshakable in one's conviction despite such contradictions makes faith into superstition.

The result of the enlightenment brought about by insight is doubt. One refrains from making a decision about such things because reason leads to contradictions, and one persists in doubt because of their irresolvability. Doubt is a necessary element of human thought, as Descartes began his methodical philosophizing with the principle: "De omnibus dubitandum est" (Everything must be doubted), not in order to remain perpetually in doubt, but to arrive at a secure foundation of knowledge through doubt. However necessary doubt may be on the one hand, it cannot overthrow faith. If faith only holds to the inner unity of the subject with its object, which is the essence of reason, then the attacks of the understanding will be powerless against it. The problem is that, on the one hand, faith seeks to maintain itself by consciously disregarding the mediating processes of the understanding, and on the other hand, the understanding does not acknowledge the content of reason provided by faith precisely because it is not proven, even though the lack of formal proof should not have led to the rejection of content. Therefore, the truth is that immediate knowledge must allow itself to be guided by the mediating process of the understanding, and only through it can the understanding arrive at the recognition of that content.

### Knowledge

The recognition of the content of faith by the understanding has thereby reached the highest activity of the intellect, and just as we successively attained the psychological foundation of art in imagination and of religion in faith, we must now consider that of philosophy in knowledge. What is presented here is, in a sense, the speculative instruments or tools of science. Knowledge is faith that has been fully developed and sustained through the mediations of the understanding. Therefore, in knowledge, reason and understanding are in inseparably united. Because of this totality, scientific thinking is distinguished by three moments: immediate knowledge, mediated knowledge, and absolute knowledge.

Immediate knowledge is necessarily the beginning of philosophical thinking. For one must not begin with something that would require mediation and proof, because in that case it would remain an unfounded assertion. As something quite simple and abstract, which requires no proof precisely because it does not yet have any manifold content within itself, the beginning must therefore be something immediately certain, and one even could say that it is something immediately posited by faith. The thought of pure being is thus the absolute beginning of knowledge, for only it is the most abstract category without any content determining what being is. Pure being, as an abstract thought, is simultaneously a determination of the subject whose pure thinking is identical with being. Descartes' statement: "Cogito, ergo sum", was therefore rightly expressed by him as an immediately certain truth that requires no mediation through reasoning. But not only do we have being through faith, being is the proper object of faith, and this faith is neither capable of nor in need of any other kind of knowledge because it does not require it. If God is only believed in, then it is quite consistent to characterize God as the intellectual abstraction of pure being in all existence, as Jacobi did, or in French philosophy, as the highest being. The belief in God is not sufficient for the absolute certainty of His existence because He requires an infinity of mediations if He is to stand justified before knowledge. 

The first moment of immediate knowledge can also be described as intellectual knowledge since this unmediated abstraction of pure being is also an object of the understanding, so the extremes of faith and understanding work together here without in the least being opposed to each other. As an abstract thought, the immediate thought of being is abstracted from its opposite and is not at all entangled with it, because otherwise mediation would occur. Thus, faith does not move from one thought to another, but remains unwavering in the simplicity of the one and only thought. Whereas the understanding, occupying the position of doubt, does indeed move from one side to the other, considering the one and the other, but only to grasp one by forgetting the other. The principle of mutual exclusion is the main principle of the understanding, and faith likewise holds so firmly to its position that it rejects every possibility of the opposite. The unwavering certainty of faith is precisely the highest abstraction of the understanding, showing that faith is a form of knowledge and as well as a moment of thinking, which is usually overlooked in the consideration of the nature of faith. But just as we have seen that faith is understanding, so too is understanding faith since it keeps mediation at a distance from it, immediately and without further consideration accepting one of the two opposites in order to reject the other.

This faithful understanding or understanding faith, however, soon realizes the state of contradiction into which it has fallen. The movement of insight seizes upon this presupposition of thought and awakens it from its slumber. For if the understanding can decide in favor of one of the opposites and accepts it in faith, then another understanding can, with equal right, demand belief in the opposite. Indeed, insight shows that the understanding's position is its own worst enemy. Something only forms an opposite to something else if and only if it carries the definition of the latter within itself. In every immediate concept of the understanding posited by faith, insight shows that it contradicts itself and contains its opposite within itself, and in this way perishes through internal contradiction. This is dialectically mediated knowledge. This knowledge no longer belongs to the understanding, but to reason, since it makes the static one-sidedness of thought dynamic, and the instrument that reason uses for this purpose is dialectics. It was a profound insight of Kant when he stated that reason necessarily encounters such contradictions. However, Kant's solution to the contradictions that reason encounters is still one-sided in that it consists only in the fact that the understanding, but not the things in themselves, are supposed to contradict themselves. 

Even if dialectics is initially purely negative, we have no reason to stop there since, through the following consideration, there is a positive result to dialectics that has yet to be produced by the movement of insight. To give the simplest example of dialectical knowledge, being contains a contradiction that makes it impossible for us to remain in the belief in this abstract category. Being has only become accessible to us through abstraction from everything determinate. Through the absolute negation with which being is afflicted, it becomes nothing for us. The thought of nothingness has thus arisen dialectically for us from that of being, which is its opposite. The knowledge of nothingness is no longer immediate knowledge, but is mediated, for nothing has come to us through the movement that brought insight to being, and consequently it has been proven. The immediate knowledge of being is thus contrasted with the mediated knowledge of nothingness. Nothingness is the development of the inherently existing, immediate thought contained in being. This mediated knowledge, however, itself becomes the object of insight again. Nothingness is indeed the result of being, but the understanding isolates it again and presents it as a one-sided abstract moment that, by forgetting the origin from which it has just arisen, appears again as something immediate. Thus, the same process of dialectics begins anew. Nothing is the same indeterminacy and absolute negation that being is, it is the general, pure thought that is identical with being. Now being is mediated by nothing, just as previously nothing was mediated by being. Being has thus ceased to be immediate since it has arisen from nothing. However, that by which being is mediated is itself a product of being, so being mediates itself. But whose certainty is mediated only through itself is precisely the immediately certain. Thus, in this double mediation, immediacy is restored and the unity of both sides is established, each opposite generates the other, and the circle of mediations closes on itself.

Initially, one could also consider this entire movement as something negative. Since being transforms into nothing, and nothing transforms into being, one cancels out the other through its equilibrium. The impartiality of dialectics demands that we make the double movement back and forth. But if I have walked a mile to the east and another to the west, then I am back to the place from which I started and have not covered the slightest distance. Every assertion can therefore, according to the antilogic of the Sophists, whose art consists precisely in disputing in utramque partem (on both sides), be countered by its opposite. If one is as true and as false as the other, then truth and falsity are related to each other only negatively, and to remain at this negative stage is the essence of sophistry. There is reason in this only to the extent that thinking is no longer blinded by one-sided thought determinations and relegates them to their proper limits, but it is a limited reason that limits itself to the negative aspect of dialectics.

The positive result, which spontaneously arises from the preceding dialectical movement, is now grasped as speculative rational knowledge. It is none other than the movement of mediation, having returned to immediacy, restoring the absolute content of faith from doubt. Similarly, when being manifests itself in nothing, and nothing in being, they are not two things that just overlap, but are inseparable moments of one and the same whole. They are opposed, otherwise they could not become each other, but they are also the same, otherwise they would not be compatible in the result. Truth does not consist in their complete annihilation, but only the cancellation of their one-sidedness. Likewise, falsity does not stand outside of truth, but is contained within truth as one of its aspects, it becomes false only when one part of truth is singled out and declared to be the whole truth. It has therefore been rightly asserted that there is no such thing as absolute error, that there is always something true to be found in every error, for error is only one side of truth obscured by its isolation. Truth consists in this immediate certainty of itself that mediates itself by mediating opposing elements through one another, and through the negation of their one-sidedness, posits itself as their meaningful totality. Truth, therefore, possesses the immediate certainty of faith, which has proven and maintained itself through the mediations of the understanding and insight.

The positive result of this highest activity of reason is therefore, more precisely, the unity of opposing determinations, but not as an inanimate rest, but as a living mediation, in which the moments, even if not their independence, nevertheless preserve their content. The Idea, as a product of reason, is the unity of being and nothing, of the one and the many, of something and its other, of essence and appearance, of matter and form, of possibility and reality, of substance and accident, of necessity and contingency, of cause and effect, of subject and object, and all other categories of the understanding. They are only untrue when they are opposed to each other and one is supposed to be refuted by the other. 

Speculative knowledge is the highest stage of theoretical spirit, the most complete development of thinking, and is thinking in the true sense of the word. As reason, it has attained absolute self-sufficiency, recognizing itself as the totality of all being. In the understanding and its empirical knowledge, the object is regarded as the rule to which the subject must submit and be integrated. By grasping itself as the substance of things, as the source from which their essence flows, thinking shifts the standard of measure into a different realm, it is no longer to be sought in the objective world, but in spirit itself. Spirit appears as the power according to which all things are judged. This is the true meaning of Protagoras' statement that man is the measure of all things, which how Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle also accepted it. But even if theoretical reason is the measure, it still remains enclosed in its intellectual world. The actual realization of this identity of subject and object, produced internally through thought and making itself real, is realized by will or practical reason.
