# Posthistory

> [ Work in Progress ]

> This webpage is a direct translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Philosophy of History Vol. II* (1881) 

The concept of the post-historical era is implicit in the idea that Reason, acting with artistic energy, is destined to mold humanity into its own image in the the entirety of social life. This is so that the individual increasingly becomes an embodiment of the Eternal Personality of the Spirit within the universal life of humanity. While in this manner the timeless prehistoric world is restored, and the lost Paradise is regained, this process is not destined to occur as it did initially, within the realm of unconscious feeling, but with clear consciousness amidst the full maturation of the individual personality, within a time that is identical with eternity. However, just as complete innocence was not present in the primordial state, neither will absolute virtue arise in the final acts of world history. Individuals remain individuals at all times, so the boundary imposed upon the universal life within them can never be entirely effaced. Nevertheless, just as the view positing suddenness in the physical transformation of the Earth, and in the moral progress of humanity, has had to give way to the concept of a gradual transition from the primordial era into the historical era, so too will the unfolding of the third world age, the approaching Realm of Reason upon Earth, be a process of gradual evolution. For Reason already holds sway, though in a different and less decisive manner, within the first two world ages as well, and in the third age, it will attain an ever-greater dominion.

As long as the substantial powers governing human life have not all been fully won, a conflict often inevitably arose between public morality and the morality of the individual. In the latter, whenever established ethical norms began to waver, the individual was left to rely on their own conscience, which often stood in contradiction to the prevailing public conscience. This collision ceases to occur once all principles have been fully attained: no statesman need any longer, even at the cost of violating the moral sensibilities of citizens who still cling to the old order, force through their innovations, even if by violent means. Therefore, the primary element in the concept of this final age is that the opposition between politics and morality, of which Kant spoke, vanishes, as much as the conscience of every individual has now found its secure anchorage within the already firmly established totality of ethical powers. However, notwithstanding the peace between individuals and the state, this third age of world history does not, for that reason, stagnate in a state of inert repose. For since individuals are continually born anew and must invariably grow into the life of the community, they remain from cradle to grave, as in the first two epochs, the raw material that must be molded into the state of being which exists *in and for itself*.

However, since this state, existing in and for itself, was brought forth by the movement of history during the second age of world history, meaning that the human species now stands complete not merely *in itself* (as was already the case in the first age), but also *for itself* (since every nation developed the principle assigned to it by the World Spirit to its full perfection), the second defining feature of the post-historical era is this: that it no longer requires a hero, a figure in whom a new principle would, for the very first time, come to conscious awareness. Because there are no new principles left to discover in this age, one that a hero would have to discover through their genius and set before the eyes of their people, the lineage of heroes loses its very *raison d'être*. It has died out in the third age of world history because it is no longer necessary, as it was in the second, for heroes to employ their more advanced, higher consciousness to lead the rest of humanity (the people who still cling to the old principle) toward a new direction. In the first age, this lineage had not yet been born, for in that era, the principles were still germinating, alive yet still latent, within the obscure and intuitive feelings of all people.

In this matter, since the species and its peoples have been fully realized and passed down from the first two ages of the world into the third, all that remains for this last age, as its sole objective, is the cultivation of the individual, and this alone will henceforth constitute the entire movement of world history. The individual no longer needs to look up to another individual, one who might lead them by the hand and awaken them to the consciousness of a new principle. Without external and alien individual initiative, everyone already has the given conditions before their eyes as a completed totality. Following the perfection of the human race in general, and following the perfection of every particular people, which embodied itself in a few great individuals (serving as its "nobility of merit"), the task at hand can now concern itself solely with the perfection of the individual. It is no longer a few outstanding spirits who are to become adequate to the concept of the species, but an ever-increasing number of individuals are to do so, imprinting the eternal Personality of the Spirit within their own selves, until all of them have attained this state.

This totality, viewed as an empirical concept, can never be given in its full completeness, since in the progression of generations extending into infinity, new individuals are constantly coming into being. Nevertheless, we must always regard the purpose of the third age of the world as fully attained, notwithstanding the aforementioned imperfection that is inherent to every individual. Moreover, the attainment of the goal—to shape oneself in accordance with the Idea—will be rendered far easier for future individuals than it was for those who have passed away, because the perfect exemplar of moral relations is already given to them as an unwavering guiding principle to which they adhere. Edmund Pfleiderer therefore rightly observes in his *The Idea of ​​a Golden Age* that there undeniably exists the possibility of a moral life that becomes ever more universal in the course of time—a progressive *collective morality*. Qualitatively and quantitatively, to a stronger degree and within much wider circles, there presents itself an enhanced opportunity to be a moral being in the true sense of the word. This holds true even if, amidst the very improvement of general conditions, the realization of the Idea (which is increasing both extensively and intensively) can occasionally give rise to temporary incoherence and grievances. The more thorough the reconciliation, the more starkly do the oppositions that have yet to be overcome stand out in relief. The individual, now standing firmly on their own feet, with the consciousness of their human dignity and with the strength infinitely augmented by voluntary association with their peers, discovers both the sufficient conditions and the inner certainty to bring to expression, within all people, that exemplar which stands clearly visible before the eyes of all.

While it might seem that life in the post-historical era will be boring, because nothing new will occur in history, and no further progress within it exists: no exciting expectations, no wars, and no revolutions will remain to stir up human excitement, but it is far more interesting for the individual to work on their own self-cultivation than on that of the world. Once the universal life, conceived as the ultimate purpose of world history as a whole, has been attained, the individual, having, become the sole purpose of that history, turns away from the struggles of practical life toward contemplation and the enjoyment of this achieved end. This constitutes the fourth point in the history of the future. Just as in the twilight years of the individual the spirit withdraws from practice into theory, so now do all individuals act in the twilight of the world—a twilight, however, that is spiritual in nature and imbued with the freshness of youth. This theory is not the source, but the result of practice: it is the *Ideal*. The highest spheres of human activity—a universal art, a universal philosophy—will serve as the ground upon which the individual cultivates and enjoys themselves. The social, political, and religious life, in their perfected forms, serve merely as the material and spiritual means enabling the individual to devote themselves to their ultimate purpose without disturbance. Living amidst the full memory of the past, the individual partakes of those pleasures which the millennia-long labor of the human race has prepared and now offers—pleasures available in ever-increasing abundance and with ever-greater ease. This is by no means boring, on the contrary, this self-enjoyment is engaging and delightful to the highest degree. The doctrine of happiness finds its rightful place here, for it functions precisely as it did in the spirit of the ancients, encompassing not only material pleasures, but also the highest pleasures of humanity’s moral and spiritual life. We may also accept the doctrine of utility to the extent that it serves this moral purpose—as man increasingly subjects nature to the power of his will and transforms the lessons of history into a means of education for the youth.

## The End of Posthistory

When the conditions of the future have completely permeated and mastered its very substance, and when natural and historical principles have guided the whole of humanity to become one family—the very self-consciousness of the globe—then so too will the institutions of the future be fashioned in accordance with the ideal articulated in the philosophy of law. The practical realization of universal reason, which has been demonstrated throughout the entire course of the philosophy of history, will then revert to the realm of theory. For we have indeed witnessed this ideal appearing in ever-clearer outline from the surging waves of history and assuming an ever-more distinct form such that it can be presented scientifically as a rounded and complete whole. Thus, at the conclusion of this philosophy of history, I need only recall the fundamental features of this ideal, confident that I have posited no chimera in asserting its realization in the future. The achievements of the historical era already provide actual, unmistakable proof of the progress of world history towards that very goal, spanning from Japan to Australia.

We shall now describe these institutions once again from the threefold perspective of social, religious, and political life. However, since religion, among the various natural and spiritual needs of the individual, only appears as one such need among others, it ceases to function as an institution within or in opposition to the state. Religion is the individual’s most personal concern, one to be reckoned among the rights of the individual within society. Standing in counterpoise to the individuality of the free spirit is its particular nationality within the state and the federation. While the final stage will be the elevation of freedom to the universality of humanity, as embodied in international law and cosmopolitan law. Thus, we may arrange the three perspectives under which we intend to outline the freedom and reason of future into the following chapters: the Individual and Society, the State and Nationality, and Cosmopolitan Law and Humanity.

### The Individual and Society

The freedom of the individual naturally presupposes freedom of the person, property, and labor. In the future, slavery, serfdom, feudal bondage, corvée labor, entails, feudal rights, and hereditary leases will vanish. Between workers, there must exist complete equality of rights and absolute freedom of contract regarding payment. Monopolies and property held in mortmain shall cease to exist, and in the latter case, such property will only be recognized as the property of a free association, and its partition can be demanded at any time by any member of the community, particularly once the original purpose of the foundation has been fulfilled. Hunting, fishing and mining are appurtenances of the ownership of forests, bodies of water, or mineral deposits, respectively. Intellectual property is protected, but after a certain period, through the principle of acquisitive prescription, it passes into the public domain. 

Freedom of transportation and trade is absolute. Initially, municipalities walled themselves off from one another, then provinces followed suit, and finally states erected barriers against one another. Commercial treaties between states constitutes a restriction upon commercial freedom of other states. Lotteries are also abolished as immoral. Suicide, crime, and murder will, as the means of subsistence become more readily available, grow increasingly rare. The death penalty will fall into disuse once intentional murder, committed without any mitigating circumstances, is no longer perpetrated. Correctional institutions, as well as solitary confinement for the most unsociable criminals, are forms of punishment to be preferred.

In family law, civil marriage and the family council take precedence, and in intestate succession, complete equality of distribution among lines of equal proximity is established. The surviving spouse inherits half of the estate, provided he or she contributes their own assets to the common fund. In testamentary matters, the testator’s *disposable quota* applies, a portion over which they may freely dispose, and the size of which varies according to the number of lineal descendants. 

Associations of all kinds foster both labor and enjoyment—production and consumption. The unrestricted circulation of goods becomes the surest means of preventing stagnation and commercial crises. Every industry forms worker associations to better organize its common affairs and to provision for the manufacture and sale of its products. Farmers unite in a manner similar to that of the ancient *Markgenossenschaften* (village commons associations). Artisans, manufacturers, physicians, artists, and teachers likewise join together in associations founded upon the fundamental principle of self-governance. Among themselves, they establish insurance funds, pensions, and the like. All citizens residing within a given locality form free associations for social, recreational, and educational purposes, as well as for the conduct of political elections.

Above all, however, the need for religion is one that unites all workers into a one community. For religion is intended precisely to manifest this substantive unity of all human beings. It goes without saying, however, that joining this association—like any other—must be an entirely voluntary act. In the exercise of complete freedom of belief, every individual is at liberty to join whichever religious community they feel drawn to, or conversely, to refrain from joining any community at all, should they find that no one resonates with them, prefer to seek their spiritual edification in solitude.

The federation of all worker associations constitutes the civil municipality. The distinction between municipalities—specifically, that of a village versus a city—is merely quantitative, a difference only in territorial extent, unless one further accompanied by a relative preponderance of specialized occupations. The village is the smaller municipality, characterized predominantly by agriculture, whereas the city is the larger municipality, characterized predominantly by commerce and industry. This distinction notwithstanding, however qualitative it may appear, a principle of absolute constitutional equality prevails. Even the disparity in size is effectively neutralized: villages, towns, and small cities may consolidate into larger administrative units, while large cities may be subdivided into smaller communities. Ultimately, however, any kind of occupation may be pursued within both types of municipality, free from all feudal monopolies or guild restrictions, all with complete liberty and without hindrance.

Every municipality is a republic in miniature that governs itself. The municipal councilors, elected by universal suffrage from all municipal inhabitants, without distinction of profession, constitutes its legislative power. Once elected, the municipal councilors establish the municipal charter, which is not imposed upon them by the state, and allocate funds for the general affairs of the municipality.

The magistracy is charged with the administration of municipal affairs—is elected by worker associations that represent the various professions of the municipality, which sends their representatives to the individual departments of this administrative council, ranging from agriculture, trades, commerce, education, and so on, in order to manage the collective interests of their respective industries within the municipality. These bodies do not merely function as committees, as the legislators do, but also as permanent departments, each managing its own specific domain, yet capable of convening in plenary session whenever matters common to all or several branches of the administration requires deliberation. The municipal education councilors are responsible for public instruction, specifically general public education, and are elected by the public schoolteachers. The ecclesiastical councilors oversee the collective affairs of all religious sects and churches, and are elected by all members of the respective religious communities. The committees of the legislative municipal councilors assist the specialists within the administrative municipal councilors and share the workload with them. 

One division of the magistracy constitutes the municipal court for civil and criminal law, its members elected by and from the jurists of the community. For specialized matters, the relevant divisions function as courts for each industry headed by a syndic responsible for representing the legal aspect. Judicial authority and administration are unified, ensuring that every administrative act is determined in accordance with the law. For the court is merely one type of administration, specifically, the administration of municipal law, whereas all other branches of administration concerning societal interests are themselves the different modes of the legal community itself. The separation of the justice system from the administration was a salutary measure, serving to liberate the administration of civic law from the arbitrariness inherent to the other administrative branches. Conceptually, however, this separation ceases to exist once if this arbitrariness ceases to function in the first place. In cases of official misconduct, the relevant divisions function as disciplinary courts.

The mayor is elected from a general election conducted by the two community authorities, convening jointly for this purpose alone. The mayor serves solely as the executive power for the enforcement of laws and administration, and to this end, they may appoint associates, assistants, and assessors to aid them in the conduct of official business.

### The State and Nation

The progress from the individual to the particularity of associations, culminating in the universality of the municipality itself within personal well-being and for the satisfaction of particular needs, so too does the sphere of particularity, in which a particular national spirit and its interests find expression, now appear as a totality. The province constitutes a single individuality in which a distinct region of the nation speaks a specific dialect. The people of the region unite within the state to form a unity of political life as a distinct nationality. But when they have already become unitary states, or when different nationalities themselves form neighboring unitary states, these simple states unite into the generality of a federation, which is the perfected state of the future.

To the province, we need not yet ascribe a political organization. It stands in the middle between the municipality and the state, between society and nationality, in order to link both associations. The province is a larger association of municipal administrations. From the church councils, school councils, trade councils, commerce councils, and regional councils of the municipalities, the administrative departments of the province are formed to manage its common affairs. The governor, as the executive head of all the administrative departments, is elected by these administrative authorities. If the municipality is responsible for general public education, then the specialized schools, as vocational education, falls under the jurisdiction of the province. Thus, trade schools, commercial schools, agricultural schools, academic schools such as gymnasiums, art schools, seminaries for the training of teachers, etc.

Here, the administration also is entirely in the hands of the people through the election of all officials across worker associations. For just as the lowest and highest officials, arise indirectly from popular elections, so too do the intermediate officials, the provincial councilors, such as provincial school councilors, provincial trade councilors, provincial judges, etc. This eliminates the problem of interference from above with the self-government of the people, and the general will directs all its affairs from itself, by itself, and for itself.

### International and Cosmopolitan Law

Once every state—by virtue of the ground of nationality upon which it stands—has, at the end of history, realized in its normal form the principle assigned to it by the World Spirit, its work as a distinct entity ceases; and the totality of ethical relations is now presented to all peoples for their enjoyment. This naturally brings them into closer connection with one another. Because they share in the enjoyment of all the individual moments of spiritual, rational life within the common purpose of the human race, the rough edges of their respective national peculiarities are worn smooth against one another. Instead of hating one another—as they did in the historical era (§ 229)—they will, in the aftermath of world history, perceive themselves as the closely bound members of that one great family which we call humanity. Thus, individual states or federal states will unite to form the broader association of a confederation of states. Here, we must consider, firstly, International Law—understood as the legal relationship of individual nationalities to one another within such a confederation; secondly, we must identify the specific confederations of states in which the peoples of the earth enter into brotherhood; and thirdly, we must conclude with the universal confederation of humanity, representing the highest stage of associative existence.

As regards international law, in the first place: it must now assume a form entirely different from that which it held during the historical era—an era in which it essentially represented the "state of nature" among nations, wherein they were embroiled in a "war of all against all," and where the legal relationship appeared merely as a powerless *Ought*. The international law of the future—which, however, is already foreshadowed by many signs in the present—consists solely of a civil, legal relationship among peoples united in a single federation of states, much like that of any association, from the local community up to the federal state. Within this transformed international law, the recognition of states goes without saying. Since the true state is generally the nation-state, the recognition of states inherently entails the recognition of nationalities. Yet, even if the state and the nationality are destined to coincide, this can nevertheless be the case only insofar as is possible. For along the borders of states, mixed populations will always be found—populations that must be assigned to one side or the other, whether by mutual agreement or by free choice. And far from becoming a source of discord among the various nations within the federation, these populations serve instead as the very link and bond of friendship between neighboring nations—none of which will seek to expand beyond its borders through conquest, once its state and its nationality coincide in the broad sense.
