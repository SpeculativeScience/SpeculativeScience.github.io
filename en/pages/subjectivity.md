# Subjectivity

## Concept

```
Concept ( Effect ( Possibility ( Inner ⟲ Outer ) ) )

Universal ( Particular ( Individual ( Inner ⟲ Outer ) ) )
```

The Universal is the identity of the Concept, the One Cause of the Effects. By contrast, the Particular is the difference of the Concept, it is an Effect of the One Cause. The Universal determines the Particular because it is its One Cause, but the Particular likewise determines the Universal because it has the Particular as its content (as an Effect) and so is determined by the Particular. The Universal is, after all, particular to its Particular for the same reason that identity is different from difference. But now that the Universal is particularized against its Particular, what the Particular is now distinguishing itself from is another Particular, which is the Universal that is particular to it. Each is determined as Particular to its Particular, but this exclusion of exclusion is their Individuality. The Universal gets to be Individual because, as the identity of the Concept, it is different from the difference of the Concept. The Particular also gets to be Individual because, as the difference of the Concept, it is identical to the identity of the Concept. The Individual is generated from this difference of difference, but it is also the shared content of the Universal and Particular. The three moments of the Concept implies and generate each other, so they are all necessary to the Concept.

## Judgment

```
Universal ⟹ U

Particular ⟹ is

Individual ⟹ I
```

Judgment is the particularization of the Concept, the mediation between the Individual and Universal. The Individual is Universal, but the "is" also implies their distinction, or Particularity.


```
Judgments of Inherence (Attribute)

	Positive Judgment: I is U

	Negative Judgment: I is not U

	Infinite Judgment: I is U ⟺ I is not U

		Positive Infinite Judgment: I is I

		Negative Infinite Judgment:  I is not U1 ⟹ I is not U2 ⟹ ...
```

The Individual is the subject of the Judgment because it is the ground or identity-in-difference of the Universal and Particular. As Individual, it is an instance of the concept, specifically, the head noun of a phrase (e.g. the rose, the dog, the toaster, etc.). However, the Individual , so the task of Judgment is to determine the nature of the Individual through a *particular* Universal. The particular Universal is an attribute, or adjective that modifies the noun.

The Positive Judgment ("The Individual is Universal") conveys that an instance of a concept possesses a particular attribute. "The rose is red" expresses that this *individual* rose has the quality of redness. However, because the Positive Judgment relates an Individual to some *particular* Universal, then we can just as easily relate it to an other *particular* Universal. Some roses are red, other roses are not red. Because the Universal has been negated, the Positive Judgment has generated the Negative Judgment ("The Individual is not Universal").

If the Individual is not the Universal, then all that is left for the Individual to judge is the Individual. The moment in which subject and predicate are identical is the Positive Infinite Judgment (e.g. the rose is a rose), whereas the moment where the subject is distinguished from a series of Universals is the Negative Infinite Judgment (e.g. the rose is not an elephant, the rose is not a table, etc.)

```
Judgements of Reflection (Class)

	Singular Judgment: This I is U

	Particular Judgement: Some I’s are U ⟺ Some I’s are not U

	Universal Judgment: All I’s are U
```

In the Judgment of Inherence, the Individual could not establish its connection to the Universal because the Universal was conceptualized as a mere attribute of the Individual. With the Judgment of Reflection this relationship is reversed. The Individual now participates in the Universal. The Singular Judgment captures the Universal as a class that has only one Individual member. The positing of one Individual member implies the exclusion of other Individual members and so the Particular Judgment appears as capturing the division between which Individuals are captured by the Universal or not. This division between some Individuals that are and the other Individuals that are not, taken together s a whole, implies the Universal Judgment, or the Universal that captures all Individuals. Since the Universal captures all its Individuals, the Individuals are no longer members of a class, but are one and the same *kind* of thing.

```
Judgments of Necessity (Category)

	Categorical Judgment: I is a kind of U

	Hypothetical Judgment: I is a kind of U ⟺ U is a kind of I

	Disjunctive Judgment: I is its kinds of P/U
```

In the Categorical Judgment, the Individual is identified a kind of Universal. If the Individual is a kind, it is made up of Individuals that are identified as being a kind of Universal. However, while the Individual is necessarily an instance of its kind, the Categorical Judgment only captures one instance of this kind, so the Individual does not completely capture or explain the Universal. This produces the Hypothetical Judgment in which the Individual necessarily implies the Universal and vice versa, but it cannot be determined whether the Individual is a kind of Universal or if the Universal is a kind of Individual. Both of these one-sided determinations are resolved in the Disjunctive Judgment, wherein the Individual is a Universal that divides into the completed set of particular kinds.

```
Judgments of the Concept 

	Assertoric Judgment: This I is P/U

	Problematic Judgment: This I is not P/U

	Apodictic Judgment: This I, given P, is U
```

An Assertoric Judgment evaluates how close or distant a member of a category (Individual) is to the category itself (the Particular Universal). The subject is an individual member of a class (noun) whereas the predicate expresses the property (adjective) which makes the subject a member of said class.

Some examples of an assertoric judgments would be “This house is bad.” or “That guy is funny.” If the house was cluttered and lacked running water, that would distinguishes that house from good houses which would have these qualities. Therefore, what makes the house bad is its distance from its ideal (however, it is unnecessary to include this information in the judgment for it to be assertoric). For some guy to be funny, the notion proof of his humorousness would be in his telling of jokes that are funny, and so on.

A Problematic Judgment is the negation of the Assertoric Judgment, whether the instance is a member of the category or not is not determined. What is expressed by this judgment is whether the subject is what the predicate says it is or not. The house could be bad, or it might not, but it cannot be both.

The Assertoric and Problematic Judgments are both declarative statements which express the relation between subject and predicate, but neither can justify the relation. New information is needed to substantiate this absence, which is where the Apodeictic Judgment comes in. The subject and predicate of the original statement are each split in two, containing within themselves their own subject and predicate: This house is cluttered, cluttered houses are bad, so this house is bad.

Because we have internally divided subject and its predicate, we can no longer say that we have the same judgment we started with, but something entirely new. This new sentence is a syllogism.


## Syllogism

```
Syllogisms of Inherence

	First Figure: I → P → U

	Second Figure: P → I → U

	Third Figure: I → U → P
```

The Syllogisms of Inherence display the three possible forms that a syllogism can take. 

In the First Figure, the Individual is identified as an instance of a Universal because of a Particular quality that it has. 

In the Second Figure, the Particular is identified as a quality of a Universal because of an existing Individual.

In the Third Figure, the Individual is identified as having a Particular quality because are both are characterized by the same Universal.


```
Syllogisms of Reflection

	Allness: I → P[I → U] → U

	Induction: U → I[U → P] → P[U → I]

	Analogy: P[U → I] → U[P → I] → I[P → U]
```

The Syllogism of Allness relates the Individual to the Universal through a Particular that reflects the content of both. The Particular reflects the content of both in being a Universal characteristic of the Individual. Since the Particular is what characterizes all Individuals, the connection of the Individual to the Universal is no longer arbitrary (unlike the First Figure). The Individuals are members of a Universal class on the basis of a Particular characteristic that all members of that class share. Although class membership indicates that the Particular is necessary because it is shared by all Individuals of that Universal class, this is only the case because the class itself has already been defined that Particular characteristic. In other words, the premise of the syllogism presupposes its conclusion. This syllogism depends on whether or not all Individuals in which the Particular inheres have actually been found, the premise of which was determined by induction.

The Syllogism of Induction relates the Universal and Parituclar through the mediation of the Individual(s). Induction looks for Individuals that reflect the character of both the Universal and the Particular (e.g. proving that metals (P) conduct electricity (U) by looking for different kinds of metals (I)). However, a Syllogism of Induction can never be completed because reflective thinking cannot determine whether or not it has found all Individual examples. What reflective thinking can determine, though, is that the Individuals it has observed so far exhibit a common pattern or a Universality that reflects the character of the Individual and Particular. 

The Syllogism of Analogy relates the Individual and Particular by generalizing their relation to a shared Universal. The basic form of Analogy is in the identification of a Particular characteristic that the Individuals have in common and because they have that characteristic in common, they belong to the same Universal. But, a Syllogism of Analogy, while formally conclusive, cannot say whether the characteristics it has chosen to generalize are necessary to the Universal itself (e.g. cats have tails, but the characteristic of having a tail is not unique to cats - lizards have tails too.). A Syllogism of Analogy can only arrive at a necessary conclusion if the Particular characteristic determines the character of the Individuals, not the other way around.

```
Syllogisms of Necessity
	
	Categorical: I[P → U] → P[I → U] → U[I → P]

	Hypothetical: U[I → P] → I[U → P] → P[U → I] 

	Disjunctive:  U[P → I] ( P[U → I] ( I[U → P] ) )
```

The Categorical Syllogism captures what the Syllogism of Analogy was missing, that is, the Individual and Universal are necessarily linked through the Particular because the Particular *is* Universal to Individuals. The Particular is necessarily Universal because it defines the character of every Individual. At the same time, Particularity is also necessarily Individual because one characteristic was selected out of other characteristics to determine what the Universal is as a category. 

If the Categorical Syllogism derived the necessity of the Particular as Universal to a given Individual, then the Hypothetical Syllogism shows that the condition for this necessity is the conditional existence of that Individual. If an Individual of that Universal exists, then the Universal necessarily divides into Particulars, which are Universal to Individuals. If the circle is already drawn, then the first point you started with does not matter, for the circle has no beginning or end.

Finally, the Disjunctive Syllogism unites the Universal, Particular and Individual by way of logical disjunction. The Particular is the disjoint sum of its Individuals, and the Universal is the disjoint sum of its Particulars, so the Universal is also the disjoint sum of Individuals. The Universal determined itself by determining the Particular and the Individual from within itself.

Every syllogism up to this point had an irreflexive second premise, but in the Disjunctive Syllogism, because the first premise is a disjoint sum that includes the second premise, the second premise and the conclusion are reflexive.
