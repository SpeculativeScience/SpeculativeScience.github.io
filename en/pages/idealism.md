# Absolute Idealism

The Absolute Idea is the all-encompassing, unconditioned universal which descriptively contains all ideal moments and is only that which is real. All of reality forms a self-comprised whole because there is nothing that exists outside of reality of which reality can be comprised. This unified whole of thought is necessarily discovered in and through the activity of thinking itself. Thus the reality of thought is shown, or is proven to be, as being a logically necessary moment in the development of the Absolute. These conceptual moments can only be contained within and derived from thought because if it were outside the bounds of thought, it would be unintelligable. What enables the derivation of one concept to the next is the speculative method itself, which shows that each concept is itself and its opposite, sublating themselves into a new concept that subsumes them both. This process continues until the result is the complete system itself, which has encompassed all of its concepts and has nothing left to subsume.

Hegel divides his system into three parts – Logic, Nature and Spirit. His Encyclopedia of the Philosophical Sciences, or the Encyclopedia for short, is his most comprehensive work that explicates each category in the Encyclopedia Logic (EL), Philosophy of Nature (PoN) and Philosophy of Spirit (PoG), respectively. It should be stated, however, that the Encyclopedia is largely a summarized and simplified version of existing works and lectures, such as the Science of Logic (SoL) and Lectures on the History of Philosophy (LHoP). 

Logic for Hegel is not what is often described in contemporary philosophy as formal logic, rather, he is principally concerned with the derivation and conceptual development of logical meanings. For this reason, the subject matter of the Logic is itself internally classified between as objective logic and subjective logic.  The Absolute Idea for Hegel is the totality of all logical meanings, the complete development that has taken place throughout the entirety of the Science of Logic. However, the system does not end here, as the Absolute Idea becomes other to itself as Nature.

Nature in Hegel’s system begins with the most universal concepts of space, time and motion, which develops into more determinate, particular concepts of physics, chemistry and inorganic processes, and concludes with describing how these processes are made purposive / teleological in the organic systems of the Earth, plants and animals. At the very end of this development we arrive at the human being, the self-determining and self-conscious organism that is free from the limitations of Nature.

This brings us to the third section of Hegel’s system, Spirit. In the original German, the actual word that Hegel uses is Geist, which does not have a straightforward, singular translation to any other word in English. The closest words in English are Spirit and Mind, although the former is more accurate since mind carries the connotation that it is only about finite minds, which is only one third of the Philosophy of Spirit as a whole.
