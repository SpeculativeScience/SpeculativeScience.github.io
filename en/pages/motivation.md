# Motivation

Note: This webpage is a direct translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Anthropology and Psychology, Vol. III* (1840) 

Since the natural will has not yet attained its concept, to know itself as an individual and at the same time recognize its destiny for universality, it actively strives to make the contradiction of its nature and destiny disappear. Theory, on the other hand, does not proceed from contradiction, but is identical with the object from beginning to end. Humans are born helpless in nature, unable to be content with the expression of nature, whereas for animals, nature provides them what humans must first acquire from it through labor. This concept of the natural will, not yet adequately realized in existence, is the ought. Precisely because the natural will is only an ought, it has not yet abandoned the immediacy of its natural existence in order to correspond to its concept. Kant and Fichte therefore rightly established the ought as the foundation of the practical, and the former thus expressed the moral obligation as the categorical imperative. It is only wrong to persist with the ought as if it were the final determination because it permanently strives towards its goal without ever reaching it.

The division of the natural will is now this: it first seeks to become aware of its contradiction in emotion, it finds itself as this duality, as immediately determining and determined by what it feels. The natural will then strives to escape this subjective contradiction by making it objective, to address the reality of its condition by realizing the ought through its own activity. This is the drive, the motivation for the action in which the subject finds its ought confronting itself as an object. That the will, as the abstract universal ought, completely identifies itself with the object of an impulse, is completely absorbed by what drives it to act and enters into such dependence on it that it becomes a passion. The three moments of the natural will correspond precisely to the three stages of sensibility: sensation, intuition, and representation.

## Emotion

The diversity of emotions does not depend solely on their content, even the merely formal feeling of the harmony or disharmony of my condition and my ought gives rise to manifold modifications, which here forms the system of emotions. The emotion exists as internal determinations within me, originates from a real object as its cause, and is finally related to an intrinsically independent objective that is also equally subjective, since it is the subject that applies the objective standard of the good to themselves.

### Experiential Emotions

Pleasure and displeasure, as the simplest internal determinations, do not originate from space, but rather belong exclusively to time, as the domain into which the mind reflects on itself. If something too hot causes me displeasure, the sensation certainly belongs to my spatial being, but this content is the emotion, which consists solely in the affection of the natural will. Because the mind is engaged in a constant change of its states, the feelings of pleasure and displeasure also fall into time, and since the moments of time are past, present, and future, pleasure and displeasure must be divided according to the three dimensions of time.

#### Pleasure / Displeasure (Present)

The pleasant and the unpleasant, to the extent they are concentrated on this moment of the present, we call pleasure and pain, the immediate feeling of satisfaction and dissatisfaction, which do not extend beyond the duration of their present existence. When pleasure vanishes, it leaves behind an unpleasant feeling of weariness, disgust, and exhaustion. Likewise, when pain vanishes, it leaves behind a pleasant feeling of having overcome it. Pleasure and pain, as merely sensual, only ever fill the moment when each of feeling turns into its opposite immediately afterwards. But even if pleasure and pain initially only apply to sensory affliction, one can also speak of pleasure or pain that is caused by prior pleasure or pain. These can indeed occupy the moment of the present, but they are not limited to it, rather, even if it has vanished in its immediate existence, it still persists in its consequences.

#### Gratitude / Regret / Nostalgia (Past)

Since pleasure and pain also relate to the past, we have now progressed to the second type of experiential emotions. Man is not merely a sensory being, like the animal, which is aware of itself only in the present, and therefore lives only in it, and enjoys nothing other than the present moment. Man, by contrast, living as self-conscious spirit, experiences all dimensions of time. The present also relates to the other two dimensions of time and vice versa. Thus, man experiences past and future pleasure in the present, yet even here the feeling remains entirely contained within itself, without relating to an object as its cause. Indeed, with pleasure and pain of the past, this is almost even more the case than with the feelings of the present moment. For past pleasure and displeasure exist only in the soul, and thereby sever all external connection.

If we retain a past pleasure within us, which is the pleasure intuited from a prior sensory experience, then this feeling is gratitude. It is the pleasure we feel about a pleasure we have already experienced, without excluding the possibility that it can relate to the present enjoyment of pleasure. In gratitude, past and present are related to each other, and since the actual realm of sensory pleasure is the present, gratitude can itself be a source of sensory pleasure.

The displeasure I feel about a past pain, which still persists in its consequences, is the feeling of regret. This is a sadness that exists as a lasting trace of past misfortune. If regret combines with sensual displeasure, as the spirit so often acts pathognomically on the body, then we have melancholy, which having depressed the vitality of the body, also acts back on the spirit. Thus, body and soul stand in constant interplay, both with regard to pleasure and displeasure.

By concentrating several states in this way into an individual moment, past pleasure can be linked with present displeasure, or past displeasure with present pleasure. This mixture of gratitude and regret is what we call nostalgia. The feeling of nostalgia belongs especially to the mind because it likewise expresses an inherent totality of opposing, calmly coexisting feelings. Nostalgia is no longer an animalistic feeling, but is a purely human feeling, because only humans can combine such opposites within themselves. Simple sensual affections, as opposites, cannot be simultaneously one, as they would dissolve into nothing.

#### Hope / Fear / Anticipation (Future)

Finally, pleasure and displeasure must also be related to the future. The pleasure conceived as a future pleasure is present in this conception because man lives not only in the past, but also in the future, always seeking to transcend it with the present. This pleasant feeling about a future pleasure is hope, whereas fear, on the other hand, is displeasure about future displeasure. If this pleasure for future pleasure is intensified in the present, such that it acquires certainty about the future, then hope becomes conviction, just as panic is the excess of fear that intensifies to the certainty of present displeasure. But this connection of present to future in the feeling of pleasure and displeasure is itself only a subjective determination that lacks an external basis, so these feelings exist only for the mind. But if either of these feelings are completely transformed into bodily embodiment, then we have anticipation. 

### Attitudinal Emotions

By relating the mind's desire to the future, it relates to something other than itself, for the future does not belong to us as the past and the present do. The mind thus distinguishes itself from the object that it derives pleasure from, thereby reaching the second class of emotions, the emotions related to external objects. Initially, the will was only focused on itself and on the theoretical perception of its empirical state within it, even though the perception as such was always produced by an external object. Now, the empirical aspect, the measured quantity which I compare with my inner self, is the external object itself. Depending on whether this inner self harmonizes with the object or not, it produces in me the feeling of pleasure or displeasure. First, the subject relates only generally to its object, secondly to its individual states and finally both relate to each other in terms of their reciprocal influence.

#### Love / Hate

In the case of pleasure or displeasure related to an external object, subject and object initially enter into a substantial relationship to one another. Each is a static substance that, according to the totality of their being, passively relate to one another.

If an object agrees with me, it resonates harmoniously within me as continuity of my own being, and in it I find no resistance, but only likeness. The extension of my self to the other is love, and it is the pleasant feeling of unity of the self with the other. This expression often applies to the relationship of one person to another, but it can be taken in a general sense where it also applies to things, such as when a person says that they love wine. Spinoza quite rightly defines love as joy accompanied by the idea of ​​an external cause.

On the other hand, if I feel myself discordant due to an external object, such that I perceive it as the limit, restriction, and narrowing of my being, then this is hatred, or as Spinoza defines it, sadness accompanied by the idea of ​​an external cause. Hatred, he later remarks, can never be good because it makes us feel the limitation of finitude. Love, on the other hand, is of a divine nature because it strives for the unity of beings, which is perfected in the bosom of the divine being.

Since the basis of the affections of love and hatred are in myself and the other, these feelings are called sympathy and antipathy. Every feeling love or hatred must have this basis, for in order to move towards or away from another being, I am always comparing their character against my own. In sympathy and antipathy, love and hate appear as groundless feelings, even prejudices, because I am not yet able to account for the agreement or discord between by character and the character of the other.

#### Sympathy / Antipathy

If I now reflect on the equality or inequality of my affectations with those of the object, the original harmony or disharmony of the subject and object remains the basis of hatred and love. However, since I now relate to the static substance of my object not only as a perceiving subject, but also to all newly added determinations of each side as a mutable entity, in which the same changes are brought about by the other, we have the dynamic relationship of these feelings. These changes are different states of pleasure and displeasure that each experiences, and each, as both subject and object, feels the pleasure or displeasure of the other and knows that its state is brought about by the other. This relationship can only exist between spirits, for only among them is this reciprocity of knowledge present.

In sympathy, both individuals have similar needs, affections, and dispositions, and this is especially true for love, as two people are united as one. Since every state of one affects the other in the corresponding way, the change in pleasure and displeasure that is felt by one necessarily extends to the feelings of the other. Because of sympathy, in love the same feeling will awaken the same feeling, one person's pleasure at the pleasure of another is shared joy, one person's displeasure at the displeasure of another is pity.

In antipathy, every feeling of one causes the opposite in the other. The pleasure that the hater feels towards the displeasure of the hated person is mockery. For the fact that the hated person, i.e. that which is negative to the subject and inhibits it, is negated or inhibited must be agreeable to the hater since the negation of the negative is the restored affirmation. Conversely, the pleasure of the hated person will increase the displeasure of the other, resulting in the hater to feel envy or jealousy, especially if the hated person achieves something that has eluded the hater and for which they also strove for. Thus, envy is as unsuitable to God as hatred, because envy presupposes that one is deprived of an advantage, but God does have such a need to begin with. If one of two people gains an advantage, it will depend on whether the other's displeasure at not having received it outweighs the shared joy. If this happens, then the person may feel resentful and envious towards them.

#### Compassion / Revenge

Just as the spirit finds itself determined by the external object of its love and hate, so too does it want to enter into a conscious causal relationship with it, without merely following its changes passively or unconsciously acting upon it. It therefore seeks not only to appear as the effect of the other's emotional state, but also to make itself the conscious originator of them, according to what it receives from the other. As the spirit finds itself determined by the other, it also wants to react to it. This relationship is reciprocal, so that both are in interaction with each other.

Each person strives to bring forth pleasure in the one who first bestowed it upon them, and this striving is itself the pleasant feeling of favor. By contrast, the unpleasant feeling associated with the spirit's desire to generate displeasure in the object of attachment is resentment and envy. The double negation contained in this feeling will turn positive again. For if resentment has achieved its purpose, and I have inflicted harm on the other because they inflicted harm on me, then my pain, by having projected itself onto the other, is paralyzed within me, and the resentment is dampened by mockery, in that the displeasure I have generated in the hated object balances the displeasure I felt earlier. From the negation of the negation of pain brings a new pleasure, the self-cancellation of disharmony brings forth a new harmony. But since the element of resentment still lingers within this new mixed feeling, this is the feeling of revenge. Revenge strives to repay the other for the pain caused to them, it desires retribution, that the other suffers according to what they have suffered. Through this negative equality of individuals, revenge begins with a connection to a higher ought or norm for action.

But since the offended party avenges the offense done to them by inflicting pain against the offender, the one against whom they have taken action now becomes the offended party and likewise seeks to avenge the offense done to them. Thus, we have an endless progression of revenge in this interaction of two individuals against one another. The immediate feeling of pain is the standard for each person according to which they inflict a new harm on the other. For revenge, retaliation has this character of being another evil, but punishment does not. For the indeterminacy of the standard applied by revenge is precisely what generated this progression into infinity, nurturing evil instead of eliminating it. At the same time, everyone should only receive as much as they deserve, for revenge is one-sided justice. Thus, the higher obligation of the norm has revealed itself as the inner standard that everyone now applies to the external condition of themselves and others.

### Normative Emotions

The comparison of the empirical state with the ideal state of what ought to be gives rise to the third class of emotions. Since now the self consider the other as an equal and wants to bring about in them the same effect that they themselves have received from them, a common standard or universal principle of judgment is established for all. The subject now relates itself and others to this absolute concept of what ought to be, not to particular subjective interests. By comparing the actual state of each individual with the desired or required state, the appropriateness or inappropriateness of the two to each other becomes a new source of pleasure and displeasure.

#### Honor / Shame

This comparison is initially limited to the individual subject. The pleasure derived from conforming to my own concept of myself is honor, in which the subject is satisfied with themselves in deriving enjoyment from their own activity. Shame, on the other hand, is the sadness that arises from the realization that this inner harmony between the is and ought-to-be is absent within me. If this self-disapproval continues to the point where hope disappears, where the inadequacy is seen as insurmountable and shame combines with despair, then we have the feeling of dejection. Finally, if this displeasure is joined by the serious resolve to transform the inadequacy into adequacy, and the confidence that this undertaking will succeed, then the mixed feeling of remorse arises. In remorse, the person tries to attain inner contentment with themselves by means of shame, whereas the shameless person will not easily experience remorse.

#### Admiration / Contempt

Similarly, the individual subject relates their inner sense of obligation to another person and can now judge this person, as it previously judged itself. This is due to the objective nature of obligation, which everyone possesses within themselves and is thereby the same for all. The pleasant feeling that the other person conforms to the demands of this inherent standard is admiration and the unpleasant feeling that they do not conform to it is contempt. Here, the relationship to the object is also re-established. Admiration is love, but not all love is admiration, for love can also have a basis in self-interest. Admiration is love because I admire what I feel to be good in itself, not merely because it serves my personal interest. True love is therefore not without admiration, and admiration can only be felt for what is truly good. 

#### Confidence / Emulation

Finally, the subject compares its judgment of itself with what it has judged about the other. If in this comparison of the two, the one making the comparison believes itself to be in accordance with its own concept but finds the other to be inconsistent with it, then the mixed feeling of confidence arises from the pleasant feeling of satisfaction with oneself and the unpleasant feeling of contempt for the other. Confidence is noble and just if the subject is not mistaken in its judgment or overestimate itself, but it becomes arrogance and conceit if the result of the comparison does not correspond to the truth. Finally, confidence becomes vanity if it takes pride in the superficial and appearing impressive to others, such as in clothing, beauty, etc. Vanity is therefore the attempt to drag confidence back down into the domain of material obligation. If, on the contrary, the one making the comparison finds themselves inconsistent with its concept and the other consistent with it, then the equally pleasant and unpleasant feeling of emulation arises, which, strictly speaking, has three elements: shame about oneself, admiration for the other, and the desire to transform shame into self-confidence. Thus, emulation begins the transition to action and to drive, whereas the emotion, because of its connection with the cognitive faculty, remained internal. By the fact that the obligation existing in and for itself has come to consciousness, its empirical state is posited as one that ought not to be. 

## Drive

The disharmony into which spirit is plunged appears as something pre-existing, in the form of an external object that contradicts it, and through whose negation it seeks to produce the desired harmony. This active feeling, which acts upon the object, seeking pleasure in its negation and thus being driven against it, is the drive whose concept we must now define. Because spirit is no longer its emotions, but has attained consciousness of its absolute obligation, its immediate determination no longer satisfies it. Spirit is driven to transcend itself, but at the same time returns to itself by placing itself in the place of the negated object. Since it is driven by the feeling of its inadequacy to overcome its present state, it must already know the what state is adequate to it, otherwise it could not make it the goal of its activity. The drive thus unites two states within itself: first, the unpleasant feeling of the present lack, and then the hope for the pleasant feeling of satisfaction. The drive, because of this twofold nature, is more concrete than emotion. The emotion, as a simple entity, has either pleasure or displeasure as its content, alternating between these two states, and even where it sometimes combined them, the mixed feeling as such was never twofold, since the opposed feelings had merged into a completely new, internally homogeneous feeling. In drive, however, this duality appears as an actual duality, and thus as a contradiction, which is precisely what drives the drive to action, whereas the internally simple emotions still lacked this impetus. The inner tension of spirit, which manifests itself in the drive as such, is now repeated in its active relationship to the object. And since the drive strives to overcome the dichotomy between subject and object, it is attracted to those objects that are capable of producing this harmony and is repelled by those that would disrupt it. Every drive therefore has an opposing counter-drive.

The system of drives must derive its classification from the general concept of the drive. Since in the drive, the determination is no longer a given one as in emotion, but one to be produced by spirit in that the will seeks to transform the immediate determination of the given object into its own. Thus, in the drive, practical spirit has progressed from consciousness to self-consciousness, and the opposition of both sides is present, the drive is therefore practical intuition. The other to which self-consciousness is related is initially an external, sensory object, to which the will develops the personal drives at the expense of the object manifesting the object's lack of selfhood. Through this negation, self-consciousness makes consciousness conform to itself, so now self-consciousness relates itself to another self-consciousness, which is still likewise differentiated into the social drives. As the identity of self-consciousnesses finally emerges from this process, the relationship ceases to be a relationship of external objects, objectivity appears simultaneously as the inner substance of spirit, and self-consciousness has become universal self-consciousness in the rational drive.

### Personal Drives

In the personal drives, practical self-consciousness knows itself to be absolute in relation to the objects opposing it. All personal drives are therefore aimed at self-preservation, which is the first way in which the mind, as the will, expresses its superiority over outer nature. To preserve oneself is thus the first natural drive of every human being.

#### Drive for Enjoyment

The sensible objects, which are unable to resist the power of self-consciousness, are eliminated by the mind in their isolation, identified with itself, and are thereby made objective by finding itself in them. This first moment of the personal drives is the pursuit of pleasure and the avoidance of displeasure. We seek what will satisfy our immediate needs of hunger, thirst, etc., and flee from what would damage our health and cause us displeasure. Since the achieved satisfaction is at the same time again a theoretical sensation, the question arises as to how the feeling arising from the drive relates to the five senses. The theoretical senses, sight and hearing, also provide pleasant and unpleasant sensations, but in this respect they do not rise above emotion, they remain enclosed in its inner determination without going much beyond intuition. To the drives, on the other hand, belong the practical senses, namely smell and taste, which then reduce themselves again to feeling as the universal, for smell and taste are only pleasant or unpleasant sensations of the sense of touch insofar as it is related to a specific organ. They ensure the self-preservation of the individual: the tongue through the desire for drink and food, the sense of smell as guardian of taste, but the sensation of the sense of touch contained within them is the stimulus that continually rekindles desire. Thus, it only becomes clear from this practical relationship why the sense of touch has also become part of the general name of feeling. To seek pleasure in these sensations, on the one hand, seems to be only a means to self-preservation, but on on the other hand, it is also the entire content of this drive and its ultimate purpose.

#### Drive for Possession

This first way of realizing self-preservation is still the most imperfect because the satisfaction, being entirely individual, is limited to the present moment. The purely sensory drive is subject to constant change, desire gives way to enjoyment, and enjoyment gives way to desire again. However, spirit does not seek to alienate itself from the object in the moment of acquiring it, but to maintain itself with it in a lasting way. This second stage of self-preservation is the drive for possession. In possession, even though I negate the object by using it for myself, not all objects are immediately consumed after they are used. Some objects can be preserved by being maintained, thereby guaranteeing my drive lasting satisfaction. Possession is therefore not merely individual pleasure, but the general possibility of satisfying my needs and providing myself with enjoyment. Forethought to procure possessions for later enjoyment lies the character of universality that distinguishes man from the animal. For if pleasure is the means of self-preservation, then possession is again the condition that makes the satisfaction of both possible. Yet, if possession is made an end in itself, it becomes an evil, as can be seen in avarice. To elevate possession to an ultimate end is even worse than seeking it in pleasure, for pleasure is desirable in itself, but possession is only ever desirable through the way I use it. However, the value of possession is higher in this respect than that of pleasure, because it represents a lasting objectification of spirit, a lasting expansion and extension of its existence in the thing possessed by it, whereas in pleasure this realization was momentary and transient.

#### Drive for Honor

The lasting aspect of objectivity that the will has attained in control, is itself still a sensual one, although as a universal it is also spiritual. The striving for pure spiritual self-preservation is now the drive for honor. In honor, the person is preserved even after the demise of their entire existence, whereas control only anticipates it. Montesquieu explicitly identifies the desire for peace with the instinct of self-preservation: "We expand our unity when we can transfer it into the memory of others. It is a new life that I acquire, and that becomes just as dear to us as the natural one." As intellectual self-love, honor is a refined selfishness, just people who live only for the momentary present remain insensitive to it. At first glance, honor seems to be dependent on the opinions of others, giving it the appearance of contingency. But we must distinguish the opinion of the moment from true public opinion from the judgment of history, which is made necessary by becoming history and so is no longer subject to change. The noblest who strive for honor therefore seek in it only its lasting quality, that they will be remembered for the good they have done in this life. With this refinement of the personal drives, however, the selfish element in it is simultaneously abolished. This is because honor cannot be realized without self-consciousnesses recognizing another self-consciousness, which directly implies the existence of the social drive.

### Social Drives

In the social drives, the will does not merely desire an object, it desires the desire of another equally independent will. By realizing its self-interest in an object that is also in the interest of another, the will transcends its natural existence as spirit. 

#### Drive for Recognition

The first form of sociability is the receptiveness of self-consciousness to the impressions of another. The individual's heart expands as they become susceptible to being moved by others. This susceptibility, however, initially appears only as a pure emotion, as an inner determination of the one being moved and without any drive towards sociability. This motivation can itself be twofold, depending on whether the one who moves me directs their selfishness towards me or not. The reaction against this selfishness, against the obstacle that I find in the will of the other, is anger. It might seem surprising that anger as the first of the social drives, but anger is even less of a personal drive, it does not at all relate to the self-interested will who seeks pleasure, possessions or honor. On the contrary, anger fights against the abstract self-centeredness that seeks to negate the other, striving to restore the violated equality of the will. The angry person demands that the other should acknowledge them, although this acknowledgment, of course, is only a demand. Therefore, in anger, sociability is indeed present, but only as the demand for sociability. 

The positive result of acknowledgment, on the other hand, is benevolence, in which I make the other, like myself, an end in itself. But even benevolence, like anger, is always only sociability in its potential form. Benevolence is still a one-sided form of social interaction, one that is not yet reciprocated by the other person and lacks the realization that can only be achieved through mutuality. Benevolence can even be felt towards another person even without that person knowing it and without having received assurance of reciprocal affection.

#### Drive for Friendship

The mutual realization of goodwill between two people is friendship. Friendship is no longer, like goodwill, a one-sided feeling that the other person doesn't even need to share, but it is a feeling that is based on the reciprocity between two people who are well-disposed towards each other. The pursuit of pleasure, in which each provides for the other, thus gives rise to many more purposes here. Friendship, especially when it is formed in youth, is as fickle and changeable as pleasure itself. When two friends direct their goodwill towards the mutual sharing of possessions, then this is a friendship of convenience. However, because utility is not the end, but only the means to achieve an end, one is not acknowledging the other here for their own sake, only for the sake of their own ambitions. But if mutual goodwill makes the self of the other as its purpose, then this is true friendship. A friendship that has its basis in any other end, such as enjoyment or possessions, is not true friendship. Since each truly has the self of the other as its end in friendship, it is one spirit in two bodies, an I that is We and a We that is I. This equality, when one no longer looks at the particularity of the two friends, but practices the principle of sociability towards all people, is the drive for justice.

#### Drive for Justice

The drive for justice demands not only that one person provide another with what that other person provides for them, to place this one person on equal footing with the other, but desires this for all people. Goodwill is extended from the two individuals to all of humanity, but this simultaneously makes the standard of judgment entirely objective, so that it no longer rests on personal feelings, but on the reason of the matter. I consider all people equal not only because I harbor the same subjective goodwill towards all, but also because equality that I want to realize is something objective. The drive for justice therefore demands that each person be treated as they themselves have acted, or expressed negatively, to not do to another what you do not want done to yourself. Equality is not violated when one person accomplished more than the other, but it is violated, however, when unequal services follow equal accomplishments, or vice versa. Against the violator of this equality, the sense of justice then becomes something negative, the wrath of justice; and that is the drive for revenge, which, in order to restore equality, returns this inequality to the one who performed unequally. Whoever turns their selfishness against another will also be repaid by a violation of this selfishness. In this way, the harmony of all spirits is indeed established in the drive for justice, but only from case to case. The fact that harmony in general is striven for not only among all spirits, but also with the entire universe, is the rational drive, where knowledge itself becomes the object of desire.

### Rational Drives

The first of the rational drives we can call the pure drive for knowledge, which desires only theory as such. But since the practical side is always also contained in this desire, secondly, knowledge is applied to it because will wants to act only in accordance with knowledge. From this follows, thirdly, the highest of the rational drives, in which knowledge and action, theory and practice, are brought into perfect harmony in the drive for improvement.

#### Drive for Knowledge

If the drive is, in essence, the striving to bring the self and the world into harmony, then this appears to be most effectively achieved in the drive for knowledge. We can call it the noblest of drives, the primal drive, to the extent that in it the drive has fulfilled its pure concept and that reality corresponds to this concept. The curiosity of children is the first manifestation of this drive and philosophy its last and highest culmination. Hence Aristotle begins his Metaphysics with the words: "All men by nature desire to know." This drive is the perfect balance of selfishness and sociability are, they no longer interfere with each other, nor do they give anything up when they seek to harmonize. The knowledge of one person, far from hindering that of another, actually promotes it, for we learn by teaching. Therefore, what is true about love also applies to knowledge, in that the more that I give, the more that I have.

This drive, however, does have one drawback, namely, that it is only theoretical and not practical. It can, of course, become practical if it is a source of enjoyment or a means of earning a living, it provides sustenance, but these purposes are only secondary to it. In itself, knowledge is only for the sake of knowledge, and as also the purpose of philosophy, which makes it a free pursuit, is then the actual core and essence of philosophy. In it, the practical aspect of the drive, which is concerned with the individual, is entirely elevated into universality and has become pure theory.

#### Drive for Practice

Since the highest thing is that the particular itself flows from the universal, the drive for knowledge must also become the determining factor in action. In our practical behavior, we do not want to follow merely the blind chance of mere impulses or feelings, we strive to act based on knowledge. But here it is again only the formal aspect that interests us, not yet the moral content. In fact, even the worst purposes can be carried out with perfect knowledge of both what is intended and the appropriate means to achieve the goal.

#### Drive for Improvement

But whoever acts from knowledge will not allow themselves to be determined solely by pleasure or displeasure, or to satisfy this or that drive by chance. They evaluate which drives can be best satisfied at any given time, and thus prioritize them in turn so they can do justice to each. Since the will is not an individual drive, but is totality of them, the person acting from knowledge will want to satisfy them all. In place of the harmony of subject and object, which relates only to a single drive, the will strives to harmonize of all of its drives with itself. This is the drive for improvement, which can be called the primal drive in the truest sense of the word. However, since it is beyond the capacity of the will in general to satisfy its drives all at once or even one after another, they must constantly limit themselves to one or another individual drive, and then to also elevate this drive to the guiding principle of their actions as the passion that strives for harmony with all the other drives.

## Passions

A drive becomes a passion when the subject is no longer the active, controlling principle over the object, but is, on the contrary, filled with a specific content which leaves it no independence and makes it a passive recipient. Even if the struggle of the spirit against displeasure has ceased, and the spirit has found its pleasure in the passion, this by no means entitles us to see freedom in it. On the contrary, the nature of the drive is most clearly revealed in passion, that the actions of man are determined by a principle that is alien to him. For the entire scope of his activity is so completely absorbed by the object of the dominant passion that it becomes the universal maxim of his entire conduct. In the dominion of passion, it is always a deficiency and a dependence when we sacrifice all other aspects of our existence to this passion and know no other striving than this one.

At first glance, passion seems to be a lower stage than that of emotions or drives because, having surrendered to the dominance of a singlular drive, the will is now ensnared in a state of dependence while intending to advance on the path of freedom. But at the same time, the domination of the will by one drive simultaneously liberates the will from all other drives. Passion, in turn, is only a psychological mold into which any content can be poured. Passion no longer has desires, but intense inclinations to which the will surrenders with the full energy of its spirit. However strange it may sound, passion is the first step towards freedom in that the will has the agency to pursue this or that inclination. Since passion, however, has the same content as the drives, the classification of instincts also applies to the passions. Just as before, the passions are divided into obsession (personal), love (social), and rational (respect).

### Obsession

Every obsession is always directed toward the pursuit of pleasure. For even when one is obsessed with increasing their possessions and utility, it is ultimately for the sake of enjoyment. Even the noble pursuit of honor is at its core an obsession with intellectual self-gratification. Since the pursuit of pleasure is the goal of this passion, it will seek to transform pleasure its isolation from momentary to lasting enjoyment. The generalization of the present moment, however, is also its extension to all dimensions of time. The pleasure-seeker will therefore, firstly, exploit the present moment in its fleetingness, for pleasure is the positive aspect of the present just as pain is its negative aspect. In their pursuit of pleasure, however, they will secondly feel joy, or pleasure about past pleasure, just as past pain brings them sadness. And this will, thirdly, also be the case with the pleasure and pain about the future. The mind is so preoccupied with the pursuit of pleasure that it anticipates future pleasure in hope, and anticipates future pain in fear. If hope projects its certainty of the future onto the present then it is called confidence, while fear in this case becomes anxiety.

### Love

Love is the exact opposite of obsession since love has its basis in sociability whereas obsession has its basis in selfishness. Of course, pleasure is not excluded from love, but it is also a highly spiritual pleasure as it is the passion that two souls have for each other. Even if, on the other hand, we must call sensuality, in the self-awareness of honor, spiritual in nature, the fundamental difference between love and obsession remains in that for obsession, one's own self constitutes the main object of passion, and the other person or thing always remains secondary, a means to an end. In love, however, the opposite is true. Love is the pleasant feeling that arises from setting another person as one's goal, and indeed, first and foremost, dedicating themselves to the well-being of another. Spinoza, in his treatise on the passions (Ethics III, prop. 13), expressed the origin of love not from personal drives, but from social drives, by seeing its cause in the other person. The fact that the source of the passion is outside the person is the main thing here. Love is the negation of selfishness into self-sacrifice, as both Schubert and Feuerbach very well describe love as the merging, first into the substantial being of another, then into the universe, indeed as death, in which love is perfected through the surrender of one's own self. Familial love is this sacrifice for the collective person of the family, which extends to the human species as a whole. Hatred, on the other hand, is a discontent with the accompanying external cause, resulting in the antipathy of one against another rather than sympathy.

The expressions of love and hatred are also used metaphorically for feelings towards animals and inanimate things only to the extent that the passion personifies them. However, it is not only the substantial being of the beloved that produces the affections of love and hatred. The affections are modified, secondly, according to the accidental characteristics of the beloved. The joy of the beloved causes sympathy in the lover, and their sadness causes them compassion. Conversely, the hater feels the exact opposite of what the hated feels. The joy of the hated person causes envy in the hater, and his sadness causes mockery or malicious pleasure in them. The latter two are mixed feelings in that another's joy is transformed into one's own sadness, and another's sadness into one's own joy. However, the accidental characteristics are not merely passively accepted, for since love and hatred are activities, the passion continues to cause new affections in itself and in others. Thus, if the beloved or hated object was previously the cause of a passion, it now becomes the effect. In the beloved, the lover wants the beloved to feel joy, and in the hated, the hater wants the hated to feel shame, each making the accidental qualities of their own into the active substance that acts on the passive substance of the other. This gives rise to the two affections of favor and disfavor. On the negative side of the process, the lover seeks to remove or dispel sadness in the beloved, and the hater seeks to remove or dispel joy in the hated. 

### Respect

In respect, love of the self and the other no longer form a contrast because each person loves themselves in the other. However, with the mutual respect of persons for each other, it soon becomes apparent that no individual perfectly reflects themselves, but that each individual they have respect for is only more or less of a reflection of who they are. Here, there are three possible manifestations of respect. Each person either compares who they are with who they want to be, compares who they are in relation to the other, or compares themselves with the other in relation to who they want to be. In the first case, if I find myself in accordance with who I want to be, this causes me to feel self-respect, and in the opposite case, I feel remorse or shame about my inadequacy. In relation to the other, this results in the affects of admiration and contempt. But if I compare both selves with each other, then affects of a mixed nature arise once again. If I find that I correspond to who I want to be and the other does not, this results in arrogance, where the person too easily deceives themselves out of self-love. Conversely, if I recognize that the other person is fulfilling a goal that I want to achieve, this gives rise to jealousy, and to the extent that I strive to be equal them, it gives rise to emulation. Thus, we now see that passion is no longer completely absorbed by the object that drives it, rather, we see that passion relates freely to its object and reflects upon it. The natural will has thereby transformed into the reflective will.
