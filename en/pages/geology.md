# Geology

Note: This webpage is a revised translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Encyclopedia Nature* (1876) 

If we view life as the highest stage of Nature, it nevertheless constitutes, as a totality, the true *prius* of the preceding stages. These stages, as the one-sided moments of life, are its *conceptual* presuppositions. Thus, having already drawn attention earlier to the absurdity of allowing the stages of nature to fall apart in time, we must be equally careful not to elevate the conceptual distinctions between these stages into absolutes—as if each stage possessed absolutely nothing in common with one another in reality. Therein lies the error in the absolute lack of relation asserted by Schultz-Schultzenstein to exist between the inanimate and the animate—given that the inorganic is the necessary condition for the organic. The moments of the inanimate, gathered together into a unity, is precisely what constitutes the animate itself. Thus, we shall see that all those individualities appearing in
isolation in the earlier stages—such as metals, sulfur, crystals, and
salts—now become moments of the geological organism of the Earth, which presents itself as the totality that stands over and against its own distinctions.

However, the stages that are to unfold within the geological organism results spontaneously from the concept of the geological organism as outlined above. For as much as process and result still fall apart from one another, we must also conceive of them here as distinct entities. The process of the Earth—serving as the presupposition for its present configuration—constitutes the primary element of geophysics, or geology proper. The second is the static, objective result—the structure of the Earth’s rocks and minerals—constituting its morphology as studied in petrology and minerology. Finally, these opposites begin to reconcile themselves, and the Earth’s extinct life reveals traces of actual life. The process proves itself to be a phenomenon still active within its object—save that it no longer retains the full intensity of the past, but can only gradually reshape it—is the life of the Earth as revealed by climatology.

## Geophysics

When the Earth, as the living power of being, first brings the three other elements, as the differences of ground, into appearance within itself, then the light of the sun, as the abstract identity, which is absorbed by the Earth, is the instigator of this process.

### Earthquakes

First, gas and vapors collect within the Earth's interior, which pervade its interior and seek to erupt with subterranean crashes, like internal thunder, shaking and tearing the Earth apart, producing earthquakes, sinkholes, fissures, landslides, and mountain uplifts. Depending on the direction of the internal winds and vapors, the earthquakes are vertical, horizontal, or vortex-like. Hot vapors, gases, fire, smoke, and mud also erupt from the cracks in the earth, which constitutes the transition to the second moment.

### Volcanoes

The second moment of the internal process is that the internal gases ignite, and the air process transitions to the fire process. The earth begins to burn within itself and allows this fire to erupt to the surface. This is the origin of volcanoes, whose eruptions are also connected to distant, subterranean lava. However, a new volcano rarely erupts after an earthquake. The process of volcanoes, however, is a more advanced dialectic of the solid Earth, because here, through the subterranean fire, the solid loses not only its mechanical immobility, but even its aggregate state, as the molten earth mass flows out of the open crater as a glowing stream of lava. The volcano is the internal process turned outward, a reaction of the Earth's interior against the surface, for it arises only when the permanent connection between the Earth's interior and atmosphere has been severed, whereas in earthquakes, the outward eruption is more isolated. Hence the possibility of thunderstorms during a volanic eruption, in which the hot water vapor rises from the crater during the eruption and pours into the atmosphere, forming a cloud of immense surface area that produces lightning.

Volcanoes are typically either central volcanoes, like the Andes, or series volcanoes, like the series of volcanoes from New Zealand to Tierra del Fuego in the South Sea. However, there are also other types such as dome volcanoes, which arise from a single eruption or from the covering of earlier eruptions by subsequent eruptions, resulting in only a very small crater. They are formed from heavier, more fluid material than the stratified ones. The Kaimeni Islands near Santorini belong to this category. If two-thirds of the volcanoes are located on islands, and the coastal volcanoes are the more active, this is attributed less to the direct influence of the sea than to the fact that the rigid part of the Earth's crust is less thick near the sea. The volcanoes could also have turned their location into an island through their own activity.

### Hot Springs

This connection between volcanoes and water leads us to the third moment of the geothermal process, the hot spring. Since the air released by earthquakes transformed into fire, the residue of the fire process, by extinguishing, sinks to the neutrality of water. Springs erupt from the inner womb of the Earth, and hot springs with mineral content, such as salt marshes, expressly form the transition and the intermediate link between volcanoes and spring formation. Yet, one should no more conceive of springs as eruptions within the interior of ready-made water than of volcanic eruptions as products of an already ready-made fire. Even here, we see everything as being in a living process of transformation and change, and to treat this as a purely inorganic process is therefore entirely unacceptable. Even more reprehensible is the purely mechanical view, as if water did not bubble up from the interior of the Earth at all, but instead originated from rain and melted snow, which filtered through the surface of the mountains into their interior and thus allowed them to break through the side of the mountains as a spring. Equally unacceptable is the purely mechanical view of hot springs, whose temperature is often 900–970 °C, and which often erupt some distance away from any volcanoes. They are caused to rise from a such a great depth because the temperature of the earth's interior is so high there. And as for cold springs, during periods of extreme dryness and drought, the wells and springs often dry up. It therefore cannot be denied that the aforementioned mechanical process is a contributing factor to the abundance of water in the springs and wells, but it is by no means the only factor, for the earth transforms itself into this neutrality. 

## Petrology

If we have already described the processes of the Earth in the general sense, then we must now consider the result of this process in the Earth's composition. The science of the Earth's internal structure and composition is petrology, which examines how different types of rock are structured, transformed and produced by volcanic, meteorological and metamorphic activity. The internal structure of the Earth is necessarily divided into three kinds. The first is igneous rock, the product of volcanic activity. The second is sedimentary rock, the product of meteorological activity. The third is metamorphic rock, which is likewise the product of metamorphic activity. The judgments of volcanic, meteorological and metamorphic activity return to themselves in the syllogism of hydrothermal circulation, establishing the necessary conditions for life to take place.

### Igneous Rock

If igneous rock rightfully bears this name, then it is regarded by some as original form of rock that is already present in the inner form of the Earth. Since the inner form of the Earth exhibits only the corpse of life, these are not themselves in process, but simply the result of the process that is coming to a conclusion in them. In this way, the organic form of the mineral has separated into a mechanical parts in crystallization. The entire Earth is a large crystal, which breaks down into mechanical differences of spatial configurations. These, however, are here the point, the surface, and the body. 

The first form of the igneous mountain range, which we can call the core rock, forms the innermost center of all formations. This fact does not prevent it from being exposed at the highest level in the mountain ranges, although it is the lowest foundation of all strata, having pushed aside everything that lay above it through volcanic uplift, but has also often been laid over other igneous rocks. Because of this position, it is not necessary to make the oldest igneous rock the youngest product. In general it is unwise to strictly transform the spatial succession of the strata from bottom to top into a chronological sequence of older and younger formations. While it is possible for this to be the case, but it is not necessary, the sequence cannot be defined as indefinite. Furthermore, the transition from one type of stone to another is only an ideal-conceptual one, not an empirical-real one. 

The lowest igneous rock is granite, the true core of all stone formations. It is the normal stone because it is precisely in it that the totality of all spatial figurations appears in absolute fusion. In it, the pebble formation is represented, in which all three moments—the punctiform, the planar, and the crystalline — form a singlular crystal. We can then distinguish between three components of granite. The first element in granite is quartz, where silica predominates. Second, granite exhibits shiny surfaces, mica, where the clay formation protrudes. Third, within its overall crystallization, smaller crystals like feldspar appear.

Granite, which represents the clay formation within the igneous rock, extends over the granitic core, forming a lens-like surface. Situated between the first and third stages of the igneous mountain range, namely the core rock and the transitional rock, it is called the low mountain range. Instead of always lying horizontally above the core in prehistoric times, it is now often deposited in vertical or inclined layers and mountain ranges. Initially adjacent to the granitic mountain ranges, the greatest force of erosion has caused the innermost core rock to break through its envelopes, while the formerly overlying seam rock, forming even lower mountains, has been despoited further away.

The closer the formations are to the core rock, the more silica, clay, and limestone interpenetrate each other. The further they move away from it, the more they appear in separate forms. From granite, the transition proceeds through gneiss, mica, and hornblende schist to igneous or clay schist. From mica schist, where the crystalline still predominates within the shell rock itself, there is ever greater flattening. The igneous slate forms the true focal point here, whether grayer or redder, whereas the quartz becomes more transparent. The clay becomes increasingly thicker, the distinctive nature of the quartz, feldspar, mica, and hornblende formations dissolves until they become fully granulated as sediment.

### Sedimentary Rock

The deposited masses of sediment belong to the second period of Earth's transformation under which the most intense meteorological processes eroded and weathered the Earth's surface into different geological formations. The stone bodies re grounded into smaller particles and precipitated into sedimentary rock, until the further compactification of layers under increasing temperature and pressure prepares the conditions for metamorphic activity.

Conglomerate rock now opens the series of sedimentary formations as the lowest layer. The large boulders, which had detached themselves from the Earth's original crystalline needles due to the most tremendous meteorological tremors, and which can still be found in large numbers as erratic blocks everywhere on our continents, were taken from igneous rock, mixed together, and first deposited on the floor of the newly formed ocean. We must recognize in this lowest sedimentary layer, directly lying on the igneous rock, the punctual moment of these layers. For without internal crystallization and binding cohesion, only the larger mountain fragments have become a whole mechanically, even through a stony cement, but not as homogeneous surfaces, so that the parts have remained qualitatively independent.

After the storms have calmed, more gradual precipitation occurred, where not all qualitatively larger stone masses, mixed together, sank to the seabed, but, slowly ground down, settled sooner or later according to their different weights determined by their quality, the actual floe deposits. Here we find silica, clay and limestone formations, in their separation and in the order just described, settling one after the other in long stratified sheets. Only these floe deposits represent the true character of sedimentary rock, its flatness at its purest. The salts of the transitional rock are said to have mixed with these smaller fragments, thus creating the salty ocean. From the dissolved igneous rock, first silica, then clay, and finally limestone separated, they baked together to form rock, and have now been deposited layer upon layer. First sandstone beds, then clay beds, and finally limestone beds. However, one or the other of these forms is often missing, depending on the available material.

### Metamorphic Rock

Since metamorphic rock is the established totality, it thus forms the highest product of the lithic realm, where it loses its rigidity and prepares the hydrothermal conditions for formation of individual life. Chalk, as the last of the floe formations, has between itself and topsoil a multitude of layers that are generally referred to as alluvial soil. These intermediate layers here also consist of sandstone, clay, and limestone, only more loosely joined together. For example, sandstone resembles sand, while clay becomes earthier. The floe beds occupy larger areas of the earth because they were deposited beneath the ocean's surface. The alluvial soil is more strongly determined by local conditions because more continuous land had surfaced and was further eroded by flooding. 

In general, there are three kinds of metamorphic formations: slate, marble and gneiss, which are the metamorphic forms of shale, limestone and granite, respectively. In the soil of metamorphic formations, topsoil in particular, the organic groundwork has finally been laid for the nurturing of life. Marl, the last modification of silica, belongs to this transition. As the rigidity of the stone dissolves, it becomes, as a neutral product, suitable for nourishing individual life. Marl is thus extremely fertile and has historically been used as fertilizer for arable land by mixing it with the soil. While the siliceous formation, ending in marl, still remains within geology, the clay formation ends in peat bogs, which form the explicit transition from the geological organism to the vegetative one. Finally, limestone opens up to animal life, on the one hand in the coral reefs of the southern oceans, and on the other hand in the production of shellfish, so that shellfish originate and live in these upwellings. Thus we see begin to see the origins of individual life everywhere, which are now found on explicitly on Earth's surface, not merely in its hydrothermal womb.

## Climatology

### Meterology

The primary consideration here is the subjective vitality of the Earth in general. The meteorological process has now, having been incorporated by the Earth as the underlying subject of its life, is grasped as a moment of the Earth's own life-process. "Just as springs," says Hegel, "are the lungs and secretory vessels for the Earth's exhalation, so are volcanoes its liver, in that they represent this process of self-heating." Without wishing to insist upon the strict validity of such comparisons with animal life, it nonetheless remains true that "such genuine springs—those that give rise to mighty rivers like the Ganges, the Rhône, and the Rhine—possess an inner life, a striving, and a vital impulse, much like the Naiads." Volcanoes are not merely the final gasps of a life that has died out. Even now, earthquakes and volcanoes continue to elevate mountains, and islands are swallowed up, while others are uplifted from the ocean floor. The sea continues to deposit land, and floods continue to alter the Earth's contours. In the Baltic Sea, the Pomeranian coast is being eroded, while the Swedish coast is gradually rising—caught within a period of terrestrial transformation. Rivers like the Oder silt up, and from there, new torrents burst forth from the Earth's interior. 

Even the more or less perfected strivings toward individual formation—to the extent that the Earth manifests processes of heat, magnetism, and electricity—become such predicates of this universal subject within the life of the Earth. Thus, in the first instance, the cycle of the seasons—through the interplay of heat and cold—brings about a general awakening and slumber of the Earth's life as a whole.

Magnetism, specifically in its declination, subsequently manifests itself in a more particular manner as a periodic fluctuation of the Earth's magnetic field with respect to the relationship between the Earth and the Sun. For the underlying cause of the oscillation of the magnetic needle between East and West is none other than the oscillation of the Earth's magnetic field itself. Just as immense oscillations of the Earth's axis relative to the Sun's axis took place in Earth's primordial history—the result of which was the establishment of a mean position—so too do these oscillations continue to manifest themselves today within the still-trembling aftereffects of that original process, but they now appear only as gradual shifts, no longer as sudden ones. Nowadays, centuries elapse before an easterly declination gives way to a westerly one. In the year 1580, the declination was 11° 30' East, it subsequently diminished to 0° by 1666, at which point it shifted over into a westerly declination, and in 1876, it has slowly reverted to its former position. At the very least, the western maximum appears to have been reached in 1814—at 22° 34'—whereas in 1860, the deviation amounted to only 19° 40'. Specifically, every locality possesses its own unique declination. Roloff (in *Der Elektromagnetismus*, p. 26) notes that prior to 1666, the declination was easterly, whereas in 1868 "it is approximately 16° westerly and has been decreasing since 1819 (at a rate of 3.8' annually for Berlin)." Just as these variations are linked to the oscillations of the Earth's axis, so too do the annual, daily, and hourly variations depend upon the position of the Sun within the ecliptic and the Earth's revolution around the Sun. Finally, a decennial periodicity—observed in declination, inclination, and intensity alike—is argued to have its root cause in the maximum or minimum frequency of sunspots.

The meteorological process—manifesting as atmospheric electricity—reveals itself as the consummate process of the Earth's own life, generating winds, rain, lightning, and atmospheric phenomena from within itself.

### Oceanography

But just as the Earth—as the universal individual—constitutes itself as the subject of the life process, each of its predicates, in turn, become the subject of a life of its own. First and foremost, the atmosphere possesses an individual life, for not only does it undergo an alternation of contraction and expansion indicated by barometric pressure readings, but in the constant fluctuations of this process, it also manifests a daily and annual ebb and flow that is dependent upon its relationship to the Sun and the Moon.

Just as the Earth’s air—in the form of the atmosphere—exhibits a life of its own, so too does its water—in the form of the oceans. This is firstly evident in the actual ebb and flow of the tides, which are conditioned by the gravitational attraction of the Moon and the Sun—forces which produce their most powerful effects in the form of spring tides. On the other hand, the sea glows across vast expanses. Wherever a ship’s keel cleaves the waves, a luminous streak trails behind it. When analyzed, this glowing water reveals a rich fauna, yet at times, the slime itself—as well as the water flowing away from these luminous creatures—also emits light. The sea bursts forth into an infinite multitude of luminous points of life. Furthermore, during the three summer months of July, August, and September, one observes a "blooming" of the sea. It is impure, turbid, and slimy—occurring earlier in the Atlantic Ocean than in the Baltic Sea. It undergoes a transformation into both vegetative and animal forms of life, giving rise to filaments, sheet-like structures, gelatinous creatures, and the like.

Finally, the land also demonstrates this bursting forth into individual vitality. The Earth is covered everywhere with vegetation, the very rocks begin to grow moss. Fungi spring up everywhere from the earth, as do lichens and the like.

### Origin of Life

Thus, we have discovered the transition from the general life of the Earth to the individual life of the animal and the plant within the Earth itself. The Earth itself, in this context, becomes an animal and vegetative formation. However, whereas such a formation typically develops from a seed belonging to a prior individual of its own species (*omne vivum ex ovo*), the transition to this state—as a third distinct aspect—lies precisely in the existence of an intermediate stage involving hybrid forms. These arise not from individuals of their own kind, but spontaneously wihout generation or seed. This is the so-called *generatio spontanea* (or *heterogenea*), which is now largely denied, as physiologists seek to apply the maxim that "all life originates from life" with uncompromising rigor—thereby admitting only *generatio aequalis* (or *homogenea*). However, in the case of certain animal organisms, this maxim appears to be not only difficult to apply, but absolutely impossible.

This controversy is an ancient one, reaching back as far as the creation myths themselves. Anyone who maintains the creation of the first individual of a given species must, by necessity, take refuge in the concept of *generatio spontanea*. Even Moses depicts the first human couple as emerging—he from a lump of earth, and she from his rib. Empedocles derives the monsters of primordial times from the mud of the sea. Even David Strauss himself knows of no other mode of origin for the first human being. Throughout antiquity—and notably by Aristotle—the *generatio aequivoca* of many animals was widely accepted. For instance, worms and insects were believed to be generated from the decay of lower animals. Some even claimed that mice and fleas came into existence in this manner—the latter from sand and urine. This tradition persisted until the 17th century, and it was then that Redi finally demonstrated the falsity of these examples. Now, ever since Ehrenberg claimed to have discovered generation via seed and egg even in molds and infusoria—the scales of scientific inquiry have tipped entirely toward the opposite side towards the complete rejection of *generatio aequivoca*.

However, the empiricists remain uncertain of their ground. Following the lead of Müller and Budge, after presenting and critiquing the arguments for and against *generatio spontanea*, Müller settles upon the alternative view that, while organic bodies are indeed the source of all organic substance, only the desiccated germs of the simplest organic beings can be "revived anew" through hydration. "The question arises," he claims, "whether organic matter, during the decomposition of an organic body, might not also—under certain influences—generate organisms of a different kind, specifically, whether it might disintegrate into tiny living cells or be revived in the form of the lowest plants, such as mold". Budge, however, concludes his discussion of this subject with the following statement: "One may, therefore, declare it to be a highly probable law of nature that young animals emerge solely from living bodies of the same species (maternal bodies) and that no new creation of animals takes place anymore." Müller does not venture quite so far into the realm of the supernatural, instead he contents himself with the conclusion that "the phenomenon of *generatio aequivoca* eludes exact inquiry as something unproven and unprovable." Since he burdens philosophy with the task of rendering a final verdict, we—by means of a dialectical examination of the empiricists' physiological arguments—shall most willingly endeavor to fulfill this demand.

To begin, parasitic worms present a challenge to the doctrine of spontaneous generation, as they appear to render unavoidable the assumption of an original transformation of animal matter into certain specific organisms. As Budge observes: “a) Parasitic worms generally die very rapidly once they have left the body, b) their organization differs markedly from that of other animals living outside the animal body, c) parasitic worms have even been found in embryos and hens’ eggs, d) certain species occur very frequently—and sometimes exclusively—in specific animal species, and e) they are occasionally confined entirely to particular, highly isolated organs of the body—e.g., the kidneys, the muscles, or the brain, f) herbivorous animals—such as sheep—harbor parasitic worms, and not infrequently, these are found even within the brain.” Müller, however, notes: “The fact that the transmission of parasitic worms—or their germs—via food does not take place is demonstrated by animals that subsist solely on plants, yet nonetheless possess their own specific parasitic worms”. Furthermore, according to Budge, “the location of parasitic worms within a given animal does not remain constant throughout the year. During the summer months, a certain worm was found in the intestines of the codfish, yet from September through December, the very same worm was found to occur within the muscles.” From all these observations, it is inferred that entozoa arise from a process of corruption or disease within the animals and organs they inhabit—given that they are invariably found under such circumstances, and that outside of this specific biological substrate, they lose their capacity for life.

Empirical science has now attempted—by means of opposing propositions and facts—to dialectically refute these assertions and observations in order to escape the concept of *generatio aequivoca*. First and foremost, Ehrenberg succeeded in cultivating mold from seeds. Furthermore, entozoa possesses a stomach, reproductive organs, and thousands of eggs. Would such an anatomical structure not be entirely purposeless, Eschricht asks, if such creatures were capable of spontaneously generating anew from mere sludge and putrid slime? Moreover, numerous physiologists have repeatedly discovered—within the blood of both lower and higher animals—minute bodies exhibiting vigorous ciliary motion. These bodies might conceivably be interpreted as the larvae of parasitic worms—a view that is disputed by others, arguing that the eggs are far too large to enter the lymphatic vessels or to circulate within the capillaries. This notwithstanding, Ehrenberg adamantly rejects the notion that entozoa originate via *generatio spontanea*. Instead, he posits that the circulating bodily fluids transport the numerous eggs—which are originally contained within the reproductive organs of these creatures—throughout the entire organism. Yet he maintains that these eggs can develop only within the specific organs where they encounter a suitable medium for growth. The mammalian fetus—in which parasitic worms have been detected—is presumed to have acquired them from the maternal fluids. As for spermatozoa, Ehrenberg suggests that they are inoculated into every animal during the very act of procreation. Thus, broadly speaking, entozoa may be regarded as an inherited trait. One might even venture the question of whether entozoa constitute a pathological condition at all, or whether they are a regular component of the animal organism—such that it is merely their excessive proliferation that signals the decay and disintegration of the organism, much in the same way that such processes manifest in death. In this way, this first tournament concludes—much like the Platonic dialogues—with a negative dialectic, without inclining toward a forceful decision in favor of one side or the other.

The situation appears no more favorable in the second arena of inquiry—that in which infusoria constitutes the subject of contention. Here, Treviranus had once again caused the scales to tip heavily in favor of *generatio aequivoca*. His arguments are as follows: 

1. Various organic substances, when infused with the same water, yield different types of infusoria—or even mold—such that the very process of decomposition and putrefaction of these substances appears to be precisely what brings forth these living entities. 
    
2. Light is also said to exert a profound influence upon *generatio aequivoca*. An infusion of iris leaves in fresh well water, for instance, will generate infusoria if kept in a vessel covered with linen and exposed to the sun. Conversely, in a second vessel situated elsewhere—whether open or sealed, provided only that it is transparent—it will produce what Priestley termed "green matter." 

This green matter subsequently generates motile filaments, and according to some observers, is capable of progressing through various stages of development to form *Conferva*, *Ulva*, *Tremella*, or even leafy mosses. On the basis of these and numerous other observations, Treviranus believes himself entitled to draw the conclusion that, throughout all of nature, there exists a perpetually active, absolutely irreducible, and indestructible form of matter—a vital principle by virtue of which all organic beings, from simple to complex, possess life. Immutable in its essence yet mutable in its form, this matter—itself formless—assumes every conceivable shape of life, thereby holding within itself the potentiality for all such forms. However, this matter acquires a specific, determinate form only through the influence of external causes—a form which it subsequently alters whenever a different set of such causes comes to bear upon it. To be sure, this notion would mechanize the entirety of organic life, and as Empedocles and other cosmogonists do, extend *generatio spontanea* into a doctrine of creation.

Empirical observation, with its contradictory dialectic, did not fail to make its appearance and sow confusion here as well. First, the fact that the aforementioned plant forms originate from Priestley’s "green matter" was called into question by Wagner. He explains the transition of infusoria into green matter by positing that the latter is simply an accumulation of the corpses of plant cells and other infusoria—corpses which continue to exist independently as motile filaments. Furthermore, Ehrenberg succeeded in artificially inducing filamentous fungi to reproduce from seeds with astonishing speed. He also discovered within the infusoria the same complex anatomical structure found in the entozoa—a structure from which, he argues, serves as proof that they originate from eggs.

As for the theory of infusions, however, it has been the subject of extensive and protracted debate. To begin with, Ehrenberg directly challenges the assertions of Treviranus, maintaining that he himself—despite employing the very same procedures—never succeeded in obtaining specific forms of infusoria from specific infusions. Furthermore, a great deal depends precisely on the nature of this treatment itself. It is argued that anyone who boils organic substances in the open air cannot prove that the infusoria do not originate from desiccated infusorian germs. Those who used ordinary water to prepare their infusions from boiled organic matter fall victim to the same dialectic, for they must reckon with the possibility that this water already contained the tiny creatures or their eggs—in which case, rapid proliferation would naturally ensue. Even distilled water, it is claimed, is never entirely pure, and even water that has been distilled five times may still harbor organic particles. Even those who have conducted experiments using fresh organic substances in conjunction with distilled water—or even artificially prepared gases—cannot guarantee that the infusorian eggs, or the creatures themselves, were not already present within the organic substance from the very outset. Finally, even if every conceivable precaution has been observed—even if the experiments were conducted using boiled organic substances, distilled water, and artificially prepared air simultaneously—Müller still raises the objection that the instruments employed in the experiment may not have been absolutely free of organic matter! Through such an ultrafine sieve of hypotheses, any notion of *generatio spontanea* must inevitably fail to slip through. To ensure an even greater degree of certainty, physiologists subsequently conducted specific experiments demonstrating that atmospheric air—when passed through sulfuric acid—prevents the development of infusoria in boiled liquids. Thus, in such liquids—which come into contact only with air that has been calcined, but remains rich in oxygen and is frequently renewed—neither the formation of infusoria or mold, nor the process of putrefaction, takes place.

If Müller deems the arguments for and against *generatio spontanea* to be of equal weight, how is philosophy to render its verdict in the face of such contradictory facts? Firstly, if the Earth, as the universal organic individual, contains within itself the decaying residues of all deceased organisms, why should it not be able—in accordance with Müller’s own principles—to reanimate these same residues? Ehrenberg’s proposition that even molds and infusoria are generated through sexual union does not stand in contradiction to this. Sexual union is not, strictly speaking, even a *proven fact* for these organisms, even though the organs associated with it have been observed, and even without such union, these creatures could still conceivably arise *ex ovo*—provided that eggs have *in fact* been observed. For might not—as is the case with plants—sexual union and sexual differentiation be rendered superfluous in this context? Might not reproduction take place—just as it can occur without sexual union—even without the presence of eggs? However, that which is quite common in the plant kingdom applies in the animal kingdom only to the lowest species. However, *generatio aequivoca* is not only, through *a priori* deduction, a conclusion drawn from the most striking facts, but empirical science itself has stumbled upon facts that lend full confirmation to this philosophical solution, thereby affixing to it the seal of exact, irrefutable certainty. This refers to the specific form of metamorphosis in certain animals discovered by Steenstrup—a phenomenon to which he gave the name "alternation of generations."

Budge describes the difference between this alternation of generations and the metamorphosis of insects as follows: “In insects, the larvae transform back into an individual resembling the parent organism. The distinctive feature of the alternation of generations, however, lies in the fact that new creatures—dissimilar to the mother—arise directly from the larva, and indeed, not merely a single animal from a single larva each time, but an entire brood, and that it is only from these beings that animals resembling the maternal body subsequently arise.” It constitutes a transition from one species to another, occurring without sexual reproduction. An organ of one animal serves as the substrate upon which another is newly formed—one that only attains the maturity to develop sexual organs during a later transformation—a stage comparable to the moment in insects when the larva transforms into a pupa. Here, the maxim *omne vivum ex ovo* is irrefutably overturned. For eggs—which typically presuppose the prior occurrence of the sexual process—here follow only *after* the development of the animal itself. As a result, Ehrenberg exercises caution, leaving it an open question whether these eggs are truly the product of *generatio primitiva*.

The tapeworm may serve as a striking example of this generational transformation. It would be truly terrifying if tapeworm eggs were liable to be carried into our mouths from every quarter—along with the ubiquitous dust of the atmosphere—with every breath we take or every sip of water we drink, such that we would be in constant peril of contracting disease. But can a swallowed pigeon egg—even if it were to reach the stomach intact and undigested—give rise to a pigeon? Or a cherry stone cause a cherry tree to sprout forth? Thus it is claimed to have been discovered that the tapeworm undergoes a metamorphosis depending on the particular animal it inhabits: what exists in one host as a bladder-worm devoid of reproductive organs transforms, in another, into a sexually mature tapeworm. There remains, however, the persistent difficulty of explaining how an organic germ ingested from the outside could, as such, successfully withstand the reactive forces of the host organism and proceed to carry out its own vital processes undisturbed within that host—albeit, naturally, modified by its new environment.

In the case of trichinae, investigations are alleged to have yielded confirmation of this very phenomenon. But counterarguments have been advanced from other quarters, asserting that the consumption of infected meat does not simply introduce trichinae, but actually *generates* them *de novo* within the host organism. However, if an organism is capable of undergoing such a transformation by virtue of the new biological medium it has encountered, then it is difficult to conceive why that organic medium itself—the very substrate upon which another organism is supposedly deposited—could not spontaneously develop into the specific organic entity that is observed to arise within it. The pathological condition of having a parasite is essentially the transformation of a corrupted,  catarrhally degenerated internal organ into just such a parasitic creature. 

The final result of this inquiry is that the Earth, as the universal womb and fertile ground of all living things, with the aid of inorganic potencies of light, heat, air, and water, is foundation of: 

1. Individual organisms of a lower order that are transformed from organic germs that lie dormant or have perished within it—i.e., precisely through metamorphosis and alternation of generations, a process which, since Ehrenberg’s discovery that entire strata of the Earth consist of millions of infusoria, is all the less incomprehensible. Therefore, the dead and the living are by no means—as Schultz Schulzenstein would have it—mere opposites, for the Earth contains within itself not only the perished remains of the organic, but is also comprised of organisms. The living reverts into the dead—only to spring forth perpetually from this "other" of its own nature—since the latter is itself organic. Thus, it is possible for an individual organism—having partially lapsed into the inorganic through the decay of one of its own structures—to generate parasitic organisms

2. New individuals of the same species result from the Earth expressly through seeds and eggs deposited within it by plants and animals of a higher order.

3. The highest animals render themselves the suitable ground in which their young find a maternal womb, from which—once fully carried to term—they are born. 

In all these cases, however, the universal vitality of the Earth has passed over into its opposite: the individual vitality that generates itself at every single point, i.e. the plant.
