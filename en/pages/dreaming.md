# Dreaming

Note: This webpage is a direct translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Anthropology and Psychology, Vol. I* (1840) 

The interconnection of natural and spiritual life is a struggle of the soul against its own power, so that the soul may become master of itself and gain control over its corporeality. This victory is only possible through the complete identification of natural and spiritual life. Since spiritual life is subjective, conscious life, the next step toward this identification is that natural life also becomes subjective and conscious by confronting itself, and finally, that the soul acquires control by way of both identities merging into one. The waking state of the individual is the realization of its free subjectivity, and thereby its entire self. This state is the true totality, whereas natural life is only the implicit or potential totality. However, the existence of otherness also belongs to the true totality. Therefore, if the waking state is to be truly conscious, it cannot exclude natural life from its dream life. The waking state must allow natural life to influence it, for it appears as an external power that establishes itself within waking consciousness, initially only in individual glimpses, but afterward becomes increasingly pervasive in consciousness.

## Sleeping and Waking

### Dreams

In dreams, we see all the mental activities produced in the waking state recounted again in the dream state, such as feeling, imagining, thinking, as well as passions such as fear, hope, etc. What, then, distinguishes dreaming from consciousness? — a question that Napoleon already posed to the philosophers at the University of Pavia. Firstly, the distinction cannot be made on the grounds that mental activity is discrete in dreams while also being continuous in consciousness. For if Napoleon had claimed, in direct contrast to Descartes, that he had not been thinking while awake at all, then the question would still be, according to Locke, whether we are not always dreaming if we are always mentally active. This was also the reason why Descartes added to his statement: cogito, ergo sum, that the soul always thinks. The difference between waking and sleeping could therefore only differ as a difference of content between both states. But this difference is also difficult to grasp since we dream as we think and act when we are awake. For instance, people who travel around and interact with others a lot in the waking state dream a lot and about many things, whereas people who live simpler and more solitary lives dream very little. Calderón thus came to the idea of ​​representing both states on stage as interchangeable, as they do indeed alternate with each other. In the play "Life is a Dream," a prince believes he is actually living his life, but in the end, it turns out that it was merely a dream. And conversely, one could say that the dream is a life — and indeed it is, according to Novalis, the true life, since in it his beloved, who had been taken from him in his waking state, remained irrevocably his. Even the greater or lesser vividness of a dream could ultimately make a difference if we did not often dream more vividly than we perceive and imagine while awake.

The answer to Napoleon's question has two sides, form and content, both of which must be derived from the difference in perspective between waking and sleeping. Regarding the form of dream life, the soul attains a deep, powerful feeling of its entire individual nature, the complete scope of its past, present, and future. Since, according to this, the entire content of the soul's life in sleep is disorganized and chaotic, the consciousness participates in the dream can roam freely within this fullness, arbitrarily grasping this and that content from the dark abyss and illuminating it with a ray of its light, without being bound by space and time, or by any of the categories through which, according to Kant, objectivity first accrues to appearances in waking consciousness. The dream unfolds within the phantasmagoria of subjectivity, whatever its other content may be. Thus, dreams jump arbitrarily from one object to another, leaps over spaces and times, and connects the most heterogeneous things with one another, even processing external impressions into completely subjective, baroque combinations. In this respect, the dream even disregards causality since it compresses the richest and most diverse content into a single moment. Furthermore, each dreamer combines things differently according to their individuality.

In waking consciousness, on the other hand, everyone sees the same thing in the same place and is certain of what they see because they are bound to objective categories, e.g. causality, space and time. The form of waking consciousness is therefore the rational in contrast to the fantastic dream life. Even if our dreams appear rational and sensible to us it is only because we already had the same experience in the waking state. The content of dream, even had by the most sensible person, can never be reliably communicated. The waking state, however, remains immutably in the common ground of the universal consciousness of the species, while the dream belongs to the particularity of the individual. Hence, rational moral action, art, science, etc., all belong to waking consciousness, and what appears of this in dreams quickly proves to be illusion and impermanence. Of course, where the dream is only a memory of waking consciousness it seems to have to have the same content, but even here the dream weaves its own fantasies into the fabric of reality. The main difference, however, is that while the waking state has general life and life in general as its object, the dream is specific to the individual. and thus the dream can be prophetic, although it is accidental whether it is. 

### Daydreams

While it originally seemed that waking consciousness entered into dream state during sleep, it was actually reversed by the latter and drawn into its own sphere. Indeed, the dream life reigns not only in its own sphere, but by continuing itself into waking consciousness, it produces a pathological state in the latter which, as in every illness, separates a function from the continuity of the whole and strives to subject the latter to it. Thus, the inner life of the soul confronts the outer life of the body and seeks to dominate it in its own domain. It is in this wakeful dream state, the hallucination, that the struggle and opposition of both sides are established, while in the dream both were still immediately identical. If in the dream the inherent totality of the soul sought to rise into the sphere of consciousness, now the conscious waking individuality is entangled by the nocturnal dream world and beholden to its power. This is all the more possible because the soul, even in the waking state, does not lose its quality of being a timeless and spaceless totality. Since spirit is the ideality of the material, the all-pervading, its knowledge is neither bound to space nor to time, and it is only bound to the extent that it is mediated by dialectical thinking. If, however, the state of immediate, dreaming knowledge is able to continue into the free, mediated knowledge of wakefulness, then, in terms of form, this always remains more or less something pathological, but in terms of content, are only a reflection of particular events. The ordinary course of events, which also include world events, is inherent in the general states of mind, and can thus, usually with overstimulated sensitivity, become individualized in waking consciousness.

#### Premonitions

The first kind of daydream is the premonition. Although they are usually associated with supraworldly events, such as prophecies and the like, it is most often personal matters that are the subject of premonition. For instance, someone might have a premonition about the ceiling of their room collapsing and immediately leaves the room, though the impending change may have already started to become sensually perceptible, such as the presence of fine dust in the air. Similarly, a sailor might have a premonition about crossing paths with a storm even when there is not a single cloud in the sky, likely because they are accustomed to recognizing the early signs of storms that they have already experienced. Animals are capable of the same thing, running restlessly back and forth even before humans notice the approach of the thunderstorm. 

#### Hallucinations

The premonition of the waking consciousness, which exists only in the realm of dream imagery, can secondly progress to a sensory affection that infiltrates waking consciousness — the hallucination, which refers not only to sight, but can also affect other senses. It is told of a sister who saw her brother, who was ill far away from her in Valladolid, lying in the hospital. Similarly, Marsilius Ficinus, when his friend Mercatus died far away from him, heard hoofbeats in the courtyard, as if from a galloping horse, and the voice of his dying friend. Metal and water diviners, especially treasure hunters, when they approach an object resting invisibly in the earth with a divining rod, feel a heaviness in their limbs, which also causes the rod to sink. Such an event, distant only in space, which is perceived directly, has nevertheless already come into existence. The most remarkable thing, however, is that facts slumbering in the distant future can also be perceived as if they were present. These are affections which, without an external object, are projected by the person feeling them from the inner totality of their natural spirit into the external world. The central parts of the sensory nerves in the brain are capable of specific sensations independently of the nerve conductors. 

#### Trances

This complete overcoming of waking consciousness by dream life, however, not only has the negative result of death as a consequence, but because both sides have overcome their opposition and permeate each other, the dream life has itself risen to the knowing and willing individuality of the waking state. This natural substantiality of the spirit has incorporated conscious individuality as the state of trance. It is an intermediate state in which the individual is immersed in a dream state that simultaneously exercises all the functions of waking consciousness, though not by means of the eye, which is rigid and closed, but through general impressions felt by the body. For instance, a pharmacist's assistant in this state once prepared correct prescriptions by feeling for the relevant containers. This is also a pathological state to the extent that consciousness is displaced by organic life as the dominant force over the body. Hypnotism, in particular, produces a such a trance, which will be discussed shortly. Finally, the circadian rhythm can also exerts this influence on pathological constitutions in sleepwalkers. They walk fearlessly into danger because they do not see it, but when called and awakened, they immediately come to their senses and flee from it. The beginnings of this state are movements, talking in one's sleep, etc. The fact that the sleepwalker can be made to hear and see in the middle of sleep proves that the individuality of the waking consciousness has not entirely disappeared, but is only concealed, since it can reassert itself even against the dreaming consciousness.

What we have achieved here, however, is no longer one individuality, but two. The soul dissociates into these two sides, which nevertheless actually form a single individual, but which have separated into its spiritual and natural aspects. And because both are aspects of the soul are united in one body, they are connected to each other in an immediate way. This influence of the spiritual on the natural appears miraculous because we cannot perceive the mediating links. Thus, we continue to venture into the more supernatural wonders of the spirit, without indulging in superstition, since we recognize that they are a necessary standpoint and transitional stage of spirit so that it may attain its freedom. The miraculous aspect, however, lies more precisely in the fact that there are now two souls who relate to each other as one, and one soul that has dissociated into two.

### Dissociation

Thus we can find both normal and abnormal states in the soul where the contrast between natural and spiritual life is developed to the point of dissociation. This standpoint is, on the one hand, a liberation of the soul's dependence on nature where its waking consciousness was completely suppressed. On the other hand, with the disappearance of its dependence on nature, the dependence on another person remains, and so the person is not only subject to the general life of nature, but is concentrated in the soul of another person. We therefore have the relationship of two souls in one person, where one is the spiritual consciousness of the other, and the latter has its natural being in the former. This relationship is initially so one-sided that one person, who has not yet attained free consciousness, as a person existing only in itself, has its entire being, physical and spiritual, in the life of another complete person: the relationship of the soul to its beloved. Secondly, the inequality of the persons has vanished to the extent that each feels its being and the roots of its life in the other, but still with the difference that in one person the waking consciousness predominates, and in the other, the dreaming consciousness, this is hypnosis. Finally, the contrast between the two identities is overcome to the extent that the natural life, which the person in the waking state distinguishes from themselves as a second identity or alter that arises within the individual and presents itself to itself, as the plural identity of this person.

## Psychosis

When the individual can no longer distinguish between real life and dream life, we have the state of psychosis, the outermost limit of psychic life, but also the point of highest fragmentation that has yet to be reconciled. Psychosis consists more precisely in the fact that both lives, the dream life and wakeful consciousness, appear equally valid and equally real to wakeful consciousness itself. The dream world completely intrudes into the real world and envelops it with its threads such that the psychotic constantly confuses the two worlds with each other, or otherwise considers them to be one and the same. However, since both worlds magically permeate each other for the psychotic, they simultaneously feel the incoherence that exists between them. Psychosis is interwoven with moments of lucidity, there is enough rational consciousness in the psychotic to understand their psychosis, but not enough to free themselves from it. The psychotic thus inhabits an entirely different world from the real one, and since the real world is what actually surrounds them, they find themselves constantly hindered by it on the one hand while constantly assimilating it into their dream world on the other. For instance, if the psychotic is certain of a dream in which he is a king, then he will react with indignation at the empirical certainty that he has no subjects or kingdom to rule over. The fact that both worlds intersect each other so directly, and that neither can let go of the other without both coalescing into this contradiction, is precisely what generates this reaction from the psychotic. This contradiction and conflict of consciousness, the awareness of two worlds of ideas as one and yet also not as one, is absolutely necessary for psychosis. Even if someone has the most outlandish ideas and desires, but knows how to distinguish between their own thoughts and reality, we could very well say they are foolish or eccentric, but we would not be able to say they are psychotic. For psychosis consists in the fact that the person's delusions already constitute how they interpret reality.

The origin of psychosis is partly physical and partly psychological. As an abnormal state of mental life, it is an illness, a disrupted flow connecting one metabolic process to another. Since it can be physical or psychological life activities whose flow is interrupted, the cause of psychosis also has this twofold nature. It is certainly possible for a disease or external injury to the brain to produce this rupture of natural and mental life into a divided subjectivity. However, it is primarily the disrupted continuity and harmony of the mind that produces this state. As long as spirit, moved by its passions, does not allow itself to be overwhelmed by passion, but remains above it, there is nothing for it to fear. However, an extremely strong emotion, such as the grief of a mother over the loss of her child, an unhappy love affair, financial ruin, etc., can seize the mind in such a way that the composure of the waking state disappears, and from this point, rational consciousness has already been undermined, since the more the mind dwells on its misfortune, the deeper the roots it takes within it.

Since in psychosis natural life gains the upper hand over free spiritual consciousness and establishes itself independently, and the modes of the soul's natural existence manifest themselves as the different fixations, it follows from this that, according to the different character of the individual, this disturbance of free consciousness will also take on a different form. 

### Mania

If psychosis has not yet reached the point of a fully developed conflict between the two souls, it takes the form of directly incorporating the entire dream life, with all its arbitrary fancies stimulated by the changing stream of external events, into waking consciousness. With the two mental states having merged in this way, the person remains completely awake while enclosed in the circle of their dream life. This is what we call mania, the distortion of the sanguine temperament.

If mania progresses to the point that the patient cannot retain any of the impressions to prevail within themselves, they let their consciousness, as if without will, follow the sequence of their dream images, which proceed unceasingly, without taking notice of their immediate surroundings. Hence mania is often associated with distractibility, but the severe kind that makes it almost impossible to focus on anything. Since the manic person, in their ignorance of certain circumstances, directs their consciousness to other things, they end up making mistakes because they are inattentive to the connection between the two. If, on the other hand, this passive distractibility becomes active in all directions, seizing hold of all objects one after another and allows itself to be determined by them without judgment, then mania arises from a weakness of the power of rational consciousness that holds together the totality of representations. The manic person therefore occupies themselves with different things aimlessly, turning every object that comes their way into a different task they must perform, one after another, that is, without knowing why.

### Melancholia

The opposite of mania is melancholia, which can easily arise from sadness and despondency in life, though it also has a physical cause. Although the manic person is very active, this is entirely because of the constant shifting of dream images that determine them from the inside, and so they are always in a detached state of mind. This detachment is shared by mania and melancholia, just as both the sanguine and melancholic temperaments are detached. The contrast lies in the fact that the melancholic person sustains their dream life with only one specific impression that obstructs and obscures all other experiences, making them appear colorless and gray. The melancholic person is fully aware of the objective connections that exist in reality, but they naturally withdraw from being an active participat in life because so much of their conscious energy and attention is devoted to their subjective state instead. However, the melancholic person adds to this fact that they also do not recognize themselves as a part of the world, viewing themselves as alienated from it, and inhabiting an inner world constructed from past memories. Objects and dreams merge into one another, and yet melancholia sees the enormous chasm before it that separates them. This thread of rationality that remains becomes the very root of the despair that gave rise to this state of mind. Melancholia is the first contrasting disturbance of the mind because its dream life is at odds with the objective world and it is painfully aware of this contrast, whereas mania is in complete harmony with both worlds, without being aware of the contrast between them, precisely because it does not distinguish between the two states at all. And even if melancholic introspection has gone so far as to consider an imagined event as the cause of its misfortune, it nevertheless sets this as the cause of its suffering as if it were something objective. In any case, melancholic introspection takes the conflict in which it lives very seriously, which can lead to weariness of life in general, and even to suicide.

### Schizophrenia

Only this third form of psychosis, the extreme of the choleric temperament, is the most intensely contradictory of the psychoses in that it combines the positive symptoms of mania with the negative symptoms of melancholia. Similar to mania, this form of psychosis indiscriminately targets any objects that confront it and transform them into its dreamlike states of mind, but here they do not remain detached from them. On the contrary, schizophrenia actively turns against both real and imagined objects in order to destroy them and thereby re-establish the imagined harmony that is supposed to be the result of this pronounced contradiction in psychosis. The schizophrenic does not allow their inner dream world to take its harmless course, like the manic, nor do they shut themselves off from the real outer world, like the melancholic, but both worlds relentlessly bleed into each other without ever achieving reconciliation. As the hydra of their hallucinations constantly sprouts new heads, the more the schizophrenic hastens to cut them off. Schizophrenia is the form of psychosis where, especially if the schizophrenic does not quickly exhaust themselves, intervals of lucidity between states can occur. During these lucid states, by precisely comparing prior states against each other they are able to recognize the entire abyss of their inner conflict, whereas the manic person loses themselves in objectivity and the melancholic person only delves deeper into their subjectivity, neither of which actually experience the contradiction of psychosis as a contradiction. But even though the schizophrenic vividly feels the contradiction between their purely subjective mental state and the objective world, they struggle to overcome it because the two constantly and simultaneously appear as the other. Instead of the weariness of life found in the melancholic, this contradiction produces antagonistic feelings in the schizophrenic that range from distrust, envy, spite, rage, etc.

### Paranoia

Paranoia is the last of the psychoses to be detailed and it is grounded in the phlegmatic temperament. Since the phlegmatic person is generally not very mobile or changeable in their views and plans, when mentally unwell they do not, with choleric vehemence, distort every idea and impression they encounter, but instead have only one delusion that is held firmly in mind and around which their entire dream world is organized. However, the connection between the subjective and objective worlds is severed only at this one point. Such a person is capable of sensibly and reliably engaging with reality on any matter except for this point and what is connected with it. Even with regard to this delusion, the person acts with sensible consistency on other matters and against whose logical correctness no objections can be made, in which lies the implicit transition of psychosis to rationality. The psychosis here is only partial and no longer paired with a contradiction of rationality, but is calmly reconciled with reality apart from this one delusion. For instance, there was a man who did not believe he could walk across a chicken yard with impunity because he believed he was a grain of barley that the chickens would eat, but otherwise he conducted the affairs of his profession quite rationally, like any other sensible person.

## Habit

The physical is initially not commensurate with the spiritual. For, as we have seen, man is born with infinitely many capacities, but he cannot yet use them. The capacities are only potentially spiritual, a possibility that the spirit has not yet acquired. The reason for this lies in the fact that the body is still clumsy and awkward, that spirit is not used to its embodiment yet. It first must be made so by the human spirit taking full possession of its body, and this can only happen gradually and with practice. The soul must transform the body into a suitable dwelling place. The functions of the body only become habitual for man through the indwelling of the soul in the body. Walking, the movement of limbs, the senses, the satisfaction of needs, etc. are all activities to which man must gradually become accustomed, and which he acquires only through repeated use. Even in these sensory activities the soul is present, and if, after acquiring proficiency, we do not proceed with a conscious will in the performance of such activities, the relationship of the soul to the body is nevertheless one of complete mastery over it. Habit is thus the first mode of human freedom. Man makes his soul into being, and gives it a sphere of activity by taking possession of his bodily existence. The spirit, present in the physical through habit, also rises above it by maintaining itself therein as its universal.

Freedom appears in habit for the first time, but only in faint outlines of its nascent form. Acquiring a skill is indeed an act of freedom, but freedom vanishes once the habit is formed. Man is bound by the chains of habit as it becomes his second nature. Freedom at this stage consists solely in not being dependent on immediate nature, but on a second nature created by the spirit. The natural is not left as it is, but has been transformed. In habit, man is therefore not dependent on another, but on himself. Of course, dependence on habit is not much better than dependence on nature, for I do not have the power to instantly abandon the habit, the acquisition of which was within my freedom. Abandoning a habit requires a long series of opposing exercises different from those by which I acquired it. Habits are even more difficult to abandon than to acquire, precisely because what is to be overcome is no longer just nature, but something natural that has already been validated by spirit. The progress of this standpoint compared to the previous ones, however, is that the natural is no longer the basis and source of the spiritual, but that the soul has made itself the formative principle of the body. Schiller rightly says that man may call habit his nurse.

By transforming natural existence into a spiritual one through habit, nature ceases to be the determining factor for the soul. Since habit thus represents a liberation from the natural, the soul possesses the power to suppress the satisfaction of natural instincts and delay physical well-being when it hinders a spiritual purpose. This leads to the resilience of the soul, in which man asserts his purposes against the demands and temptations of his natural existence. Just as routine practice is the means to habit, routine labor is the means of resilience. Through continuous labor, man succeeds in enduring pain, cold, heat, fatigue, and so on. He overcomes these physical discomforts so that they no longer hinder his activity. Likewise, resilience can also be spiritual, for man can steel his mind against misfortune and thus feel the blows of fate less acutely, nor allow himself to be determined by them.

With this negative result of the habit inhibiting certain bodily functions, it is finally combined with its positive aspect, which was the skill acquired from the habit itself. The habit by which purely natural skills were developed was purely positive, so the body does not need to be transformed, only trained through the development of the capacities inherently dormant in it. However, with intellectual skills, a determination that is foreign to the body enters into it. Therefore, in addition to practice, the resilience and refinement of the physical being must also be added if the body is to be made suitable as an instrument for an intellectual skill. In the case of mechanical and artistic skills, the body initially resists not only with passivity, but also with the tendency to prefer the satisfaction of the body to intellectual determinations. Only through effort and repeated practice does spirit acquire obedience from the body. Virtuous skills also belong to this category. Temperance is resisted by the natural urge that strives for immoderate satisfaction and it is only through the repeated inhibition of desire does one acquire the skill of maintaining this moderation without effort. It no longer costs any effort later on, as it certainly did initially, because the dominion of spirit has gradually been established, and this dominion is now exercised unconsciously and directly over the body. It is this relationship that is the reverse of the earlier states, for now it is the mind that is certain of its dominion over the body. The influence of spirit is most clearly demonstrated in artistic skill, and no less so in the most ordinary and everyday things than in the most extraordinary. The skill of reading, for example, is acquired only by paying attention to each letter, focusing on each one individually, and then combining them into complete syllables and words when pronouncing them. Through practice, the eye learns to combine many sensations into a single perception of a whole word or even a whole line, without needing to bring the individual perceptions to conscious awareness. Likewise, when playing the piano, the eye is coordinated with the finger, and without seeing the keys, the finger feels not just one, but a whole series of them in quick succession, perfectly correctly, and makes no error even when it has to jump from one note to another. This coordination of fingers by the eye is a completely spiritual relationship, despite the fact that it has been reduced for us to something commonplace, and has completely lost its wondrous quality. 

After the body has become an expression of the spirit in this way, it must be the complete and total embodiment of spirit. The body is not only an instrument of the soul, but it must, in its external appearance itself, represent the inner character of the soul. While we first saw how natural being forms the character of the soul, now the spiritual character reshapes its body. The character of the individual is therefore both a product and a cause of physical existence, and it is through this interaction, that character continues to develop.
