### The Sublime

In the Sublime, the Image appears through the transcendence of the Idea as that which is not the Idea, that is, the sublime is the form of the Beautiful where the ideal moment stands in negative relation to the Sensible. When the Idea transcends the limit of its Image, it returns precisely to its pure universality, not only as a determinate Idea, but to the universality of the Absolute Idea, so that its life vanishes not only of the individual of this species, but of all individuals of all species. However, the Idea exists only in its individuals, and through the Beautiful it is essentially brought to full manifestation in one individual. Therefore, in the Sublime, the one individual is simultaneously the essential appearance of the Idea and at the same time disappears against its universality: this is a contradiction, and this contradiction is the Sublime.

The movement of the Sublime, therefore, has its basis in the qualitative relationship of the Idea to the Image. However, a new relationship of the aesthetic object now arises, namely its relationship to surrounding objects. For if the supremacy of the Idea is to be seen in a single object, others must stand beside it to contrast the Image and Idea existing in a balance of tranquil unity. Thus, the concept of magnitude now asserts itself here, the qualitative becomes quantitative, and the concept of magnitude includes a measure-relationship within itself. For the sublime object does not merely appear as large, but as utterly or beyond all comparison (as Kant suggests), and this presupposes a measurable comparison with the surrounding objects. Even if, in this relationship, the Idea acting within the seemingly vast object leaves behind everything surrounding it as something vanishing in comparison to its infinity, that object still appears to be a sufficient bearer of the Idea. Negation is only complete when the object, which in each case is the sublime bearer of the Idea, vanishes in comparison to it despite its greatness. Within the general negativity of the Sublime, therefore, a distinction is made between two forms: a positive one and a negative one.

The contrast between these two forms, however, is only relative. In both, negation is merely an effect of the positive activity of the Idea, which (according to Solger) underlies all the sublime as an act of living movement which often, but by no means always, presents itself as a sudden eruption. The negation itself, however, is only apparently narrower in the first, positive form than in the second, negative form, for, on closer inspection, it extends not only to the surroundings of the sublime object, but also to the object itself as a limited subject. It is only the power of the Idea active within it that extends its boundaries, and indeed so far that they can no longer follow the extending power at the end, but rather their vessel breaks, where the negative form in the narrower sense enters. The outflow of negation from the positively active remains valid even where complete stillness, an absence of life, which must still manifest itself sensuously, prevails in the sublime appearance, for in this either a previous or a possible and impending one reveals itself to recognize a standing, still self-contained development of force, and in both cases this support is doubly strong due to the infinity of the background.

Beauty is pure form. Pure form is essentially a measure of the proportions of the form, one that, while indeterminate in the abstract, arises strictly from its quality and is precisely defined for every sphere of life. This measure transcends the Sublime, indeed into infinity, yet at the same time, according to the very definition of its essence, it must, as a contradiction, retain form or a defined measure. Form as a boundary must simultaneously remain and dissolve into uncertainty, so the Sublime is both formed and formless. This contradictory determinacy manifests itself in the Sublime appearance either by partially conforming to the form and partially deviating from it, creating the illusion of an infinitely flowing deviation, or by the form being held firmly in its entirety yet so expanded that the subordinate parts disappear. In the latter case, the object would be beautiful were it not for the distance of its surroundings. In general, since the Sublime is a relational concept, it thereby draws into its sphere much that would fall under a different category without this distance. In both cases, however, the object is dark, and darkness is a characteristic of all exaltation (Burke).

The progression of the Idea, which in the Doctrine of Beauty could only be indicated, is more precisely defined in the Sublime according to its principal stages. The question arises whether this contrasting form of beauty is even possible at certain stages because the differences between these stages also establishes an essential difference in the forms of the Sublime. However, this does not exceed the scope of the abstract concept of beauty, since it concerns only the general categories, not the specific realities of these spheres, and the question of the act by which beauty is actually realized remains entirely unanswered.

#### The Sublime Object

According to the identity of thought and being, this classification establishes the form of the immediate or pre-existing as the first, objective form. The pre-existing is something that encounters the Idea realized as self-consciousness from the outside and therefore belongs to the sphere of the idea as unconscious activity. Since the negation that grounds the essence of the Sublime only truly occurs through the separation of self-consciousness, the mind in the subject seems to have to lent the object so much from its own that the object is thereby superseded as such. However, a significant difference remains whether the subject contemplates the Sublime where this lending is necessary, or where it is not, and the categorization borrowed aspects of objectivity constitute the decisive difference. It is therefore the existing illusion that something sublime exists outside the self-conscious mind that must first be grasped.

All sublimity, compared with beauty, is quantitative. However, there is a difference between whether quantity is the determining factor and whether the quality of the objects compared in their relative sizes is the determining factor. It certainly follows from the nature of beauty that not the abstract category of extension, but only the extended, can have aesthetic effect. However, as soon as quality as such asserts itself in aesthetic appearance, another form of sublimity arises. The first and most immediate, however, is sublimity in the narrower sense, the purely quantitative in which quality only plays a incidental role.

##### Sublime Space

The closest and simplest form of being in general, and also of the quantitatively sublime, is the form of being outside and alongside one another through the mutual exclusion of bodies: space. The sublime of space is either positive or negative. The positive arises first when an object stands in such a ratio of size to its surroundings that it appears to extend to infinity. For this illusion to be created, it is required not only that surrounding objects provide a standard of measurement, but also that a certain evenly continuous repetition of not overly sharp cuts on the surface deceives the viewer into the illusion that its continuation has no end. Sometimes serenely uplifting, sometimes threatening, the height appears, the depth restless and unsettling, the breadth expansive and arousing longing.

The transition to the negative form of the spatially sublime is already concealed within the very filling of a space with a multitude of objects so vast that it appears infinite. Here, a definite number encompasses the many, and at first this space seems infinitely large. However, since the sight of the many leads to the Idea of ​​infinite multiplicity, it is already given that, compared to the infinite filling of infinite spaces, every individual space-filling object and every definite space vanishes. The contradiction inherent in the entire category of space is announced by a vertigo of feeling. This nothingness, however, truly comes to be seen in the genuinely negative form of spatial sublimity: emptiness. It appears partly threatening through the Idea that that which infinitely fills space, but for which no space suffices, could break forth. The Idea that a certain space-filling entity, which was there, has sunk into nothingness and that everything else must likewise sink, that thus that which, by its very existence, establishes the category of space, is transient, thereby giving rise to the category of time.

##### Sublime Time 

That which excludes itself in space as something limited, also has its limit within itself and is therefore subject to the cycle of becoming and passing away, is the endless process of time. The sublime aspect of time appears in a positive form when an appearance outlasts the surrounding things for so long that the observer combines in it the feeling of its finitude with the feeling of infinite time. The quality of the object gives this feeling different degrees of specificity, but the sense of time remains the foundation. However, if the conception is actually realized in relation to a single living being in the sense that it is conceived as infinite, then the Sublime either sinks into the tedious or it is elevated into the horrifying.

Time is also defined by certain dimensions, depending on whether the subject perceives a prior or impending sequence of time. One perspective is the present, the other the past, the other the future. The past always evokes a melancholy, albeit of a different tone, the future a mysterious anticipation, and the present vanishes in its attempt to hold onto it, possessing no particular feeling for itself, except to the extent that it connects the two. These dimensions flow into one another in such a way that the inherent contradiction of the temporal form enters into the perception. Even that which was long-lasting, capable of representing the positively sublime aspect of time, now appears to represent, and with it everything permanent as transient, expressing the pure infinity of time, whereas the longest existence is a vanishing point. This is the negative form of sublime time. But since infinite time is only the continuous setting of vanishing points, the feeling of the nothingness of this entire category arises, and the contemplation seeks the sphere of another.

##### Sublime Power

The law of aesthetics demands, as the sublime of space and time reveals itself as exhausted, a form that both posits and contains them, and transcends them, in that, originates from itself as an inner point of unity and remains within itself. This form is the manifestation of the power that forms its origin and thereby fills space, but in expressing itself essentially as movement, it transcends space in time, and likewise, in remaining constant in its changes, it overcomes time. This form is no longer just quantitative, but is also qualitative, yet in such a way that the qualitative remains essentially bound to the quantitative for the time being, with one rising and falling with the other. As a luster-like vibration, the movement is usually associated with a sound, hence the dynamically or powerfully sublime is frequently, but not always, acoustic.

An object is dynamically sublime when its power surpasses that of its surroundings to the point that its own power, although limited by a measure, nevertheless seems to exceed all measure. Power is measured by resistance, so a possible resistance is imagined as if in vain. Since power is an independent being, but not yet separated from the quantitative and reflected within itself, and since it manifests itself in movement which, where it advances recklessly, has a destructive effect, since finally the spectator counts themselves among those surrounding them that can be affected by this destruction, the sublime nature of power generally appears as something terrible.

Where the quantitative incorporates the determination of quality, there is no indistinguishable and static relationship between the two, for quality progressively determines itself according to different degrees of intensity, and each of these entails a different relationship to the quantitative. At the lowest level, actual movement comes from the outside through the force of impact, and for this reason, measure and quantity are still essential. At a higher level, however, power resides within its own origin, exists in immediate unity with it, and does not necessarily act through the magnitude of the measures, but always as unrestrained, wild movement. On a third level, however, it gathers itself, albeit without actual reflection within itself, intensively in the depths and even places itself in an inverse relationship to its origin: quality already outweighs quantity.

The relationship of power to its origin, however, is also determined by a difference in form, whereby the differing position of power to the quantity of the origin is a co-determining factor. Either the power breaks the harmony of form, either of the entire species or an individual member of the species, through the predominant development of power in individual members, exhibits a disproportion of forms, whereby individual members step out of the subordination assigned to them by the whole and thus pervert the unity of the structure. This is Ugliness, which is permissible in beauty if it is terrible (Lessing), or else, while the forms retain their harmony, they reveal extraordinary power, whether through the expression of intensity or proportionate extension. The same distinction occurs both in the expression of potential and actual movement. A particularly intense power is called magnificent when it gathers other valuable natural objects around it for its own glory, majestic in its moderate movement, and solemn when it acknowledges an even higher power through these objects.

The power, especially that which has been intensely accumulated, can also, in the transition to a harmful eruption, remain contained and slowly expand, its movement interrupted by pauses before its release. This form is still positive, but it already approaches the negative, for the movement, which holds back the full eruption, so tenses the anticipation that the premonition of an infinite power, capable of negating the highest effect with an even higher one, is awakened. The impression of the slow and uniformly interrupted movement is particularly reinforced by the quiet, equally progressive and expanding tone. The actual outbreak, however, despite the expectation, indeed because of it, has a doubly powerful effect of terror.

The truly negative form only appears when the actual development of power, which at first appeared to be the greatest possible, is actually broken by an even higher power: the image of general destruction. This image, when it directly imposes itself on the senses in the details of the destructive process of the noble, living form, intensifies the ugly into the horrific, which Lessing defines as disgustingly terrible, and this is justified in the beautiful under the same condition as the ugly in general.

The peace and quiet that follows destruction, through its traces, reveals a power that is not exhausted even in this greatest possible effect. The possibility of an infinite new development of power looms, and this impression can be evoked by peace and quiet even without a preceding eruption, if it is accompanied by signs that herald a destruction greater than any other. Now, the entire emphasis of the perceived infinity is combined with the negativity. This power, however, which still conceals itself as an infinity behind the greatest possible effect, is truly no longer anything quantitatively infinite. Above every power, a higher one can be imagined, and the culmination of this intensification through the Idea of ​​a power that is simultaneously manifest and hidden, truly unconditioned, is in fact the abolition of this entire category. The progression into infinity dissolves into the ideal, truly self-contained unity of reflection: stillness and tranquility are the introspection of both the power and the observer upon themselves.

At the same time as power, however, all forms of the objectively sublime are negated because they are contained within the sublime object as negated. It now becomes clear to the perceiving consciousness itself that only a twofold deception on the part of the subject has placed the appearance of the truly sublime in this entire sphere. The first deception consisted in the fact that the limited object was perceived as extending into infinity, and the second consisted in the fact that this infinity of extension was conceived as the true infinite. The subject would not find any object sublime if it did not impose its infinity upon it through a certain anticipation. As soon as the subject reflects on this, it is no longer the object that appears, but the subject that is elevated to the sublime.

#### The Sublime Subject

The negation in the objectively sublime thus now appears as a negation of the objectively sublime, and this negation is nothing more than the subject positing itself as the ideal unity of itself. In this ideal unity, the infinite extraneousness and juxtaposition of finite things are superseded by being-in-itself. The self appears situated in space and time, bound to finitude while simultaneously being beyond it as true infinity. The self differentiates itself within itself, goes beyond itself in knowing and acting, but in its differences and expressions always remains with itself as its own self-conscious ground, which knowingly goes from itself and back into itself. Among the activities of the self, however, only the will comes into consideration. Intelligence cannot ground the appearance of the sublime, except to the extent that it conditions the entire appearance of the will and its consequences.

A subject appears sublime when, through the power of its will, it surpasses the surrounding subjects to such an extent that its own power, although limited, seems to ascend into the unlimited. Indeed, true infinity resides in the subject as such, and to the extent that the category of quantity is entirely overcome. However, the finiteness with which the subject is bound extends itself into this infinity and introduces differences in measure, which the will can indeed elevate and even surpass to a certain limit, but only in such a way that a new measure arises as a result. In the sublime realm of the subject, this measure appears posited with itself, and negated in comparison with the measure of the will in other subjects. Apart from this comparison, if the conditions for it were present, the sublime subject would appear beautiful, but this comparison introduces the contrast that grounds the sublime.

#### Sublime Passion

This form of the sublime, since the subject is to first become what it is in-itself, initially reappears in the form of immediate determinacy or as power. It is no longer mere power, but power that has its origin from the inner life of the subject, or force with consciousness, yet in such a way that only the power of the movement, in which the immediate and that which is willed with consciousness disappear into one another, determines the aesthetic impression, i.e. the movement of passion. It resembles the sublime power in that it is essentially terrible and that quantity, in the sense of the multiplicity of subjects, is of great importance in it.

But if passion becomes a state that determines and perpetuates all of subjectivity, and if subjectivity thereby loses its spiritual universality in its immersion in a particularity, it may still be terrifying, but the observer reflects on the pure freedom of the will, and the phenomenon transforms from sublime to ugly. Pure freedom, when it confronts aesthetic demands, is still divided from the sensual power and fullness of passion, and initially exists in fluctuating alternation alongside the unfree determinism of passion. The appearance of this restless will can only be an aesthetic object in connection with and in contrast to higher forms of will.

#### Sublime Evil

The false union of these opposites arises when passion, as the immediate will of the subject, is unspiritually elevated into the form of abstract freedom, and thus the will, posited as a principle, asserts itself as a universal and rational will. This reversal of the true unity is evil. This evil is sublime when, in this reversal, such significant powers are at work that the resistance of the surrounding subjects vanishes into nothing, and thus the negative essence seems to expand into a terrifying, solitary infinity of positive efficacy. These powers require both the extraordinary violence of passion and the subtlety of intelligence that glosses over the perverse principle and carries out the attacks, as well as the ability to abstract from individual gratification for the broader purposes of passion.

The inner reversal necessarily manifests itself in the deformity of form and work as ugliness. Just as true infinity, required for the sublime, exists only in the subject, so too does true ugliness exist only in evil. If the inner contradiction of evil and its corresponding distortion of form and work were considered separately in their nothingness, an ugliness would arise which would be aesthetically either reprehensible or permissible only on the condition that it would lead away from the present sphere through a transition to another aspect of beauty. Only the positive powers united around a false center in evil have a destructive effect since evil is essentially the lust for power. Therefore, these powers spread fear and horror, thus diverting attention from the pure consideration of the inner contradiction to this side, and under this enchantment, ugliness in the sublime is justified.

The negative form of sublime evil occurs when, upon the seemingly perfected villain, an even greater destructive power of malice is unleashed by another. Both can represent evil in the form of a threatening holdback or a full-blown eruption, and the most effective manifestation is when the greatest possible destruction, in the ensuing stillness and quiet, conceals an infinite possibility of new crimes, for the full power of negativity is now united with the effect of the anticipated infinity. But as soon as the observer delves into this abyss, they perceive in it the inner self-destruction that reaches its limit in the development of its work, where external destruction ensues. Evil cancels itself out and leads to the necessity that the will of the subject be reconciled with the universal and rational.

#### Sublime Good

The will of the subject that submits itself as a member of the general and rational will is the good will. However, the subject as an individual can only elevate one specific moral Idea from the totality of Ideas contained in the general will to its purpose in life. When the will of the subject, together with the entire scope of its personality, merges with the moral purpose that gives the subject's entire life its unity and continuity, then this subject, in comparison with surrounding subjects of lesser moral strength, appears to remain both a subject and the total subject of the species. The subject, however, must measure its moral strength through actual activity in resistance. In this struggle, it must be aided by the sublime power of passion. Good will that is united with the power of passion is called pathos in the positive sense. The word can also objectively denote the content of the good will, but never in the abstract.

Pathos, as a dormant force, can threaten an eruption and return to a threatening stillness. This stillness also acts as a negative form within the positive, more strongly than the eruption itself, but in a different and deeper way than stillness in the sublime power as such. For where the sublime power was doubled by the restraint, the pressure of infinity did not yet encompass the vision of a rupture occurring within power itself through the negation of reflection. Pathos, as a truly spiritual power, contains its negation within itself, enabling it to actively oppose and transcend its eruption. From this arises the truly negative and incomparably stronger form of the morally sublime.

This negative form initially presupposes an even higher manifestation of the moral will by which, as it appeared, the greatest possible moral strength itself is defeated. However, the consideration now does not turn to the active subject in this relationship. The suffering subject, since it bears within itself the negativity of spiritual infinity through its of moral elevation, it freely acknowledges the destructive power in the midst of the suffering that it has prepared for it. Now, suffering may come from wherever it may—from blind force, from passion, from a wavering, evil, or morally stronger will—and the subject still recognizes it as good. But this very aspect leads us completely away from the present sphere, and the latter is maintained only to the extent that the focus remains on the suffering subject as it overcomes its suffering through the power of moral freedom. This spectacle of the moral will proving itself in suffering is the negatively pathetic.

The will opposes its own suffering with the infinity of its freedom and transforms the crushing movement into a courageous one. This demonstration of freedom appears all the more profound the more the suffering affects not merely the sensual, but even the feeling that it is intellectually justified. This act of negative pathos is thus divided into two moments. The first is the suffering, where if the other moment is to prove its power, it must continue to the point of extreme fury, thereby causing the ugliness of destruction to reassert its validity. The second moment is the validation of freedom, which sets a limit to the feeling of suffering and transcends it into a feeling of victory. If this second moment is absent, or if suffering is met only with a resistance of a plaintive and gentle nature, then the sentimental arises, which has a limited place in beauty.

The crucial question now is whether freedom, in the face of the crushing affect of suffering, possesses an affect of an uplifting nature, or whether it opposes it with its abstract invincibility in unemotional severity. The first form is the weaker, positive counterpart to the negatively pathetic, and the second form the negative and stronger one, which is still easily destroyed by the appearance of insensitivity and is only appropriate under certain conditions, proving a will for which the subjugation of emotion has become second nature. This consummate firmness, in its outward appearance, is called dignity. But by becoming fixed in its appearance, its inner value is easily lost.

The concept of the matter, however, demands that the source of suffering not be overlooked. According to the context initially presented here, it must indeed originate from a subject that surpasses even the one previously conceived as sublime in moral strength. Now, the vanquished subject, in its self-overcoming, reveals a profound inner infinity, and this too must be attributed to the vanquishing subject to an even greater degree. Through this, the determination of quantity comes into play anew, and an infinite intensification arises in which the higher subject surpasses the lower one in the depth and extent of its moral power. Since jealousy and enmity are eradicated through self-overcoming, the good wills unite in mutual complementarity. The multitude becomes important in a higher sense, and the image of the good arises as a power infinitely reinforced by the multiplicity of subjects 

In truth, however, both movements—that of infinite ascent and that of infinite extension over many—above and beyond, negate the sublime subject. For if a more sublime subject can be conceived above every morally sublime one, then no single subject is truly morally sublime. If the good grows in power and significance to such a degree that it becomes common to many subjects, then it is precisely the limits of subjectivity that are negated in this relationship of mutual complementarity. The contradiction posed between infinity and finitude in the subject, between the particularity and universality of its moral pathos, as well as between the latter and the totality of all moral ideas, comes into effect, and it becomes apparent that the subject in its sublimity is more than itself.

#### The Tragic

The higher form of the sublime supersedes the lower form in the twofold sense that the latter is negated as an independent form, but is also incorporated into the higher form as a moment its activity. Therefore, the superseded form does not cease to exist alongside the higher form, but serves the higher form as an object of its purpose. Over this relationship reigns an ever-penetrating contingency which extends to all that is beautiful. Moreover, the sublime is indeed a fermentation within the beautiful, but the beautiful does not disappear through the transition into the beautiful. Instead, it exists alongside it and increases its interconnectedness into infinity. Thus, an incalculable complex of infinite interaction and transition arises.

After the last form, this complex that appeared to be the highest and truest form has been abolished, representing a measure without law or unity. However, this form has not only been abolished, but has transformed into something sublime that is greater than subject itself. This sublime being simultaneously reveals itself as something that permeates, transcends them, and unites them the arrangement of subject as a common entity or collective subject. But this is not merely collection of subjects, but the same true infinity that was present in one subject but burdened with the contradiction of particularity, which also operates in the other, thereby supplementing the deficiencies of the one with the perfections of the other. For precisely this reason, it is not a single subject, but a pure, active unity, as an infinite reciprocal supplementation of subjects, that eternally generates itself as a universal subjectivity or an absolute subject.

If the lower form is contained within the higher form, then this absolute subject is not only true infinity in good subjects, but in all subjects. Since the good will governs the subordinate forms of the will, it is the ruling power in the world of subjects as well as the the inner ground in of the objectively sublime and the harmless forms of beauty that are entangled in this circle. Since subjectivity can never truly transcend the objective ground of its existence, infinity initially reigns from below as strict objective necessity, and the contradiction between this proposition and the first, that the good will governs the whole from above, remains for the time being. But just as much as the absolute subject remains the true infinite in subjects as they rise above the objective ground of existence, or absolute freedom in the freedom of individuals, it forms from this a second, spiritual objectivity. For the freedom that appears through the exchange and complementarity of subjects, shedding the contingency of individual subjectivity, becomes the power of moral necessity.

This necessity, as the law of the moral world, extends into different spheres of moral life, and the absolute moral power extends into particular moral powers. For the absolute moral power can give itself no other content than permeating the natural drives with the freedom of the Spirit, and the natural difference between Nature and Spirit, in their transformation, itself grounds the difference between the moral powers. This difference takes the form of an opposition, but the opposition is resolved into harmonious unity in the Absolute Idea, which is the absolute subject.

The dual form of objectivity or necessity is to unite into one whole, and this union must presuppose that both forms are mutually dependent, since it is precisely the interplay between the binding force of the one and the freely transcending force of the other that generates the moral life. However, this can only manifest itself in a movement or process that must now be shown. Through the dissolution of the necessity that rests on the immediate ground of life and the moral necessity with its particular spheres into a unity, the whole of a necessity now comes together as an infinite chain of events governed by an absolute law. This order, however, is realized in the complex of its measures in an unfathomable way and in the execution of the infinite movement in which it interweaves the activity of nature with moral activity, proceeding in the course of chance, extending over infinite spaces and times, binding everything to everything else, and is thus necessarily hidden from the limited perspective of the individual. This remains true even though moral necessity presents itself aesthetically in a limited appearance, whereby that abolition of the disturbing chance in the general sense is already presupposed and is thus obscure.

To understand this movement, it must first be established that the sublime subject has not simply vanished, but is a suspended moment of it. As such, it reappears in such a way that the relationship has been reversed. Previously, the sublime subject expanded beyond itself and remained a subject. Now, the sublime object moved to the side toward which the subject expands, appearing as a limitation which the most sublime imposes upon itself and then suspends again. The subject arises against this background, and since this background is present before it, the subject also comes from it. The sublimity of the subject is therefore their own, the background is within themselves, but the background extends infinitely beyond them just as much. This contradiction initially lies undeveloped. The subject, with all the property of its sublimity, is indebted to the background. This is potential or original guilt.

The subject is active, it is pure activity. In acting, the subject objectifies its freedom and thereby intervenes in the complex of objectivity or necessity. However, the action is inevitably bound up with the particularity that limits the will of the subject. The action of the subject therefore separates what was together and violates the absolute unity of the objective chain of events. Either the first form of necessity is separated from the second, so that one is acted upon according to the law and the other violated, or one moral sphere is separated from another with the same consequence, whereby its opposite becomes a contradiction. But both cases amount to the same thing, for it is precisely at this point that the unity of both forms is proven by the fact that there is nowhere a place where the the dark foundation of life would be incorporated into a moral context, thereby becoming a duty that would be in harmony with other duties. This painful separation is now actual or real guilt. To feel guilt is a work of freedom, but of a freedom that cannot act otherwise because it is only the freedom of the individual subject. It is nothing other than the realization of the original guilt or innocence. All the more clearly, however, does it become apparent that the subject, in developing its greatness through its actions, thereby unfolds its infinite smallness, which vanishes in comparison to the whole. This contradictory movement can be called the ironic.

The injury reveals itself to the subject by agitating the background, causing it to move as an absolute whole, whereby the isolated action is drawn into an unforeseeable chain of consequences, such that the subject does not more than it recognize its own in this. Despite the insight into this deprivation of what was desired, the subject must nevertheless maintain it as its own and stand by it. The objective sequence of consequences, however, are essentially also a counterattack by the violated against the violater. The seed of evil nurtures its own suffering. The suffering that follows is infinite and stands in disproportion to actual guilt, but not to original guilt. Just as the former follows from the latter, so too does it stirs up against the perpetrator the infinite evil accumulated by the collective guilt of all individuals, in and for which the individual actual guilt can no longer be distinguished. This movement, in which the subject strives to create good and creates evil, can also called ironic. This irony is amplified when the threatened person foresees the evil and plunges into it through the means by which they try to avoid it.

Suffering, though inwardly infinite, can outwardly come to an end, allowing the subject space to complete their work. Within the sphere of the sublime subject, perseverance in suffering was explained by the recognition of the purpose of suffering as good. Now, however, this purpose is not only understood as good in general, but as the absolute good of the purpose being realized in the grand scheme of things. By recognizing their suffering in this context and as a consequence of their guilt, the subject purifies themselves and their work, no longer carrying it out according to their own sense, but in the sense of the absolute whole.

The suffering can, however, continue until the subject and its work are destroyed, so that the background has become entirely the foreground. But the work is not thereby abolished, the objective chain of consequences outlives the subject, and in accordance with the concept of the whole as a moral unity, in this process it purifies itself from the isolation imposed upon it by the subject. In its demise, if the subject also assimilates the consciousness of this purification and the justice of its suffering, then the complete reconciliation has occurred, incorporating the subject into this perpetuation as a surviving figure of it, otherwise the whole, which only acts through subjects, would negate itself. This conclusion can no longer be called irony, for the absolute subject acts entirely positively in this negation.

This entire movement is called fate or the tragic. All previous forms of the sublime lapse, each one transcending its prior identity, and into which it incorporates them all as their unity. This highest form now proves to be the one which preceded the former as its own. However, it cannot lose itself to any higher form, and self-transcendence is the essence of the sublime appearance. The absolutely sublime occupies the entire ground of the appearing phenomena as a hidden power, creates the illusion that these are the subject of the sublime, and then dissolves them into itself. The disappearance into something other becomes serious, but this other reveals itself as the One that truly underlies everything, only appeared to disappear into something else in the earlier forms, posits itself within them, and just as much abolishes this positing as a limitation. It is an analytical process through which the last result is posited as the first.

The dualism that pervades all that is sublime has already appeared as the law of tragedy. The sublime subject is granted, on the condition that they understands its sublimity as an emanation of the absolute, and despite its suffering, which they explicitly acknowledge as a consequence of their guilt, they are granted the opportunity to victoriously achieve the good cause and secure their own happiness. Negation is thus also contained in this position, but this form, as in all levels of the sublime, is nevertheless the considerably weaker one, for here, the true subject of the sublime only manifests itself as positive power when it truly negates the independence of the lower forms incorporated within it.

In its negative form, even the last illusion is abolished, as if the subjectively sublime could save its independence. Since even the highest and noblest of this kind cannot escape the guilt that leads to its downfall, the absolutely sublime emerges in all its majesty. The extraordinary, in its struggle with the equally legitimate mediocrity, always outwardly perishes in order to leave its purified striving to posterity. This truth, as the highest law of the absolute idea realizing itself in the moral world, enters into beauty along with the entire life of the Idea and rises to the pinnacle of the sublime.

##### The Tragic Universe

The negatively tragic element is defined as a difference of forms whose first element, in its obscure form, condenses the elements contained within the whole that morality remains a mere possibility of guilt and its punishment. Therefore, the absolute subject only appears in the immediate form of a blind power, wherein an individual subject sets out an example that the individual must perish because it is individual. Since arrogance, though close to the subject thus favored, has not yet occurred, guilt remains original guilt. Evil does not arise from a violated moral will, but by chance, in which even this form nevertheless finds its meaning in the reconciling Idea of ​​the necessary universality of death. The entire movement occurs on the basis of strict, objective necessity, in which the world of moral necessity slumbers undeveloped.

##### The Tragedy of Guilt

The subject overreaches through the necessary nature of freedom, transforming the original guilt into actual guilt. However, this guilt is simple. That is to say, if the will is initially good, it does not lie in an objective, unavoidable contradiction into which its pathos would fall, but in some error which is that is more or less connected to the moral strength of such a will. However, it establishes a stricter aesthetic unity when it appears as the immediate opposite of the former, and thus appears avoidable according to the abstract concept of freedom, avoidable, but is unavoidable in view of the determinacy of personality. However, in this realm, it is by no means merely the sublime good that appears. While the transition to the tragic is indeed mediated through it, as the unity filled with all preceding forms of the sublime, this places one or another form from the sublime of the subject in the foreground as a means and object of its movement. Only passion and the restless will are incapable of occupying this place. Apart from the good will that goes astray, only evil can result as a further possible center of tragic movement, and if this pinnacle of the subject's hubris forms the lever, then guilt is not merely in that sense of some kind of error, but also because it is unmixed.

At some point, guilt violates the moral complex. Other subjects suffer through it, and since the guilt is simple, it initially seems that they suffer innocently. But if this were so, they would be means to the end of tragic subject's guilt, which contradicts the absolute value of subjectivity, were it still entirely undeveloped. Therefore, they must not appear completely innocent, they must have presented the guilty subject with a vulnerability through some fault, and this vulnerability must also be intrinsically connected with their virtue. However, if they suffer disproportionately for their minor guilt so that the guilt of the tragic subject may be fully revealed, then either the standpoint of the first form of the tragic emerges as reconciliation, or the elevation in suffering reveals an inner infinity which softens the harshness of the disproportion.

The punishment is inflicted through the violation of the moral complex, and the aesthetic unity is all the greater the more it appears as the simple flip side of the guilt. However, if this guilt is of an indeterminate nature, the punishment may arise from an unfortunate accident. If it is of a specific kind, then subjects are intentionally its means. These means are either, as the injured parties, although not entirely innocent, yet in right against the guilty principal subject and the punishment appears immediately just, or they are not the injured parties and, by inflicting the evil upon the principal subject who is not guilty against them, themselves become guilty and punished, but this puts their immediately undeserved and accidental suffering caused by one's awareness in connection with one's guilt. Acknowledgment of the connection between guilt and evil is always required. However, this can be voluntary, allowing the subject to even inflict the ultimate evil upon themselves, or involuntary, compelling the conscience to yield to the evil will. If the latter is the central point, then external destruction must represent the pure reversal of self-destruction.

In this second form, chance is indeed placed within the context of a moral whole, but a higher form of its abolition is required, which can only consist in the fact that guilt is not merely linked to the moral striving of the principal subject by a closer or more distant subjective bond, but coincides with it at one point. Thus, it is no longer just any guilt and no longer violates just any relationship, but rather that moral power which would be in inner unity with the other, which is the content of that striving, if that striving were not guilty. Now, even in the second form, the purer case is that when the punishment is inflicted by subjects who are both guilty and in the right against the offender, and the sought-after higher form of the tragic will arises when these subjects, in carrying out the punishment, become guilty in the same way as the first subject. The seeds of this form are already prepared in the second.

##### The Tragedy of Moral Conflict

The subjects that bring about the tragic event now move into a relationship that binds them so closely that no striking intervention of chance has any room to maneuver. The binding element is the spiritual center of the complex of necessity, the moral Idea that unites the circle of moral powers. From this circle, the aesthetic law of limitation distinguishes the contrast between two certain moral powers arise, which essentially demand something of one another and are in harmony in the pure Idea. The purest case, however, is when this opposition does not fall within one subject, so that it cannot act without injuring one or the other member of that subject, but when each member, as its pathos, falls to one of two subjects who stand opposed to each other as champions of a multiplicity of subjects.

Among these two powers, one has precedence over the other, although the latter is also justified. For the life of the moral Idea entails a constant opposition between free progress and the necessarily existent, between the new and the old, for the will constantly creates new forms, and once they are no longer appropriate to it, strives to shake them off as a deadening slump, while they nevertheless appeal to permanence as long as the form satisfies the needs of the still-developing sphere of the will and the new form has yet to be created. This gives rise to struggle and conflict because both laws—that of the new creation and that of the existing old—are valid. However, because the moral Idea is absolute movement, the right lies with the first side.

Since each of the moral powers is assigned to a specific subject, a separation of mutually opposing demands necessarily occurs. Due to the limitations of its individuality, the subject can only elevate one moral power to the content of its life. Now, in its contemplative consciousness, it may justly consider the pure harmony of this power with its opposite. However, the determinacy of the situation demands a specific action, for only one thing can be done. In this turmoil, deliberative consideration yields to the one-sided strength of pathos and justifies only this through its underlying thought. But the passion in pathos is simultaneously hatred of the other pathos and its representative. For hatred is love perverted, which throws the resentment—for which the subject is itself responsible, because it cannot simultaneously absorb the other demanded by one pathos—upon the one who has absorbed it. It is precisely the unity of the opposites in the Idea that divides the representatives of its opposing limbs and makes them enemies. Thus, the agitated deed irritates and disturbs the tranquility of the otherwise unmoved unity of the mutually demanding moral powers.

Through the action of one subject, the other, imbued with the pathos demanded by the Idea as essential as the pathos of the first, is violated in its rights and is driven by its own pathos to launch a counterattack. But the second subject it is in the same position as the first subject, it is also wrong in its right and falls into guilt. Both subjects now experience through their own actions the opposite of their intended purpose. What they have willed comes to pass, but so does that which perverts and negates their intended outcome. Through this, both subjects suffer infinite harm. The intensity of the tension demands that this not only consist in the infinity of inner pain over the perversion of the highest purpose of life, but that blood be shed. While the subject who stands in the more obvious right plunges into death through the swiftness of fuller pathos, for the subject who fights for what exists, a bleak survival of dearly lost souls after apparent victory is more fitting. As for the subjects drawn into this struggle, but not directly involved, the same applies to the relationship between their downfall and their guilt in the second form.

In this form of the tragic, negation is the harshest since the innermost core of moral will itself contains guilt, and in the rigorous dialectic of action, everything arises from within and penetrates into the inner self. Precisely because there is no appeal to chance for guilt here, there is also no lament about the randomness of punishment, and so this form is the most just and its reconciliation the most profound. The demand of the moral law contained in each of the warring forces is fulfilled by the deed, but at the same time, the one-sidedness in both deeds is eradicated by the opposing one, thereby opening up the prospect that they will survive their defeat in a purifying way. The subjects, having been shown their inner selves, expand the clarity of thought with which they previously only justified their pathos to just consideration. Hatred is extinguished in love and acknowledging that they have erred. Even if they perish, their united image is incorporated into the moral harmony that hovers over their corpses.

### The Comic

The pure harmony of the Beautiful is negated in the Sublime by the negative stance of the Idea toward the Image, leading to the contradiction of the former surpassing the latter while maintaining their inseparability. The reconciliation at the end of the Tragic is not a creation, but a deceptive postponement of the positive validity of the Image. This validity, however, is maintained by virtue of the pure interconnection posited in the concept of the Beautiful, for regardless of the dependence of the Image on the Idea, they are both meant to completely encompass each other. The very essence of beauty demands a rectification of this disturbance, a complete satisfaction for the diminished right of the Image. This can only consist in a new contradiction, in the now negative position which the Image now adopts in relation to the Idea, by resisting its connection with the Idea and asserting itself as the whole without it.

The independence of the Image from the Idea is a contradiction because the Image is nothing without the Idea. The Idea establishes a phenomenon in which the lowest is placed at the top, by the  individual, which is destined for subordination by the general, is arrogated to that side of the Image which is meant to represent the general in essence. This contradiction is Ugliness. Now, Ugliness has already appeared in the Sublime, and this seems to contradict the correct sequence of concepts, for it is now derived from the negative position of the Image in relation to the Idea, when it originally followed from the negative position of the Idea in relation to the Image. However, it followed from an excess, though it reverses the order of the form, too terrible for the emphasis to fall on the reversal itself. What would have been a perversion of the Idea that governs the structure as its generic concept, resulting in Ugliness, thereby enters into a different standpoint in which it serves the Idea, namely that which in each particular sphere conditioned the Sublime.

For this very reason, the contradiction of ugliness was not present in its severity where it appeared in the sublime, for the inversion of harmony, by which the lowest is placed at the highest, did not assert itself there as beautiful, but was only a means of the terrible. All ugliness that is terrible falls away from the pure ugliness that appears in the present instance, since the given transition demands the ugly as such, i.e. an appearance that not only rebels against its own Idea, but in that this inversion itself presumes to be the Beautiful.

If pure ugliness is to arise, then beauty must summon in appearance that which, unless the further act of supersession in the idea follows, causes the idea to perish in atrophy: the confused transitional forms between the realms of the idea that is real as unconscious life and the entire realm of contingency, as it governs both the emergence of individuals and their infinite individuality, which, however, must not here, as in the doctrine of the sublime, escalate into terrible malice, and is effective in the exchange of solicitation. The sublime has not abolished chance, but has held it strictly together through the binding idea. Now, it must break in in its entirety, and even the utterly disturbing form of chance, which is asserted as unaesthetic or meaningless evil, does not fail to appear. If the idea does not have the power to control those chance occurrences, then this actual atrophy must also break in.

#### Amusement

According to the law of the origin from the immediate and its the progression to the mediated, among the three forms mentioned, the most immediate form must appear first. The extent to which the activity of subjectivity extends in the synthesis of the two elements of the Comic does not establish an essential difference. The Comic has a process before them in which they either insinuate reflection into the ridiculed subject, or presents themselves as the subject of ridicule for their own pleasure. In the former case, the Comic seeks out the sensually determined objects or events as amusement, and in the latter, the Comic expresses their exuberance in a completely tangible form, so that even action without speech suffices to bring the amusing into being.

The sublime that succumbs to the amusement of the comic is therefore always already something whose spiritual essence does not come to consciousness as such, but is embodied in a fixed form. For instance, the sphere of power, propriety, external expediency, and passion can constitute the subject matter, but can the highest realms, though only in a tangible, embodied appearance. The counter-thrust against which this sublime falters, and which here more often comes from outside than from within, will necessarily resort to the lower and cruder forms of existence and violate decency most vehemently, not only where the struggle is explicitly directed against it as the first element. The natural ground with which the subject is bound is completely disturbed in order to free itself from it. For this reason, in the entire process, this whole form can be described as exceptionally naive.

The generative activity of the entire process is naive, it requires a tangible form for both the first and second elements because it does not yet distinguish the inner essence of the object from its outward appearance, and it cannot do so because the subjectivity has not even completed this separation yet. For this reason, and because the tangible nature of the form necessitates a completely public and presentable character, this stage of the comic can be called both folksy and as the original ground of all comedy that persists despite all progress to the finer stages of it. If they lack deeper awareness and introspection, they are without any secrecy or intentionality and go along with the flow of things in a comfortable manner.

#### Wit

#### Humor

Wit has not only spread throughout the entire world of the sublime in terms of its subject matter, but has also worked through the world of objects drawing on the most remote to everything closest to it. The comic has indeed abandoned this summoning of the distant as irony, but while they took the first step in engaging with their object through ironic self-containment, they have nevertheless not lost the result of that arbitrariness. If the subject now spreads itself infinitely over everything and everyone, both in other forms of wit and in ironic wit, through the need inherent in wit for ever new material, then the point must arrive where the scientific awareness of the lack in wit becomes its own and the subject subsumes itself under its comic activity. Now the reflection that separates the object and subject is abolished. Reflection arrives at the reflecting subject itself and abolishes the first one-sided reflection.

But if the very nature of wit drives it to this reversal, then the infinite series presents itself as a further reason for the necessity of this transition. The completion of this infinite position of subject over subject cannot yet occur since wit as such lacks its own condition. However, if there ever is a wittier one above the witty one, who makes the former its object, then wit as such must experience that it cannot exclude itself from its own activity. The friction of wit against wit, together with that which propels it forward by virtue of its inner essence, throws its consciousness inward. Thus, from the multiplicity of witty subjects, just as in the multiplicity of sublime subjects, a new unity appears in the the unity of the comic subject and object. This unity, however, does not negate the individual subject as in the tragic, but presents itself as a single, undivided personality by virtue of its justification in the comic, in which the divisive reflexion of wit that defined the comic has ceased to exist.

First, this personality must be considered as it is inherently determined. If it is to carry out the entire comic process within itself, it must bear the first element of this process, the sublime, within itself as its own content. In wit, the general determination of the comic that preserves the sublime by destroying it as an alien power, has become uncertain, whereas its fulfillment in the objectively comic is secured by its immediate and untroubled comfort. Because the witty subject excludes itself, it does not inquire about its authorization to be comical, and it can perform the comic act just as well with any disposition in spirit. The humorous subject, however, can be the whole of the comic only if the first element is present within it as a reality, that is, in its emotional life, as the power of the spirit and its moral enthusiasm.

This sublimity is now bound up in one and the same subject with the infinitely small. It is initially irrelevant whether this impact is a particularly sensitive one, as it manifests itself through the burden of an appearance contradicting inner sensibility, through dire circumstances, adverse accidents, petty weaknesses, or whether it is merely a feeling of general human weakness and dependence with a slight degree of one's own. On the one hand, in this form of comedy, the keenly sensuous observation of amusement and the sharpened eye of wit are preserved for every provocation. On the other hand, this sharpness is further heightened by the depth and purity of the emotional life becomes most sensitive to the pressure of the infinitely small. Therefore, every provocation becomes an infinite feeling of pain, and since life is a series of such provocations, the general mood hovers between the enjoyment of the highest elevation and the deepest sorrow at these infinite inhibitions.

Thus, the existing personality is determined and the reflection of wit has initially been extinguished. But from this being, humor frees itself through the movement of a second, tracing back to its own subject entire reflection. Through this, the comic understands itself as one subject and interweaves the two opposing elements so that they substitute the infinitely small for their sublime self and the other for the infinitely great, which only finds its fulfillment through the unity of the subject that has turned inward. They now recognize the infinitely small within themselves as justified and infinitely valuable because they have grasped it as the ground of the sublime. The comic is still not proud of this because they cannot do without the sublime, and thus the contradiction of moral greatness and smallness, of enthusiasm and despair, dissolves into the pure unity of self-exaltation and self-mockery. Thus, the fruit of a personally experienced struggle, in which the awareness of wit returns in a higher form than one won, takes precedence.

But since the moral content of this personality is by its very nature a universal one, a coexistence with all that is sublime and the reality of the Idea in general, and since the sharpness of observation and feeling reveals to the humorous subject everything petty, poor, and bad with which it is entangled everywhere and always in its realization, its own self is simply the image and focal point of the contradiction that arises from the world that makes it possible. In terms of subject matter, humor can also grasp this or that sublime thing. It will certainly turn to its highest forms even more than wit and inwardly pursue them into their depths as the inner presence of spiritual life. But the essential distinguishing feature of humor is that it has the awareness of universality and recognizes every sublime thing that it accompanies in its case as a form of the absolutely sublime.

Humor, therefore, knows that wherever it pursues any sublime element into its disturbance, nothing is pure, and its pain is as universal as its enthusiasm, its deepest disgust and weariness with the world. As for the nature of its counterpart, precisely because they seeks the sublime as emotional life, the realm of inner disturbances opens up to them above all, and they have the deepest insight into its origin. However, this does not preclude external chance and the most egregious opposition. The distinctive feature of humor is that it consciously combines even this into a universal subject with the sublime that is entangled within it. In this way, the comic extends themselves to the world, the contradiction in themselves becomes contradiction in the world, and what appears to them as something entangled is, because in truth the whole of existence eternally encompasses itself in subjectivity, the world as an infinite subject.

This synthesis of the entire universe into a single subject, however, just as it makes the pain all the more incisive, is also the first step towards reconciliation, which arises from this general contradiction just as it does from the individual aspect of the humorous subject itself. If the unity of the contradictory elements is also the subject here, then this universal subject is the same free movement, which yields its sublimity to the infinitely small, but only because this also truly incorporates it into itself within the entire universe as its homeland. If the smallest is in the largest, then the largest is also in the smallest. Humor is therefore loving towards the folly it dispels, not only because it sees the universal in every individual folly, including itself in its mockery of the world, but also because in both the former and the latter is contained the consciousness of the infinite value of the infinitely small.

Now the question arises whether an objectively and a subjectively humorous personality can be distinguished. Only in a purely relative sense can this be affirmed at this highest level, for the free personality, to which the self-consciousness of contradiction is attributed as a permanent characteristic, is the condition of humor. The subject of deeper moral vitality, whose pure self-consciousness is broken by the inappropriateness of its appearance, the pressure of chance, and the unconquered aftereffects of unfreedom, is necessarily thrown back upon itself and carries out the entire comic process, concerning itself and the world, within itself. This process can certainly develop more or less into a free skill, but when the comparatively more conscious humorist emerges from the comparatively less conscious one, the former does not cease to represent the entirety of the comic within itself.

If one considers how humor carries within itself the duality of its consciousness as a constant worldview, and in this light views and reveals everything that appears to ordinary consciousness as a true existence, throwing itself around in all forms of amusement and wit in endless transitions in order to assert this worldview, then one understands how it must present itself to common understanding as madness. However, by always starting from themselves, immersed in their own contradiction and only seeking its reflection in the world, the comic loses the simple view of objectivity and their ego, although not in the sense of self-love, but of self-mockery.

#### Naive Humor (Whimsy)

Humor develops its essence only in a series of stages, the first of which appears as an immediate form, the natural disposition towards humor known as whimsy. The jolly person grasps the unity of the objective and the subjective comic while also expressing a general premonition of the contradiction hidden in the tangible situation before them. However, because they remain unconscious of the situation, they have neither reached the moral depth nor experienced the infinite pain that true humor presupposes. Therefore, the premonition of the contradiction leads neither to a deeper introspection nor to a true universality of thought, and so the feeling of infinite contradiction remains within the natural element of unbroken joviality.

Into this immediate pleasure, the harsher experience of general moral impurity and common evil, from which even the pleasurable subject cannot escape, must break in as a source of inner struggle. The youthful abundance of sensual well-being, however, prevents this from progressing beyond the initial attempt, and humor remains naive, experiencing only a fleeting glimpse of deeper contradiction. However, if this natural power, lacking self-restraint, leads to unscrupulous and crude indulgence, and if it produces other evils and ugliness as a result, then the demand for the destruction of the sublime seems to be negated. But at the same time, with this rupture of natural well-being, the subject turns inward and moral life awakens as conscience. However, the deterioration does not go so deep that it cannot find respite on the foundation of that first, not yet lost, natural pleasure through a constant interplay of self-aggrandizement and self-accusation.

####

The entire first stage of humor deteriorates because, although it carries out the process of its movement, it does so not with full awareness. Even the last form presented, although already more internal, is too preoccupied with itself to escape this fate. Therefore, if a pure personal unity is to arise in which all comic material liberates itself from itself, the first requirement is that the actual process be less crude and bothersome. The more delicately organized and inwardly cultivated personality is surprised by a nobler kind of unfreedom and seeks to free itself from the pressure of that weakness through self-mockery and teasing of others' weaknesses. This liberation, however, is itself still more a work of the self-reliance of natural health than of thinking consciousness. It therefore does not yet develop into the universality of comic consciousness, and in the case of deep entanglement, it suffers too much internally to be completely free.

But the deeper work of education also breaks this last vestige of naive self-reliance. The thinking subject turns inward and recognizes its own contradiction and that of the world in its cutting harshness by thinking it in its universality, but succumbs in the midst of attempting liberation from this pain. This is either because it is itself too deeply entangled in the contradiction in a real sense and works itself to pieces in morbid bitterness, or because, according to the now established inwardness of consciousness, it nurtures a self-tormenting thought within itself that invents disturbances that are not there, proliferates the really existing ones in a poetic way, and thus creates an infinite feeling of pain. Self-liberation stalls in both cases, and an unresolved despair remains at the power of the idea to assert itself in and through its contradictions through an unconquered anger. These are subjects who cannot overcome experience.

However, this reconciliation is itself flawed because it is an injustice against an injustice. In the sublime, the pure unity of the beautiful was negated by the Image, and in the comic, it was negated by the Idea, wherein the beautiful was meant to fulfill its image not under the condition of a break through ugliness, but seamlessly. Therefore, the comic cannot be the conclusion in which this movement finds its rest. Subjectivity,
which knows itself as all content, must therefore, in order to restore the idea to its rightful place
, release it again as objective power. But as soon as it reappears as such, it becomes sublime, and through this negation, once again  falls prey to the comic. This cannot move any further, but can only move back into its opposite that it is what it is, in order to arrive back at itself.

Since the sublime and the comic are two one-sidednesses that demand and condition each other, and neither can move anywhere but into the other, the requirement arises that the unity of must continue to move. This movement, however, is nothing other than simple beauty itself, or more accurately, that movement has now reached its end where it began. Each of the two moments in beauty asserted its right through the negation of the other, and in that each turned its right into injustice precisely through this negation of the other, thereby being driven back into its opposite. The conflict of between the opposites are extinguished by their original unity, which now reveals itself as the driving force that, as immanent to and active in each of the opposites, compelled it to move towards the other.

This original unity, however, has now become something else. It has unfolded the contradictions contained within it and returned to itself as its own result as a mediated or complete unity. As such, however, it is not a new, special form of beauty, but it is nothing other than the whole of beauty itself that is present precisely in these contradictions, passes through them, and returns to itself from them. This living unity is to be understood as the unity of the objective and the subjective, for the sublime excludes the Image from the Idea in its subjective contingency, while the comic excludes the Idea from the Image as objective power. If beauty is to move forward, it can only do so according to its concept, having been fulfilled by the unfolding of all its moments, moving beyond itself, that is, beyond the abstraction of its universality into the form of its immediate existence in art.
