# The Method of Philosophy

> This webpage is a direct translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Encyclopedia Logic* (1876) 

Having thoroughly grasped the object of philosophy, we must now also examine more closely its method, i.e. what it means to *do* philosophy. Just as the purpose of philosophy aims to bridge the divide between our inner self and the external world, leads us to *knowledge* as the object of philosophy, so too does the object of philosophy now lead us to its *method*, for the activity of this very thought, which thus rediscovers itself in the external world, also constitutes the very means by which we acquire knowledge. The purpose, object, and method of philosophy are, therefore, the one and the same absolutely free activity of thought. Thus, the distinction of philosophy from theology and the empirical sciences will become clear to us from yet another perspective, namely, that of its origin.

In religion, truth has come to us from externally through upbringing and instruction. Before we are even capable of rendering an account of our own representations and thoughts, religious conceptions have already taken shape within our minds. Religion has been revealed to us as something fixed and immutable, brought down from the heavens, something we dare not challenge or tamper with, but to which we must submit ourselves, although we may rightfully demand that it also prove its validity before our own inner consciousness, so that the testimony of the spirit may accord with it. Theology, the philosophy of religion, likewise proceeds from the *given*. Its scientific reasonings are contingent upon dogma, upon symbolic books, and so forth. Philosophy casts off every fetter of givenness, beginning from a standpoint of absolute presuppositionlessness. Since, in this way, it can attach itself to nothing external, it must ground itself solely out of itself and through itself. Thought precisely has this freedom, acknowledging nothing outside itself upon which it might depend. Thinking is the charter of nobility that makes man the free lord of creation and distinguishes him alone from the animal. Philosophy cannot begin with the bondage of belief, but must rather begin with the independence of doubt, detaching itself from all content that cannot be justified through itself alone. Beginning with Descartes, the proposition has rightfully been placed at the very forefront of philosophy: that one must doubt everything. Out of the negativity of absolute freedom, philosophy must construct its world of ideas.

Since philosophical thought is unbound from every higher revelation, so is it no less unbound from every lower one, it therefore relies neither upon experience nor the senses. What is true in things is not, in fact, what the senses present to us, but what we discover in them through our own thinking. This contemplative method of philosophy that generates the truth out of itself is called speculation or speculative thinking. Casting aside everything we previously possessed, even burning our very ships so that no return is possible to the abandoned shore, we plunge into the ethereal sea of ​​pure thought, to swim forth and see to what new shores this voyage will lead us. Thus, we now observe how the activity of thinking reveals itself to us as the only means of philosophy, a means that it discovers entirely within itself, and which it finds to be entirely sufficient. Thought creates itself, and as it develops, it ceaselessly generates new thoughts. This act of creation simultaneously generates the objective essence of things and the cognition of them. Being and knowing are thus one, and from time immemorial, truth has been articulated as this very identity of thought and being, of subject and object. 

The object that thought generates is thought itself and is absolutely immutable. These creative thoughts are not *our* thoughts, those we might arbitrarily conjure up within ourselves, rather, thought generates itself within us, and our sole task is to simply observe this inner unfolding, allowing it to take its course. Philosophical cognition is, therefore, a creation *ex nihilo* in the true sense of the word, just as theologians maintain that God created the world out of nothing. Philosophy thinks the thought of creation, and this very act of thinking is creation itself, just as in primordial creation, the Word of God is designated as the prime mover of the created world. 

The face of the philosopher is that joyful expression which the young Schelling still wore when he asserted the proposition: to philosophize about nature is to create it. If Schiller assigns to philosophical cognition only a subordinate position, then we shall indeed concede, along with Gabler, that this creative thinking is a *re-thinking*, not of a preceding conscious thought, but only of the unconscious thought of the world. And if, according to Werder's Logic, the limit of philosophy is defined by the claim that it cannot make a single blade of grass grow, then this very act of "making grass grow" is, in fact, a subordinate activity of unconscious nature, subordinate to the generation of the blade of grass *as a universal*, an act that originates from thought alone, subordinate to the self-conscious, generative concept of grass as a form of vegetative life.

Even if philosophy neither presupposes revelation nor experience, its relationship to them, in the course of its development, is by no means exclusively negative. Skepticism can therefore rightly be regarded as an excellent beginning, as an introduction to the study of philosophy, as much as the spirit renders itself the pure ground and origin of truth. While philosophy might appear irreligious, as Bacon observed that "a drink from the cup of philosophy leads man away from God, toward atheism", he nevertheless added, with equal accuracy, that "one must drain it to the very dregs in order to return to God once more." Philosophy by no means contradicts revelation, though it takes a different starting point from it, it ultimately arrives at the exact same result. The meaning that philosophy it assigns to dogma differs from the meaning immediately intended by revelation, but in so doing, revelation is thereby purified. The difference in form is, in this instance, also a difference in content. For philosophy transforms religious representations into philosophical concepts, and along this path, many prejudices are shed, and we are compelled to renounce many once cherished opinions as truth breaks through in its unadorned clarity. To sacrifice this truth to religious presupposition, elevating revelation to the status of a philosophical principle, as Schelling does, is nothing short of shameful apostasy to the freedom of philosophical inquiry, a regression to the centuries old era of Scholasticism.

Just as little as interfere with revelation, we interfere with experience. Instead, once that is given to the senses (being as independent from philosophy as revelation itself) has taken shape, and indeed, once the philosopher has been steeped in and nourished by both, we detach ourselves from them in order to see whether, through our own efforts, we can arrive at their results. We seek to demonstrate the validity of both Experience and Revelation. Philosophical thought consists of the free, self-generation of facts that were originally given through experience, reconceived as pure constructs of thought, such that truth resides precisely in the correspondence between what is thought and what is experienced. However, just as revelation must be cleansed of the dross of intuition, so must experience be cleansed of the dross of sensation. The senses veil the truth beneath a material husk, and it is thought alone, by identifying the universal essence in the sensuous matter, that refines it into a pure concept. Thus, experience and revelation are reconciled with philosophy in the exact manner described, and in this sense, we might allow them to stand within the framework of positive philosophy, although certainly not as foundational principles of philosophy. As a result, if philosophy is an exact science, then its "exactness" resides as much in the evident nature of thought itself as it does in the evidence of experience, and it is these two forms of exactness that must ultimately coincide.

However, to gain a closer acquaintance with philosophical thought, we must now compare it even more precisely with the methods of thinking found in the other sciences. Reason, understood as philosophical thought, is the reconciliation of subject and object to which we previously referred. If we investigate the etymology of the term, *Vernunft* (reason) signifies *Vernehmen* (apprehension), the act of apprehending or taking things up into oneself. Thinking, as Aristotle observes, touches upon things; and in this very contact, as if by magic wand, the object of thought is itself transformed into thought, just as Midas turned everything he touched into gold. To the extent that thought absorbs the essence of the object into itself, and relates only to itself in so doing, thinking is, according to Aristotle, the *thinking of thinking* or absolute thought, recognizing itself as all objectivity, the primordial essence of the universe. The object or material is itself the universal activity of thought, it is science, or as Aristotle puts it, the subject matter itself. This is precisely what religious conception implies when it interprets God’s words and thoughts as His very act of creation, as exemplified in the interpretation that Philo offers of that passage from the Old Testament: "God said, 'Let there be light,' and there was light."

If reason represents a return to the primordial state of the spirit, only traces of this state are present within sensation. The unity of the subjective and the objective, which was a *conscious* unity in the realm of thought, is an *unconscious* unity in the realm of sensation. For this reason, sensation constitutes the starting point, just as thought constitutes the culmination of the spirit’s self-activity. In sensation, for as long as I am only sensing, I and the object of my sensation are immediately one. I and Blue, I and Pain, I and Anger, etc. do not yet distinguish themselves from one another, and even Aristotle states that in actual sensation, the sensing subject and the sensed object coincide. Sensation is the discovery of the thing within my experience, and is therefore exact. Sensation never errs, so says Epicurus, only the one who interprets it falsely. However, since sensation is merely the natural unity of subject and object, animals live within this unity and do not transcend it. This primordial state is, however, not the highest, but rather the lowest, and the Paradise in which this initial state is said to have existed, according to the Persians, bears the meaning of a zoological garden. We must, therefore, step beyond this standpoint in order to attain the highest, conscious unity. If one were now inclined to posit feeling as the highest state, this would certainly find its justification in the fact that, within sensation, everything is still contained within it, as if it were a seed. However, this deficiency, however, lies precisely in the fact that it has not even begun its development.

The fragmentation of the opposites held together within the unity of sensation is now the province of the Understanding: the subjective mode of thought that exists solely within our heads, before which objects stand opposed and appear as something alien. It constitutes the intermediate stage between sensation and Reason. Setting out from and attaching itself to sense perception, it must tear apart the unity inherent in that perception in order to re-establish it in a higher mode. These opposites that have been torn apart is what Aristotle terms *tà ex aphairéseos*, what we would call the "abstract", whereas the "concrete", referring to either the immediate unity of sensation and to the mediated unity of Reason, Aristotle employs not (as is common in Latin) the image of "growing together," but rather that of "pouring together" (*tà synkechyména*). The Understanding begins its work precisely when, in the act of intuition, we distinguish our sensations from their objects, viewing the latter as the causes of the former. The objects present themselves as an infinite multitude of particulars, while the *I* stands as the enduring universal amidst their transience, accompanying all these diverse sensations, elevating them into its own universal categories, and thereby transforming them into mental representations. But at the same time, the Understanding explicitly posits these sensations as distinct from itself as existing externally to it, within the dimensions of space and time. The Understanding renders sensations objective by transforming them into independent, self-subsistent things. By elevating things into the abstract form of universality, it certainly does link them to the *I*. Nevertheless, despite this linkage, subject and object remain external to one another, rendering the connection between them a merely subjective one. According to Kantian terminology, the intellect allows only the qualities of things, the external aspect of their appearance, to be assimilated into the I. The "thing-in-itself", the true essence of things, remains on the outside. Yet this "thing-in-itself" that lies behind the veil of nature's interior, a veil that appears to be impenetrable to the intellect, is in truth, nothing other than an empty thought, a lifeless abstraction that exists solely within our minds, just as "matter" is merely the mental construct of an object. The true nature of things is their rational concept itself. The intellect fails to perceive this, it therefore remains arrested in an unresolved antinomy, fails to bridge the chasm between the I and the Infinite, and therefore becomes skeptical.

The Understanding, however, is necessary to Reason *because* it serves as its presupposition. For Reason to achieve its reconciliation through the resolution of the opposition, that very opposition must be completely developed in all its acuteness, and only then can the presupposed unity become a unity generated from within the spirit itself. True philosophical thinking is, therefore, simultaneously an understanding Reason and a rational Understanding, and only now do we fully grasp the truth of the proposition cited earlier that thinking heals the very wound it inflicts. Thinking requires the Understanding to open the rift and Reason to close it once again. Within this process, each side also retains its significance. Just as the rift is superseded, so too does it persist. For things are truly objective, not mere appearances, when they are absolutely independent of the subject while simultaneously remaining thoughts. Since the Realism of philosophy is absolute, its Idealism is likewise absolute. Each side of the opposition constitutes the totality of truth, and the reconciliation consists solely in the reconciliation of existing oppositions. Having returned to the exactitude of immediate, Reason becomes the self-conscious exactitude of experience.

We are now ready to set forth, in a systematic division, the system into which thought unfolds and differentiates itself. Logic, as the realm of pure thought, constitutes the first moment of this whole. But each moment is in-itself or implicitly the whole, for within each of these members, the Absolute Idea is mirrored at one of its specific stages, posited under the specific determinations characteristic of that stage. To philosophize means precisely this: to not conceive of anything in its isolation, but to discern the whole even in the smallest detail. However, if Logic depicts the creative Idea within pure thought, before its activity erupts into the reality of the world, then Hegel characterized this by stating that Logic represents God prior to the creation of Nature and finite Spirit. However, this must not be interpreted as though Logic preceded Nature and the human spirit in terms of time, nor as though thought once existed independently of reality hypostatized within God as a personal being. Instead, since Reason exists only to the extent that it actively and eternally creates itself, the Absolute Idea and its products can only be separated in thought. This "prior" status is simply a priority of deduction, since in reality, both exist in an inseparable unity. Thus, from a different point of view, the eternity of creation arises even here.

The second moment of the system constitutes the opposition to this state of the Absolute Idea being "enveloped within itself": it is the Idea's realization in the external world. The first *Diakosmos* remains within itself, as Proclus puts it, while the second proceeds from it. The Absolute Idea posits itself within the element that is other to its own nature, namely, in Matter, even though Matter itself is a product of the Idea. The Idea, having divested and alienated itself, is Nature. As the Idea existing within the element of otherness, it immediately harbors contradiction within itself. Nature is thought, just as Anaxagoras observed long ago, and Reason resides within Nature as surely as it does within animate beings. But this Reason constitutes only the core and the inner essence of Nature. In its appearance, Nature is beyond thought, though the thought that is veiled within its interior constantly seeks to break free from its material prison and restore itself from its loss.

Thirdly, this occurs in the Spirit, wherein Nature returns to the Idea. Because the path was first traversed from Above to Below, so that Thought might posit itself as all Being, now Matter strives from Below to Above, in order to elevate itself to Thought. Nature achieves this in the human brain, the blossom of natural creation, as Schelling puts it. Within the substance of the brain, Matter rises to the universality and fluidity of Thought. In truth, Spirit is co-eternal with the moments or genera that bring it forth. Spirit is Thought actualizing itself within Nature, constituting the absolute interconnection of Thought and Nature. In Spirit, Nature liberates itself from the form of Otherness, and Thought, by superseding Nature’s fragmentation, is reconciled with itself as self-consciousness. Spirit is the highest form of the Idea, whereas Nature constitutes its lowest form of existence. Spirit shares the strengths of Logic and Nature, while having superseded their weaknesses. In Logic, the purity of the Absolute Idea is preserved, but does not yet exist for itself at all. In Nature, the Absolute Idea exists for itself in existence, but at the cost of the purity of Thought. Neither Logic nor Nature *think themselves*, they are both simply Thought. Spirit is the Idea that thinks itself, the Idea that preserves its purity in its very act of existence. However, since Spirit acquires this correspondence only through its own conscious activity, its sciences unfold in several stages from finite to absolute Spirit, ranging from psychology, law, aesthetics, theology, and so forth.

In the place of the introduction to philosophy provided in this preface, Hegel once posited the magnificent edifice of the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, aiming to guide ordinary consciousness, starting from sense certainty and passing through the manifestations of Spirit, all the way to the absolute knowledge of Logic. We have nothing to add to the lush, shimmering hues of that work, but Hegel himself later abandoned this propaedeutic to the system, beginning directly with the Logic instead.
