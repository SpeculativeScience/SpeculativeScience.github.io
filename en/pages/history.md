## Ancient Law

The initial overcoming of the arbitrariness of the individual is effected by the superior power of the universal spirit—which confronts it and, because it still appears to the individual as something alien, manifests itself in a religious form. This constitutes the standpoint of the East, wherein all law—qua the taming of arbitrariness—is divine law (*jus divinum*). This arbitrariness, having absorbed the universal into itself, appears secondly—upon the foundation of this universal spirit—as the realm of political freedom. The universal element that inwardly animates every individual, rendering the public as the embodiment of the national spirit, is the free state. In Greece, all law is public law (*jus publicum*). Finally, in Rome, the individual grasps the universal essence of law in the context of their own individuality, advancing as a legal and moral person from divine and public law (the starting point) toward private law (*jus privatum*). Thus, while cosmopolitan law appeared in our exposition of the archetype of law as the final stage, it is the first stage of the history of law. Conversely, that which is logically primary—private law—proves to be one of the most recent products of legal history. The intersection, struggle, and final reconciliation of these three spheres of law, which initially arose in chronological succession, constitutes the legal history that follows in Europe and America.
