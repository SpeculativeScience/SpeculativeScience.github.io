# Politics

The greatest union of people now constitutes the state, where the personality of an entire people finds its expression. The first element here is the internal organization of the life of the people as a self-contained polity, i.e. constitutional law. Secondly, there is the relationship between peoples who stand external to one another as persons, i.e. international law. Thirdly, the people return from this state of "otherness" to a state of "being-for-itself," in that within the specific state, the individual rises above the particularity of the various peoples through their mutual interaction to attain the universality of humanity, i.e. cosmopolitan law.

## Constitutional Law

Since the state the highest stage of the existence of freedom that we have hitherto ascended, the particular interests of the families, professions and associations of the people have been superseded. In the true state, the universal will is mediated through the particular wills of the many associations and through the will of individuals themselves. Hence, while the state appears to arise from the family and civil society, it actually constitutes the foundation upon which they rest. Thus, the state must be the last subject to be submitted to our examination of public law, for that which is the richest entity, encompassing within itself all lower forms can only be truly comprehended once the lower forms have been fully explained as its antecedents. But to demonstrate how the state embodies the interconnection of the universal, the particular, and the individual, we shall begin by elucidating its general concept before proceeding to examine its specific details.

While at the highest moment of private law, we already observed the formation of families in the pursuit of a common ethical purpose, these individuals had not yet attained true freedom. The family represented their freedom in the external sphere, whereas internally, they remained subject to paternal authority. In the realm of voluntary associations, extending even to the wider community, the members did indeed evolve into free persons within the context of those groups, yet without forfeiting their unity of ethical purpose, for they came to recognize their fellow members as brothers. Nevertheless, the common objective of these associations remained centered upon particular needs, i.e. material well-being of the associates and their families. This knot of particularity does, however, have its own justification. Indeed, it serves as the first rung intended to elevate us, acting as the connecting link, towards a more universal interest. For particularity, being itself a specific form of universality, inherently encompasses universality within itself. It is precisely through the particular interests of associational life that the public life of the state is formed, to the extent that these interests are rooted in the universal interest. Consequently, while the individual acting as a private citizen may believe they are just pursuing the interests of their family and social standing, the universal spirit of the people reveals itself as their true essence. It is the state that first brings this "universal man", hitherto potentially present in every individual, into actual reality by broadly delineating the image of reason, albeit limited by within the specific particular of the nation.

If one then inquires into the specific content of the state's purpose, the principle of the division of labor, much as in the case of the aforementioned associations, once again comes into play. For the state is also particular association when viewed in relation to humanity as a whole. Every nation has a distinct mission to fulfill within the course of world history such that the archetype of universal humanity is completely realized in all its purity. While the precise nature of this mission only becomes fully apparent through the study of the universal history of law, given that the well-being of the whole constitutes the highest law, all subordinate objectives of political life must yield precedence to it. However it is precisely the task of constitutional law to discover the specific form in which the ultimate reconciliation of all particular interests with the universal interest may be effected, a form in which the many particular interests continue to exist and flourish within the universal interest. To elevate the public welfare to a universal principle of constitutional law without regard for the particular rights dissolves the entire constitution into the particular perspective of a single party.

Furthermore, it cannot be denied that the state has a definite relationship to nature. Admittedly, the state does not possess a physical body since the physical body of each individual uniquely distinguishes them as an individual. Instead, the spirit of a people possesses an invisible existence in the minds of individuals, it becomes one person precisely because all individuals feel themselves to be one within it, specifically by knowing their own finite personalities to be subsumed and preserved within the universal personality of a people. Nevertheless, the geography of a people can be safely designated as its body, one that is just as imperishable as its spirit, and one that receives the mortal remains of individuals after their death. While the individual spirit, during its lifetime, acts on behalf of the universal spirit of a people through its deeds, it also seeks, through the enduring consequences of its deeds, to be immortalized within the life of the people, just as the individual, in a physical sense, desires to be buried in the soil of their native land. 

The concept of the state, as set forth in the preceding discussion, must now realize itself in the actual organization of the state. We term this organization its constitution, the spirit of a people born into its true body. Like every living being, the state forms a logical syllogism. In accordance with the three figures of the syllogism, the logical relationships of life are itself threefold, in that as life relates first to itself, then to another living being, and finally to its own species. Here, within the sphere of constitutional law, we are concerned solely with the people’s relationship to itself, the process of self-formation that falls under the rubric of the first figure (I-P-U). The individual (I) is united with the universal spirit of the people (U) through the medium of their particular profession (P). Yet precisely by virtue of the interconnection of the three terms within the universal spirit of the people, this spirit encompasses these terms as the very activities that characterizes its life.

The first element, the universal foundation upon which the entire state rests, is the articulation of its will, that is, its freedom in general. The primary activity of a people thus consists in giving voice to its will, and this is the function of legislation. Through its laws, the people demonstrates how it has realized the legal reason at the specific historical stage it currently occupies. This activity of formulating the universal guiding principles by which all things within the body politic are to be governed (U) is the people's faculty of understanding. Just as blood circulates through the entire body of the animal, so too is the law the general will that flows through every vein of the nation. Secondly, all the particular spheres of national life (agriculture, industry, commerce, courts, art, religion, and science) must constantly be referred back from their individual instances, to their lawful particularity (P), and to their determinate universality. This subsumption of the individual instance to its particular law is, pre-eminently, an act of judgment, and so we may compare this activity of the national life to the faculty of judgment. Thirdly, once all events within the state have thus been theoretically referred back to the law, the task then becomes to carry the law into execution in the individual instance, and so this constitutes the other extreme term of the syllogism (I) that we may call the will of the people. These three terms then combine, in turn, to form three judgments. Like the justice system, legislation relates the particular to the universal (P-U), drawing upon the customary law derived from administrative decisions (I-P) in order to make it the content of written law to be executed (I-U). Since the time of Aristotle, this intermediate power has been designated as the judicial power, yet this designation arbitrarily isolates the administration of law from civil society, which we have already rejected as illegitimate.

These three activities are essential to the people, and none of them can be absent if a state is to actually exist. The only question is what the relationship between them should be, whether they are to be united within a single organ, or distributed among different organs. Here it must be stated that, just as the most imperfect form of animal life is that in which all vital functions are still united within a single organ (such as the polyp, which is essentially nothing more than a stomach), so too is the most rational state that in which each activity possesses its own distinct organ. Each organ is as autonomous as it is conscious of its unity with the whole. By virtue of this autonomy, each organ is termed a "power". However, the term "power" can easily reintroduce the misconception that the state is a mechanism, one that rests upon a "balance of powers", wherein each power is obliged to check the others as much as possible to prevent any encroachment upon its own sphere. This would reduce the life of the state to a futile game of seesaw, one that would indefinitely delay all action and allow vital activity to stagnate, as so often occurs in modern constitutional states. The necessary complement to the autonomy of these powers is their ethical unity. This is not to suggest that a civic-minded disposition is sufficient, for such a disposition remains something purely subjective, lacking any firm, objective basis. While it is true that, even in a written constitution, the rights and functions of every branch of government must be precisely delineated, this itself constitutes a significant safeguard for the people's liberty. But a mere piece of paper does not suffice on its own unless the members of the body politic also recognize themselves as united in their spirit. Hence, it is only through the mutual interconnection of the written constitution and the civic spirit that the concept of political life is perfected.

This ethical quality consists in the fact that, while each branch of government seeks to preserve its own independence, it simultaneously acknowledges and fully respects the independence of the others. To this end, the boundaries of each branch must be demarcated as strictly as possible, and each must operate with authority within its own sphere. This does not, however, preclude it from having aspects and extensions through which it relates to the others, provided that it does not compromise their absolute autonomy. In conventional constitutions, legislative power is distributed among two chambers and the executive branch. It is from this arrangement that conflict, friction, and discontent arise, such as when one chamber rejects what the other has approved, or when the sovereign exercises its right of veto against the will of both chambers. On top of this preponderance of power, the executive branch then possesses the additional right to appoint administrators, a prerogative that places the entire administrative power in its hands. The English system of so-called "parliamentary government" offers only a feeble counterweight to this situation, to the extent that parliament participates in governance only to the extent that it holds the power to withdraw its majority support from a ministry and compelling the Crown to appoint different ministers. By contrast, the actual separation of powers guarantees that the legislature need not interfere at all with the administration, which is entirely independent of the executive branch, and can instead leave to the latter full responsibility for its actions, making parliamentary government logically superfluous.

If we now inquire into the nature of the organs through which the three powers are exercised, it goes without saying that legislation, as the general will of the people, is the concern of all, whether directly or indirectly. Administration, as the faculty of judgment, demands the specific insight into the particular case at hand. Thus, it is a select few, the most insightful individuals in each respective field, who must stand at the head of the administration. The act of the will, however, belongs to an individual person who stands at the head of the executive power. Within these three powers, the three forms of judgment regarding the essence of a thing, the individual, the particular, and the universal, are recapitulated. And so the three forms of constitution posited by Aristotle have each become distinct aspects of the true constitution. Yet the true constitution does not, in fact, consist of such separate parts at all, precisely because the one-sided constitutional forms have themselves been transformed into living members of the whole.

### Legislative Power

With regard to the legislative power, as with the other two powers, we must speak firstly of the functions of its activity, secondly of the persons who are tasked with exercising them, and finally of the arrangements that will guarantee that the independence of each power will be preserved and not encroach upon the independence of the others.

#### Legislative Functions

The functions of the legislative power are, first: the articulation of the general will in the form of law, second, in relation to the administrative power, the granting of the means that the people provide for the fulfillment of the state's purpose, and third, in relation to the executive power, the determination of the size of the military force, which must be regarded as the arm of executive power.

##### Legislation

The legislative power, as Hegel puts it (*Philosophy of Right*, § 273), is not called upon to recreate the state anew, but exists within a legally constituted order, so its task is to further develop its laws in accordance with the principles enshrined in the constitution. In this regard, it is to be noted that the constitution and the legal code exist only as they are constantly being further developed, for the failure to legislate would constitute the regression and death of the state. But if the state has been destroyed by a revolution, then this power is certainly tasked with rebuilding it upon a new foundation. In such a case, one may certainly say that assemblies specifically convened for this purpose create the constitution anew, such as the two French Constituent Assemblies of 1789 and 1848, and the German Assemblies of 1848, whereas in 1830, the office of establishing the constitution was by chance entrusted to the regular legislative body in France. When it comes to the question of who possesses the right to propose legislation, the answer is that it is the people, because they, after all, articulate their general will through the law, they must also have the right to amend it. The basis for such amendment lies in the exigencies of specific cases, for within any sphere of social life, circumstances may evolve in such a way that an existing legal provision becomes obsolete and that new legal provision becomes necessary to regulate it. The legislative power itself, whose very function is the creation of law, may very well perceive this need. However, the desire for legal amendment may be felt even more keenly by the administrative and executive powers, which stands in closer proximity to the specific case and experiences the need most directly, whether domestic or foreign. Finally, the people as such also possess the right of proposal in the form of addressing petitions to the legislative power, though the latter retains the authority of decision in the legislative process. Moreover, the legislative power also has the exclusive right to interpret the laws, since it is the body that enacted them in the first place, though it is possible that a judge may assert their own interpretation in the course of adjudication until the legislature officially finalizes its own interpretation. 

##### Finance Law

As for finance law, the second means by which the legislative power relates to the administration, it will be greatly simplified in our state. This is because funds will need to be appropriated only for general state affairs, whereas every particular association, since it manages its own affairs, must raise the necessary funds itself. However, just as the community, the municipality, or the province must provide whatever the individual cannot accomplish alone, so too will the state ultimately step in to assist and grant support upon request. Since every tariff barrier and indirect tax is objectionable, the only option that remains is a progressive income tax, under which every association is assessed according to the percentage rate corresponding to their income, for the purposes of state, provincial, and municipal taxes alike.

The general rule governing every finance law, however, is to first determine the necessary expenditures, and then determine the revenues, that is, the proportionate share that each association contributes from their income. If Hegel terms finance law strictly a matter of government (Works, Vol. VII, 2, § 544, pp. 417–419) on the grounds that such a law is not, like the budget, authorized annually, then his assessment of the matter is entirely correct. Indeed, he confirms that, within finance law, the legislative power stands in a specific relation to the administrative power. However, if in doing so he contends against "the high-sounding title of budget authorization" by noting that the whole budget cannot be contested, but that its primary components must be regarded as permanent and that only the smallest fraction is of a variable nature, then with this very proposition, he thoroughly paves the way for our own reactionaries who also want to eliminate the parliamentary government In the possession of such coercive measures, Hegel purports to have discerned a merely mechanical conception of the relationship, and to demonstrate how futile such measures are, he advances the claim that, in such a scenario, the state itself would be in shambles, with no true government amidst a fragmented assemblage of political parties. He has not even the slightest inkling that the modern constitutional monarchy is, in its very essence, a *party* government, much like that of the English Tories and Whigs (a reality that we, regrettably, observe everywhere), and is often the ruled by a rather small, albeit powerful, political party. The only means of preventing the living spirit of the people from being extinguished in the midst of this actually existing mechanism is to bring first one party and then another to power through , in order to minimally achieve the right middle ground and balance, although through a system of swings from one extreme to the other. In our state, government is not a party matter, so there arises no problem of balances or a seesaw system. Instead, through the living unity of the members of state life, parliamentary government has been superseded by the self-government of the people, whose will the executive power only has to execute.

##### Military Spending

Apart from financial contributions, it would seem that only one other specific activity could be required of citizens: namely, national defense, since this is a matter of an entirely universal nature. Therein lies, thirdly, the relationship between the legislative power and the executive power. Even here, it must be stated that the legislature is required to appropriate the necessary funds each year in order to determine the size of the armed forces. These forces can then be raised through voluntary recruitment, since in peacetime, the army is intended to maintain public security. But even in times of war, there is the implicit assumption that able-bodied citizens will come forward in sufficient numbers to defend their homeland. If there are not enough recruits in the event of an offensive war, or if the legislative power refuses to appropriate the necessary funds, then such a war lacks popular support and should not be pursued. The legislative power cannot wage war, even if it were inclined to appropriate the necessary funds, and conversely, the executive power is unable to wage war unless the funds are actually appropriated to them. But the most rational outcome would be, as it is envisioned as the ultimate goal of world history, that no one would any need to give their assent to war, because war in general, being inherently irrational, will have become impossible.

#### Legislative Personnel

As concerns the persons comprising the legislative power, the most immediate and natural conception is that all adult citizens assemble in primary assemblies to vote on the laws themselves. If unanimity prevails, then the general will is also the will of all, and is certainly also the rational will, however one might still argue that even an entire people can be swept away by passion, as may have easily befallen the easily excitable Athenians. Nevertheless, in the event of a consensus of votes, the general will (the law itself) remains always indubitable, precisely because it is the will common to all. However, if the general popular assembly divides into a majority and a minority, this does not mean the general will is lost, for even though it is no longer the will of all, it remains the rational will. The question is by what criterion this is to be recognized. Now, since the majority stands closer to the totality, the rational will is to be presumed to reside within it, and even if with less certainty than in the totality, still with greater probability than in the minority. The rule of the majority is an inference of induction. If Rousseau characterizes the rule of the majority as an oppression of the minority, it nonetheless remains the sole means by which, in fulfilling the task of the state, arbitrariness is elevated to freedom. The minority must regard itself as the element of arbitrariness, it must therefore submit to the decision of the majority, which is to be deemed the more rational one, and in a spirit of free ethicality, assist in its implementation. However, in principle it is certainly conceivable that the minority might be in the right against the majority, where "the smaller, the sounder" (*minora saniora*), as Mr. Trendelenburg notes. If higher reason truly lies on the side of the minority, then the latter have to convince the majority through rational arguments and gradually grow into the majority itself, as is frequently the case.

However, the fact that the majority of citizens makes the decision themselves is only possible in small states, since we have already had to declare it inadmissible in large municipalities. The retention of popular referendums held in public forums, after Rome had expanded to encompass the known world, was one of the primary causes of its decline. A resolution born of calm deliberation and prudent counsel requires a smaller assembly. As a result, the idea of ​​convening the entire populace to decide is self-evidently precluded. The people can only dispatch plenipotentiaries or representatives to deliberate and make decisions on their behalf. It is here that the maxim "the smaller, the sounder" (*minora saniora*) finds its true significance, for the more rational minority consists of the "best" citizens, selected from among the populace by the vote of the majority. To condemn majority rule on the grounds that popular passions would inevitably gain the upper hand even within a general assembly is merely a pretext for Bonapartist despotism.

One major difficulty lies in determining how the representatives of the people are to arise directly from the people's own election. Here, a wide variety of procedures have been proposed. The most rational approach is that, since the people as such are to generate the general will from within themselves, they should also step up to the ballot box as such. Specifically, they should vote by secret ballot, as this constitutes the surest means of countering any undue influence. Since every particular interest must yield precedence during the enactment of laws, the people will vote on a per capita basis. Depending on the size of the state, this means that there would be one delegate for approximately every 30,000 to 70,000 inhabitants, ensuring that the assembly does not exceed a total membership of 400 to 500. Electoral districts are to be established in such a way that, following the organizational structure of districts, municipalities, and provinces, various consolidated and individual municipalities are combined, and large cities are subdivided into the requisite number of electoral precincts to achieve the required population quota. It is preferable that each electoral district have its own designated deputy, as is the case in municipal elections, so that the district may appear before the assembly as a distinct political entity or autonomous corporate body. It is not sufficient merely to ensure that no independent (*sui juris*) adult citizen is excluded from being elected, they must also be eligible to stand for office. Furthermore, universal suffrage must be *direct*, not mediated through the intermediate stages of primary voters and electors, thereby diluting the original vote. The representative of the people must by chosen directly from the mass of the people themselves, for they are intended to embody their general will in its undivided integrity. One objection that has been raised against direct elections is that unless one opts for a more restricted form of election, rather than the very broad ones currently in use, the result will be either minority elections or merely relative majority elections. The candidate who garners the most votes is declared the winner, even if the remaining candidates, taken together, have received a greater total number of votes. By contrast, it is argued that absolute majorities are more easily achieved through an electoral college, but this is a serious misconception. For restricted elections remain, in essence, minority elections, as much as the candidates advanced to the final round were themselves nominated by the minority. Thus, minority elections are entirely unavoidable, and relative majority elections are, by their very nature, always of this kind. But the people truly desire an election of absolute majority, they must cease to divide themselves into parties, for only then will they easily be able, by overwhelming majority, to identify the most worthy candidate within their electoral district.

A profound mistrust has arisen regarding universal suffrage, largely because Napoleon III, who strives to reconcile all extreme opposites, has succeeded in bringing together both despotism and universal suffrage. Through a system of prefectural administration and rule by the sword, he managed to restore the French Empire and to annex Savoy and Nice to France, even against the will of the people. This distortion reaches its absolute extreme when, in the name and by means of the general will, that very will is treated with utter contempt. This phenomenon can be explained, at least in part, by the fact that the rational will, which is fragmented among various parties and nations, finds itself captured by his personality. Thus, his uncle also considered himself the truer representative of the people or the entire nation, whereas he regarded the elected deputies merely as representatives of their respective constituencies. For the Bonapartist invariably relies upon universal suffrage to elevate themselves to power. Their downfall is inevitable the moment that, in Europe just as in America, the general will truly manifests itself, unaltered and undivided, through the universal vote. When the people themselves have seized the initiative, rather than entrusting the substance of their will to an authorized individual, they make the elected individual merely the executive agent and formal head to whom the people themselves dictate the content of their resolutions.

After all that has been said, it is evident that the rational form of representation for the legislative power is the unicameral system. The arguments cited in favor of the bicameral system are based on external contingencies, such as the utility of a dual deliberation, given that the deliberation of a single body could be prone to error. To counter this objection, one can simply introduce multiple rounds of voting within a single chamber. Indeed, a second vote is already mandated in cases of constitutional amendments, such a vote tha is repeated after a certain interval, or one that requires a two-thirds or three-quarters majority. For instance, in England, every ordinary bill undergoes three readings. However, if this dual deliberation is intended to involve the consultation of distinct bodies, regardless of how those bodies are constituted, it inevitably obscures the unity of the people, who ought to express their general will as a single, unified entity. 

Any configuration of the bicameral system completely destroys this unity of the people and is therefore pernicious. For example, in the Belgian system, although both chambers are elective bodies, the Senate is composed exclusively of those subject to higher taxation, thereby driving a wedge, based on the social disparity between greater and lesser wealth, into the political unity of the people. Here, the second argument advanced in favor of the bicameral system comes into play: namely, that within the legislative process there must exist one element that is static and moderating, and another that is dynamic and restlessly forward-looking. This concept notably formed the basis for the Council of Ancients and the Council of Five Hundred under the French Directory. However, it is evident that for this dichotomy to be fruitful, it must manifest *within* a single assembly. For if it is distributed across two separate assemblies, each possessing an absolute veto power over legislation, it is clear that should the two houses fail to reach an agreement, nothing will be accomplished. As a result, the moderating and steadfast body gains the upper hand, as it alone has the power to impede progress, whereas the more vibrant and dynamic body cannot, on its own, bring the progress it envisions to fruition.

Any legislative Senate always bears an aristocratic tinge, as much as legislation is, after all, intended to be democratic in nature. In Europe, we still observe, to a greater or lesser extent, the rule of the aristocracy, even if it is merely an aristocracy of wealth. Since a House of Nobles is objectionable from the standpoint of reason, the question as to whether the equality of heirs may be infringed upon by a right of primogeniture for the benefit of the state, must now, from the perspective of constitutional law, be answered with a most emphatic negative. The fact that in Prussia, through an electoral law established in an unconstitutional manner, the aristocracy of wealth (*timocracy*) has, by means of the three-class system of primary electors, infiltrated even the House of Deputies and remained the root cause of eighteen years of regression. The House of Lords sprouted entirely from the soil of medieval feudal law, and it was only after the various strata of wealth had all united in desiring democratic progress has the impropriety of this electoral law been neutralized. But regrettably, the House of Lords remains as a stumbling block of the utmost difficulty to remove, for it possesses the absolute capacity to obstruct every form of progress, and indeed makes the fullest possible use of this power. This is all the more so because the Crown, which alone would be capable of altering the House through the mass creation of peers, has come to believe that it has instead achieved the definitive entrenchment of this House upon its feudal foundations.

In connection with the alleged advantages of the bicameral system, a third argument is advanced: namely, that it enables the representation of the various interests existing within the populace. The Austrian Constitution of 1860 explicitly declared this to be its objective, providing for the representation of the landowners and the "intelligentsia", as it is commonly termed, in the Upper House, while assigning the representation of smaller landed property and the industrious classes to the Lower House. However, these various interests are nothing other than the *estates* of civil society. Thus, we have arrived at the question of whether the *estates* constitutes the true constitutional order, that is, whether the estates of civil society are also the estates entitled to political rights within the state, just as the legislative assembly itself is not infrequently referred to as the "Estates." This institutional arrangement originated in the Middle Ages, a period in which the distinct interests of the various estates constituted the primary force in political life, and the sovereign could only govern through private agreements with them. Each estate granted specific funds to the sovereign and in return, sought to secure particular privileges for itself. The estates consisted of the nobility, the clergy, and the third estate, i.e. the burghers, whereas in regions where the peasantry had been liberated from serfdom, a fourth estate of the farmers was included. In England, essentially the very same system prevails, save only that nobility and clergy sit together in the Upper House, while representatives of the counties and boroughs sit together in the Lower House. Hegel, like Montesquieu, regards the English constitution almost as the archetype of a constitution, and consequently integrates the estates into the state as well. However, he grants them only a minor share in the legislative power, alongside the government and the King (*Philosophy of Right* § 301).

However, in terms of scope and terms of nature, with Mr. Trendelenburg following in his footsteps, Hegel diminishes this share of the "estates-based authority" (Works, Vol. VII, 2, § 544), by attributing to it only a limited horizon, while ascribing to the ministers alone the overview of the whole, such that it is the ministers who are supposed to bring about the very best in legislation. But in truth, to borrow one of Hegel's own expressions,this amounts to giving legislation "the itch merely so that one may have the opportunity to scratch it." However, in the face of the law, particular interests ought to fall silent. An assembly that arises from the entire people through universal suffrage and direct election, wherein its representatives are elected solely as individual free citizens, will possess precisely that overview and free perspective on the general interest that Hegel only recognizes within the government. On the contrary, we shall see that the government, precisely because it functions as a *party* government, is ensnared to its own particular interests, and that it is the legislative power alone that is tasked with discovering the general interest amidst all the particular interests. Nor, conversely, are legislators mere proxies for their constituencies, for local interests are no more entitled to exclusive precedence than societal interests. Within the community or the municipality, local interests find significant representation, so it must be regarded as a perversion of the legislative process when Louis Philippe sought to secure a parliamentary majority by promising individual deputies that he would give due consideration to their local interests. However, elected representatives have an ethical obligation to resign their mandate if they no longer consider themselves to be aligned with their local constituency.

The legislative power we have established is, therefore, what has in recent times been called a constitution resting upon the broadest possible foundation. If this power, acting as the *terminus major* (U) that forms the middle term in the third figure (P-U-I), then this constitutes the true logical conclusion of political life, as much as all powers must ultimately emanate from the people as such. For this reason, we have chosen to begin with it, precisely because the Law, as we have stated, is the true sovereign. Conversely, Hegel began with the "princely" power of the executive, for in his view, it constitutes the foundation and the decisive apex of the entire structure of the state, the ultimate decision arrived at after deliberation has oscillated back and forth (*Philosophy of Right*, § 275). In other words, he has constructed the pyramid with the apex as its foundation, leaving the administration and the legislature, the middle section and the broadest base, to hover atop this sharp point. Indeed, in his very latest exposition, specifically in the third edition of the Encyclopedia (Works, VII, 2, p. 417), this "ultimate decision" is even designated as "absolute", such that the "monarchy of estates," as conceived by Hegel, is in reality a system in which the estates are reduced to a merely advisory role. This is precisely as Frederick II had envisioned a century earlier and what Frederick William III brought into being in 1822 by granting a "provincial estates constitution", despite the fact that he had previously promised a national assembly of estates endowed with decisive voting powers seven years prior. It is probably true that a government will find it difficult to act against the voice of the people's representatives, even if they are only advisors. However, there is a vast difference between whether the necessity of submitting to their decision constitutes a written law or a moral obligation. A truly rational constitution lies in the requirement that the demands of ethicality are enshrined in a written document — a document delineating the rights of the people. This is precisely what Frederick William IV refused to accept in 1840, yet ultimately swore an oath to uphold in 1850.

#### Legislative Safeguards

The safeguards for and against the legislative power are greatly simplified in our constitution, precisely because the separation of powers eliminates encroachments by one branch upon another far more effectively than is the case where such matters are solely regulated by statutes. Nevertheless, even here, every branch of government must still be surrounded by certain institutional mechanisms that legally secure its independence against any encroachment by the others.

##### Public Transparency

The first of the safeguards for and against the legislative branch is the public nature of its proceedings. Through this, the people are constantly apprised of the actions of their representatives and are thereby enabled to demand their accountability in electoral assemblies, which may convene for this very purpose at any time. Under the watchful eyes of the people, their elected representatives will at all times strive to prove themselves worthy of this trust, and thus the people themselves, much as is the case with the public nature of the justice system, will come to recognize the common cause as one that is both known to and desired by all. This public transparency then redounds to the immense benefit of the representatives as well, for they know that it enables them to remain in constant harmony with the people, while simultaneously providing them with a firm foundation of support for their public actions.

##### Public Opinion

The second, higher, outcome generated by public transparency is public opinion. When the people participate in public proceedings, they will grow accustomed to reflecting upon public affairs and exerting influence upon them through the medium of public opinion. True public opinion represents the highest pinnacle of ethical sentiment, for within it, every individual, guided by their most deeply held personal convictions, internalizes the very purpose of the state within themselves. However, since the element of chance is always present, public opinion may itself waver back and forth amidst the strife of political parties. Thus, public opinion finds its most substantive expression only in the decisions of the legislative assembly. The endorsement of the general opinion constitutes the surest guarantee of the popular legitimacy of these decisions, just as these decisions simultaneously attain their greatest stability when they are rooted in public opinion. When ministers declared their intention to disregard public opinion, such an attitude can be deemed rational on the one condition that they possess a vision of progress toward which they have a well-founded hope of elevating the people. 

##### Freedom of the Press

In England, where so much is said about public opinion and the weight it carries, *The Times* has all but secured for itself the prerogative of shaping public opinion. Apart from the legislative assembly, it is primarily in the press that the public opinion finds its expression. The free press constitutes the third safeguard acting both for and against the legislative power, and as a result, it has been termed the "fourth estate" within the state (with far greater justification than Mr. Trendelenburg applied to the standing army). In all free constitutions, therefore, the imposition of censorship and restrictions upon the press is prohibited. Indeed, it would be peculiar, in cases involving crimes committed through the written word, to pursue a path of prevention *prior* to the act, rather than the path of punishment *after* the crime has been committed, as much as one would not confiscate every knife in existence to prevent murder, and so forth. Likewise, even the application of criminal provisions against the press must be approached with the utmost caution. In England and North America, the discussion of public affairs is permitted in the most unrestricted manner possible, and should individuals feel aggrieved, they retain the right to sue for defamation. Public figures and prominent personalities, however, deem it beneath their dignity to respond to attacks by the press in any manner other than through their own speeches and actions. Generally speaking, the press itself serves as the most effective remedy against the press. The representatives of the people safely allow it free rein, as much as public opinion finds an even more authentic expression within the proceedings of the assembly itself than it does in the press. The anxiety with which governments of young constitutional states refuse to protect the press merely removes, as has been observed, the safety valve that prevents the sudden bursting of the boiler, thereby offering no safeguard against an outbreak of popular indignation. No less striking is the situation in which the administration (run by the ministers) requests funds from the legislature, via the budget, in order to create its own press that may very well be hostile to the legislative assembly itself. In our state of reason, however, such an occurance would be impossible. For within the state of reason, there exists no administration hostile to the legislature, as much as each branch of government always sweeps before its own door and never encroaches upon the domain of the others. Associations of scholars and the like possess their own specialized academic journals, periodicals, and so forth, that may naturally inform public policy with their articles. The influence of the administration upon the legislature must always remain purely advisory, never as an influence that obstructs or impedes legislative power.

##### Right to Petition

Fourthly, if we add to the freedom of press the right of popular assemblies (meetings) to convene, for example, to generate petitions bearing many signatures regarding a specific matter currently under legislative deliberation, we have thereby identified the ultimate expression of public opinion. However, it goes without saying that, unlike in the era of the French Revolution, petitioners are not permitted to appear in person, much less with weapons in hand, before the legislative assembly to impose their wishes upon their representatives, as it were, at the point of their pikes.

##### Right of Adjournment

The purpose of the fifth safeguard must address instances where the legislative power fails to express true public opinion, to which early constitutional states have answered that the executive power has the right to dissolve the legislature. It is argued that this right holds significance only to the extent that the executive power, being itself a participant in the legislative process, seeks to ascertain whether or not the general assembly constitutes the true expression of the general will. Thus, even under this premise, the dissolution of the assembly serves no other purpose than to act as an appeal to the people to arbitrate between the two powers. Unfortunately, dissolution is all too often employed as a means to stifle the true will of the people, and by way of electoral manipulation by the party currently at the helm, to secure for itself an artificial majority within the representative body. Should it succeed in this endeavor, it boasts loudly that the country stands behind it. Should it fail, however, it imputes to the victorious popular majority the very electoral manipulations it itself committed, and speaks, in the manner of Napoleon, of an "unnatural party passion" stirred up by the press, which supposedly gives way to a more dispassionate perspective. Due to the possibility of such abuse, and because, in our state of reason, the executive possesses absolutely no decisive voice whatsoever in the legislative process, the right of dissolution must be entirely abolished. The best means to maintain or restore harmony between the people and their representatives, aside from the press and popular assemblies, is to ensure that the assembly is renewed at a frequent, legally mandated interval such that, upon the expiration of this period, the electoral assemblies convene automatically to reconstitute the general assembly. The notion that the executive branch holds the exclusive right to convene the electoral assemblies and the national legislature constitutes yet another vestige of the separation of powers which we have abolished in our ideal model of the state. Consequently, the national legislature remains in continuous session throughout its entire elected term, adjourning only at its own discretion.

##### Public Salary and Pension

That the representatives receive daily allowances from the public treasury for their work follows self-evidently from the principle that all labor carries its own remuneration. This payment may be regarded as a sixth safeguard, to the extent that it renders the position of the people's representatives more independent. Moreover, it is only in this way that it becomes possible for anyone to be elected without being subject to property qualifications, since merit alone determines the outcome. Conversely, were one to view non-payment as a safeguard on the grounds that only wealthy individuals could be elected as representatives, one would clearly run the risk of transforming the constitution into moneyed aristocracy, a form of rule as pernicious as that of the hereditary nobility.

##### Inviolability of Public Office

The seventh safeguard is the inviolability of the people's representatives. Since the representatives are directly chosen and elected by the people, the presumption of their integrity exists, (at the outset, at least) to an even higher degree than it does for any ordinary citizen. As a result, arrest on suspicion of a crime may take place only with the consent of the general assembly, otherwise a member of that body could be removed in defiance of the general will. During periods of adjournment, enough time remains for the implementation of such measures. An exception to this rule is the case of being caught *in flagrante delicto* (red-handed), for in such instances, there exists no mere suspicion, but the certainty of the crime committed. Under these circumstances, the individual’s expulsion from the general assembly must follow immediately, for anyone who violates the law can no longer contribute to its establishment. Nor may any representatives be held accountable for their votes and speeches to any authority other than the general assembly itself. For we must not draw hair-splitting distinctions between expressions and opinions to contrive some form of judicial accountability.

##### No Party Rule

A final safeguard of liberty, one that might be perceived as the existence of an opposition party, is entirely unnecessary in our state. This would only function as a safeguard if an opposition party was tasked with defending the popular will against a ruling government party that actively opposed the people's desires. In such a scenario, the opposition acts as the driving force seeking to enforce the demands of the people. As a result, the absence of an opposition in a constitutional state would cast the appearance of despotism upon the government. It is recounted that Pitt, once thrown into the utmost perplexity by the disappearance of the opposition in parliament, considered nothing more urgent than to purchase an opposition for himself. However, if the executive power serves only as the formal apex of the substantive will of the people themselves, then the role of the opposition within the assembly is reduced to little more than representing the minority, that faction which, in opposition to the majority, champions new ideas that are striving to break through against the established and prevailing norms within the assembly.

### Administrative Power

As the intermediary link between the executive and legislative powers, the administrative power itself is nothing other to the state than what, in the context of the municipal order, was the municipal council. The term council is the most fitting designation for this power, for its specific duty in any given instance is, by virtue of its insight, to offer counsel, to weigh matters from every perspective, and to give concrete expression to the requirements of the law. Thus, the administrative power is structured into municipal councils, provincial councils, and the state council. Since we have already addressed the first two of these bodies in our exposition of the municipal and provincial constitutions, it now remains only to set forth the functions, personnel, and powers of the state council or senate.

#### Administrative Functions

The functions of the administrative power are, once again, partly those particular to itself, and partly those relating to the other two powers.

##### Administration

First, in matters pertaining to its own sphere, the administrative power constitutes the higher authority for the municipal and district councils, serving to represent the different interests of society, and thereby integrating society into a constituent element of the general life of the nation. Although every association manages its own affairs autonomously by virtue of self-government, and although in our state, any supervision of lower associations by higher ones is naturally dispensed with, it remains the case that, just as the economic interests of an entire province are submitted to the provincial council for adjudication, so too must those of the entire state be submitted to the state council. Likewise, just as disputes arising between individual municipalities are resolved by the provincial council, so are disputes between the various provinces resolved by the state council. It must be a fundamental principle in a constitutional state that all administrative matters be resolved by due process. Therefore, the judicial power, as defined in conventional political theory as a distinct, independent entity standing in opposition to the government, ceases to exist in this form, for *all* spheres of administration must be elevated to the same level of judicial independence. Admittedly, the term "administration" is generally understood to denote the more or less arbitrary decision-making exercised by the administrative power by its officials. But now, an entirely opposite trend prevails, one that aims at nothing other than drawing an ever-increasing number of cases into the administrative sphere, even those of an evidently judicial nature.

From this endeavor sprang the establishment of a Court for Conflicts of Jurisdiction, an institution introduced in 1848, modeled after French law. This concerns not conflicts between one court and another, but conflicts between courts of law and administrative authorities. Its true purpose is to conceal the exclusion of judicial recourse, even in purely legal matters, under the guise of a quasi-judicial decision, thereby enabling the exercise of even greater arbitrariness via administrative channels, unhindered by judicial scrutiny. The monstrosity of such an institution is further compounded by the fact that the administrative authorities involved often act as judges in their own case, rather than submitting the matter to judicial adjudication. We must categorically deny the legitimacy of such a procedure, particularly given the specific composition of this court, which consists partly of administrative officials and partly of judicial officers. While this arrangement may be touted as a guarantee of impartiality, as if it ensures that every case were brought before its proper adjudicator, in reality it is nothing more than device cunningly contrived to cast a veil of pseudo-law over the proceedings. Since any infringement upon persons or property, regardless of the social sphere to which the issue may pertain, must be resolved through judicial channels, it follows that lower administrative authorities (or the state itself, if the individual believes their rights to have been infringed upon by them) must appear before the court, just as any private individual would, as much as these authorities are (corporate) persons themselves. In a free state, Hegel rightly observes that legal disputes involving the public treasury are easily lost (*Philosophy of Right*, § 221), for the treasury must possess a very clear legal title if it wishes to see its claim recognized, lest it even give the appearance that the administration might arbitrarily wield its power against the individual. In a free state, this is altogether impossible, for the administrative authorities represents *all* societal interests, in every respect, as strictly in accordance with law and right as the ordinary courts determine civil and criminal matters. Conversely, the ordinary courts themselves only constitute a specific sphere of the administration, i.e. private law. Thus, just as the municipal trade councils, for example, incorporate within themselves the commercial courts of first instance, and the provincial councils those of the second instance, acting as wholly independent authorities empowered to adjudicate the law, so too does the state council constitute the third instance in contested cases.

The state council possesses a decisive voice not only as a higher authority overseeing the interests of national economy, ranging from agriculture, industry, commerce, law, public health (as administered by physicians), and public safety, but also extends to interests that arise only within and alongside the state itself. Thus, the military, diplomacy, art, religion, science, and even the state's financial affairs (i.e. its shareholdings and income taxes authorized by the legislative power), are all subject to specialized administrative bodies. In all these spheres, the crucial task is the subsumption of the individual case under the applicable legal provision. In the archetypal free state, whatever the ministers are charged to execute must invariably be decided collegially, by a majority vote of the councilors, and moreover, that the councilors are directly elected from the ranks of the people. Whereas under the current system, even when the councilors appointed by the head of state act merely in an advisory capacity vis-à-vis the ministry, without binding his decision, they still exert a decidedly heavy pressure upon the populace in their capacity as representatives of the Crown. If this particular relationship constitutes what is customarily termed bureaucratic rule, then such a phenomenon is utterly alien to our state.

##### Legislative Consult

The second function of the administrative power relates to legislation, and here the state council takes the place of a senate or upper house. Just as judgment, insight, and specialized expertise constitute the distinctive characteristics of this power in general, so too does it extend this influence outward as the mediating center of the two external branches. Therefore, regardless which of the three powers a legislative proposal originates from, the opinion of the state council or senate must always serve as the starting point, much as the Athenian *Boulē* and the Roman Senate lent their authority to a matter before it was submitted to the people. If the legislative proposal originates from the administrative power itself, then prior deliberation within the senate goes without saying. Yet even when a minister of the executive branch or a representative of the people introduces a motion, the deliberation within the legislative assembly must invariably be preceded by the solicitation of an opinion from the senate (*senatus consultum*). In the legislative activity of the senate, we find all the previously cited advantages of the bicameral system, such as dual deliberation, prudent moderation, the consideration of particular interests, and so forth, without encountering its attendant disadvantages. Here, there can be no question of jealousy between the people and the government, nor of any mutual obstruction of powers, precisely because co-equal powers cannot stand opposed to one another. What has already come to pass regarding financial legislation has been generalized in those instances where only a unicameral legislative assembly exists, so the opinion of the senate is not binding upon the legislature. Nevertheless, when it concerns the allocation of funds for the lower councils, the senate's involvement will carry greater influence than if the proposal were merely put forward by the lower councils.

##### Executive Consult

Thirdly, regarding the relationship of the administrative power to the executive power, the senate is required to provide its opinion in this sphere as well. For once the substance of a decision has been established by the relevant department of the state council, the formal head of the executive branch still retains discretion regarding the specific manner in which the action is carried out, as much as the true significance of the decision’s content is realized through its proper execution. To determine the proper course of action, the executive power may seek the counsel of the insightful, particularly with experts in the field, or alternatively, such expert opinion may already have been incorporated into the collegial deliberations of the relevant department regarding the subsumption of the specific case under the law. In any event, the senate no longer possesses the decisive vote in this context, a power it otherwise exercises in matters strictly pertaining to the public functions of government.

Furthermore, when the matter at hand concerns military directives, diplomatic missions, and the like, which constitute the true domain of the executive power, the ministers will certainly still require the insight of experts, but for that very reason, they do not possess the right to divest themselves of any portion of that responsibility. The fact that the senate in North America, whose counsel the president is bound to heed in matters of war and peace, diplomatic appointments, and the like, simultaneously functions as a legislative body constitutes a serious inconsistency. That said, the allocation of war funds, the authorized strength of the armed forces, and all laws pertaining to the organization and structure of the military are, in any case, contingent upon the appropriations and enactments of the legislative power.

#### Administrative Personnel

As much as the individuals holding administrative office are elected by the workers belonging to that profession, the conflict between professional interests and the constitutional order is resolved in the simplest possible manner: the profession-based election is assigned to the administrative power, while election by head count belongs to the legislative power. Furthermore, since indirect election ensures that the "aristocracy of merit" (the only aristocracy our state tolerates) invariably springs from popular sovereignty itself, the objective that Plato originally demanded is thereby achieved: that those who hve knowledge should govern. Thus, just as municipal councilors are elected directly from the elections held by workers associations, and district councilors emerge from the specialized departments of the municipal councils, so too is it advisable to have the state council constituted from the ranks of the provincial councilors. However, while each profession is exclusively responsible for electing its own representatives, it goes without saying that it is under no compulsion to elect members of its own profession, even though it is possible that such members appear to be the most suitable candidates. Practice and theory are two different things, and we can readily imagine that a profession might prefer the expertise of a scholar to a member of its own profession. Fundamentally, the guarantee for the quality of the election lies within the electoral body itself, the workers associations, for its own prerogative is to seek out the very best representatives for itself.

Like the lower councils, the state council is divided into as many departments as there are divisions of labor within civil society, including the state’s court acting as the third instance of appeal so that it may guide each specific branch with the utmost insight. In the administration of societal interests, each department shall decide its own affairs independently. Only when a matter touches upon more than one sphere shall the relevant departments convene to reach a decision by *curiae*. In the event that a division of opinion (*itio in partes*) arises, the final decision shall rest with the entire council in a joint session. When rendering opinions on legislative proposals, the preliminary deliberation and preparation of the content shall fall to the department whose specific domain the proposal concerns, while the final decision shall rest with the joint session of all departments. Finally, in cases involving opinions on measures to be implemented, deliberation within the relevant specialized department shall suffice, unless the executive prefers to consult the entire senate for advice.

#### Administrative Safeguards

The safeguards against bureaucratic rule, the system of oversight from above to below and vice versa, of which Hegel (*Philosophy of Right*, § 295) speaks at such length, vanish entirely. We are no longer dealing with bureaucratic rule at all, since self-government has supplanted the supervisory authority of the administrative bodies.

##### Local Oversight

Oversight from the bottom up is naturally inherent to the electoral process as its primary safeguard. However, the members of the various professions and the lower councils must inevitably submit to the decisions of the people they have elected. In this act of obedience, they remain entirely free, for they are merely following the authorized representatives they elected. In a constitution founded upon the broadest possible basis, the current of the will, of liberty itself, flows from the bottom up. With the demise of bureaucratic rule, the official character of the representatives of administration, who in the police state serve as the representatives of the sovereign's majesty, is likewise lost. The subordination of the lower ranks to the higher, which under the feudal state, bore the character of fealty, has under the absolute monarchy, degenerated into a condition of utter servitude, inasmuch as the commands of the authorities, acting as agents "by the Grace of God", must be obeyed without fail. The constitutional monarchy forever struggles in vain to attain liberty as it clings to these inherited premises of authoritarian rule. In our state, all workers, to whatever sphere of social activity they may belong, remain private individuals, for even the representatives whom they dispatch to the councils step aside from their own profession only for certain duration as they are called upon to represent therein the interests of their respective profession. The attributes traditionally associated with senators apply equally to these councilors. As individuals distinguished within their respective spheres by virtue of their insight and specialized expertise, a higher minimum age might also be appropriately be prescribed for them. While these officials are elected for three years, the councilors are chosen for six, ensuring that overly frequent turnover does not paralyze administrative affairs. The safeguard against the state council ossifying into a bureaucratic autocracy lies in the fact that the councilors, though eligible for re-election, must immerse themselves anew in the purifying bath of popular suffrage.

##### Electorate of Worker Associations

Another guarantee, derived from the state examinations of civil servants, is also provided in our state through the election of workers by their comrades. This should not to be taken to mean that civil examinations are to be abolished, but that we leave it to the worker associations to require their members, whether as apprentices, journeymen, or masters, to furnish proof of their competence. It goes without saying that independent "masters" who hold themselves above any worker association, regardless of how many people may place their trust in them, will in any case lack the right to participate in state council elections. As citizens, they may participate only in elections for the legislative body and the administrative tiers beneath it. This will undoubtedly serve as a new incentive ensuring that even those who feel most self-reliant in their professional endeavors, hoping to prevail in the competitive struggle solely through their own strength, will nonetheless, for the sake of securing their civic rights, not disdain being a member of a worker association. A vibrant civic life will cause such eccentrics to disappear more and more. 

##### Accountability of Public Office

A third safeguard, which in modern states lies in the accountability of public officials, is a highly precarious matter, for the lower-ranking official shifts their own accountability onto their superiors, being legally obligated to act in accordance with their will. The higher-ranking officials, i.e. the ministers, are certainly accountable to the representative body of the people, where usually it is the Lower House that brings the charges and the Upper House renders the verdict. However, quite apart from the fact that a law establishing ministerial accountability is sought after in vain in many states, or is restricted solely to high crimes against the state, ministerial arbitrariness easily breaches all barriers as a powerful tear asunder the very bonds that fetter the people. In our archetypal state, all citizens are equal before the law, so each individual is accountable to their own competent judge, and any citizen may file suit against any councilor for a perceived violation of their rights. In matters that fall under the jurisdiction of municipal law, every councilor is referred to the ordinary courts and is subject to the full hierarchy of judicial appeals. In this system, the judicial department of the state council constitutes the court of final appeal. However, in cases of official misconduct, such as if a councilor demonstrates a lack of the insight required for their position, violates the honor of their office, or generally administers their affairs poorly, they are held accountable by disciplinary authority of their own department, acting as a council of honor composed of his peers, and they are likewise entitled to avail themselves of the full hierarchy of appeals within the disciplinary courts. If the councilors lack the two lower instances, and thus possess a lesser safeguard, then their exalted position results from a process of careful selection, their insight and education serves as a safeguard, however subjective, that they will rarely ever be guilty of such crimes or abuses of office. On the one hand, while this position of theirs mitigates the heightened risk inherent in a single-instance adjudication, on the other hand, their greater accountability simultaneously demands that very heightened risk.

##### Inviolability of Public Office

Fourthly, the inviolability of their persons is also shared by the councils with the legislators, and indeed, with every single citizen. Libel directed at the councilors can, without prejudice to the equality of all, assume a higher degree of culpability, but only to the extent that what is violated in their persons is not merely their own liberty, but what has already been articulated as the general will through the choice of the people, even if such libel is not, as was the case in Athens, explicitly deemed an act of *lèse-majesté*. We must by no means attribute the heavier penalty, applied particularly when libel occurs during the performance of official duties, to any special sanctity inherent in these individuals, but solely to the general cause they represent. If Hegel attempted to derive the exceptional status of public officials from the premise that the official relationship substantive ethical relationship rather than a mere contract (*Philosophy of Right*, § 294), then the contractual aspect of the relationship here excludes its ethical character no more than it does the case of marriage, given that a public official can sue or be sued on the basis of both the contractual and ethical dimensions of their relationship to the office.

##### Public Salary and Pension

Finally, regarding the guarantees afforded to this profession, seen partly in the form of a state salary and partly in the granting of a retirement pension, this relationship also traces its origins back to municipal law. Just as the salaries of our councilors do not constitute an exceptional status, since it is always the persons who benefit from the work who provide the remuneration (i.e. the collective body of the municipality, province, or state), so too are retirement pensions of the same origin for all workers. For worker associations of every kind, or even of local communities, guarantee such pensions to their members through their regular contributions. Likewise, it is conceivable that members of a profession might collectively participate, as a corporate body, in an insurance firm for this very purpose. In our state, there can be no question of a retirement pension being granted due to a change in the ruling political party holding office. For we possess neither an autocratic government, under which a monarch may arbitrarily appoint and dismiss their officials, nor a parliamentary government, where the administration is subject to legislative majority, but a system of self-government, wherein the administration is grounded within itself and is autonomous, just as the other powers are. Should the electoral bodies fail to re-elect these councilors, then the educational councilors or medical councilors, for instance, are under no obligation to return to their private practice. They may, at their own discretion, maintain their practice throughout the duration of their administrative tenure, as a form of dual activity. However, the situation here is no different from that of a judge who refrains from voting whenever their personal interest is at stake in the matter at hand.

### Executive Power

The last term of the legislative process at the culmination of the life of the state, the executive power, certainly encompasses within itself universality and particularity as the individuality of the state. As much as this power encloses the whole within itself, Hegel (*Philosophy of Right* § 275, 278) designates it the true representative of the state’s sovereignty, for this sovereignty can only properly reside in the state itself, and not in its head. We are willing to grant Hegel the point that the individuality embodied in the executive power must be an individual person (*Philosophy of Right* § 279). However, if he seeks to ascribe sovereignty to this power on the grounds that, after all particular considerations have been weighed, the final decision falls to it, then such a state more or less approximates an absolute monarchy. Therefore, it was fundamentally contrary to Hegel’s own principles to begin with this power (which he terms the "princely power") since the most perfect and supreme element within any whole appears to him only at the very end, as the third term. But by placing this princely power at the beginning *and* the end, Hegel nevertheless characterizes its unity in the most inconsistent manner. Even if the executive power is sovereignty itself embodied as a person, we cannot interpret this concept to mean that the dissolution of all particular aspects of state life becomes fixed once again within this single person. Instead, it signifies that this power, like all other aspects of state life, is reabsorbed into the fluidity of the its circulatory system, only to reappear from it once more.

#### Executive Functions

The functions of the executive power are, quite simply, none other than these: that the executive power, acting as the contentless apex of the entire life of the state, makes the popular will a reality in every direction. Thus, while this power indeed encompasses the entire state, it does so only in the sense that all the vital currents of the state’s life flow into it, so that it may bring this content into existence without adding anything of its own. The majesty of the head of state lies precisely in the fact that the content of their public actions, whatever the content of their private life may be, is formed solely by the general will. Although Hegel, in the *Philosophy of Right* published under his own supervision, consistently speaks of the "final decision" resting with the Prince, his lectures—which also found its way into the *Additions* of the first edition (edited by Gans) within the *Collected Works* (§280)—holds that the Prince merely "dots the *i*", an interpretation that is closer to the inherent logic of the matter. Although there can be no doubt regarding the authenticity of this statement, Hegel’s son nevertheless omitted it from the second edition. Shortly after Hegel had made this remark in his lectures, attempts were made to denounce him on this very account, alleging that the statement constituted a disparagement of the monarchy. However, the King had replied, quite rightly: “Without the dot, the ‘I’ would, after all, be no ‘I’.” This sentiment pleased the modest monarch, who desired nothing more than to be precisely this dot upon the “I”, whereas others would have sought to make themselves the entire stroke itself. If in an addendum to § 279, it is stated—equally unequivocally and in keeping with Hegel’s intent—that the monarch’s activity must not be arbitrary, but is bound to the concrete content of deliberations and often limited merely to the affixing of a signature. While the formal aspect of the decision is thereby correctly recognized and sharply highlighted, the executive activity itself is conversely underestimated, for the manner of execution comprises aspects of action that are of the utmost importance, quite apart from the content itself.

##### Defend the Constitution

The first function that falls within the domain of the executive power is to consistently represent the personality of the people. If all participate in legislation, and only the best in administration, then necessarily, in execution, only an individual can represent the will of the people. However, we by no means wish to locate this unity of will in the fact that all other activities of state life are exercised in the name of the head of state. In our state of self-government, administration naturally cannot be conducted in the name of the head of state, but only in the name of the mandate-granting bodies. Instead, function of the executive power within the internal affairs of the state consists solely in ensuring the sovereignty of each particular sphere, allowing each to operate autonomously and preserve its independence. Therefore, executive power must, this specific respect, indeed constitute the power of the state, as Spinoza (and following him, Mr. Trendelenburg) would have it, but it remains only a formal power to uphold order without stifling liberty. If this power becomes *actual* only by virtue of having the material force of the army behind it, we are nevertheless far removed from positing the military as a fourth branch of government, as Mr. Trendelenburg would have it. On the contrary, the military is strictly a subordinate force, the blind arm of the general will of the people, and the guardians of the law, as Plato ​​accurately terms them. The provision that the military can only intervene at the behest of the civil authorities has even found its way into existing constitutions. Conversely, reactionary parties seek to ensure that this power serves, in all matters, to uphold the substantive arbitrariness of an absolute monarch against even the most clearly defined claims of the general will.

If the executive power internally safeguards the sovereignty of the people by taking into account all particularities, then this concept, when directed outward, assumes the significance of subsuming all the particularities of the people into the general personality of the state *qua* state, positing it as a single unified person vis-à-vis other heads of state. It is within this external representation that the executive power derives the right to accredit envoys to other nations, to conclude treaties of commerce and other agreements, and to decide matters of war and peace. From this arises the second, higher purpose of the army: that it is destined to preserve the very existence of the people whenever that existence comes under attack. However, in such a moment, the task of national defense cannot be left to the handful of servants of the executive power. Instead, it must be the people in arms, led by the head of state, that causes all individuals to appear as a single, unified, executing will.

##### Administrative Counsel

The second function of executive power relates to the administrative power. In the first case described, the executive power was not bound by the opinions of the administration, but only to its own responsibilities. However, with respect to the execution of societal activities, the executive power it is strictly bound by the content of the opinion voiced by the administrative power, by the collegial decision of the Senate, and it is responsible for the manner in which its execution is carried out.

##### Promulgation

With regard to legislation, the executive power is by no means a "factor" in the legislative process itself. Even if the right of initiative is not denied to the executive, the absolute right of veto certainly should be. With such a right, the constitution becomes mechanical, a fetter on the movement of the general will. When the exercise of unbridled arbitrary power—even if restricted to constitutional veto—can no longer affirmatively impose the specific content of its own aims, it negatively obstructs what is rational. Moreover, it remains a very dangerous weapon in the hands of ministers, more dangerous than the right to dissolve the legislative chamber. For by exercising this right, the executive power places itself in direct opposition to public opinion and to the entire body of the people.

However, this raises another question: if the absolute right of refusal is indeed harmful, what then of the *temporary* veto? Consider, for instance, the system in North America, where the head of state has the right to return a legislative bill to the legislative houses. In such a case, the bill remains in abeyance until the end of the head of state's term of office, unless Congress chooses to pass it by a two-thirds majority. In Norway, by contrast, a bill automatically becomes law during the third legislative session, provided that it was passed by the chamber and rejected by the King during each of the preceding sessions. This temporary veto is a half measure, a *compromise* that effectively entails a division of powers lasting for a period of two or four years. It is somewhat less harmful in the American context, given that Congress retains the power to override this time-bound restriction, which is itself exercised only on the rarest of occasions. Thus, the conditional right of refusal essentially serves the purpose of ensuring more mature deliberation, much like the institution of double or triple readings already mentioned, although over a longer period. The only rational function of executive power in the legislative process consists in the promulgation of laws through the executive signature. Should the executive power withhold its signature, the presiding officer of the general assembly enacts the law by affixing their own.

#### Executive Personnel

At first glance, it would seem that, regarding the executive power, one could speak not of persons in the plural, but only of a single person. However, the various spheres lower than the state already present a multitude of persons upon whom the task of execution devolves. What the chairman is to every association, the mayor is to every municipality, the governor is to every province, the head of state is to the state as a whole.

##### The Presidency

Here arises the great question: should the head of state be hereditary, or should he be placed in office through election? Arguments in favor of the utility of hereditary succession are found in the feuds among royal families in elective monarchies, and in the internal discord of those realms themselves, as witnessed in the examples of Germany and Poland. As the argument goes, hereditary succession endows the state with unity and stability, provided that the eldest male heir, without any division of the inheritance among multiple brothers, ascends the throne. Since the fall of Napoleon I, this principle of legitimacy and primogeniture has been made the very cornerstone of constitutional wisdom, and the forces of reaction have clung to it with anxious tenacity, thereby seeking to preserve absolute monarchy.

Instead of reducing the status of "being by the grace of God" (the divine attribute of kings) to birth or nature, as if nature were more divine than spirit, Hegel sought to ascribe a deeper, spiritual-metaphysical significance to the principle of heredity. In doing so, one can observe his evident struggle, spanning three lengthy paragraphs (*Philosophy of Right*, §§ 279–281), to bridge the gap between the monarch’s ethical personality and his natural, biological existence. Were we to follow his words in an attempt to grasp their true import, he would likely assent to our explanation. The state, he maintains, is in truth something divine, something elevated above the caprice of human will. It is the substantial spirit of the people, constituting the firm and enduring element amidst the flux of its various accidents. The representative of this abiding spirit of the people is the head of the ruling family, who by virtue of primogeniture, invariably stands at the apex and is thereby removed from the realm of arbitrary choice, as much as the French adage proclaimed: "The King never dies" (*Le Roi est mort, vive le Roi*). To embody this substantial majesty of the people's spirit, a majesty entirely independent of its accidental manifestations, it is Nature rather than the will of individuals, that must determine this representative, and here lies the inception of his error. He seeks to exclude human arbitrariness from the process of selection, or as he clarifies, the element of contingency inherent in the will is substituted with contingency of Nature. But is the latter truly any better than the former? He himself once said to me, when I was arguing with him against hereditary legislators during the time of the July Revolution: "Oh! Nature does it just as well as election." However, Nature is not meant to have the decisive say in the state, at most it is the incidental garb that clothes the spirit of the people. How could it possibly be left to *it*, of all things, to determine the crowning element of the edifice, the very pinnacle of spiritual life? Secondly, how can Nature make the choice as well as the free will of human beings? Nature has put the inept, the foolish, the bloodthirsty, and the like, on the throne and it has done so all too often. In electing a head of state, however, the people gather themselves together in their highest spiritual flowering, by placing at their head the one whom they deem most worthy. It is a more profound concept that this idea of ​​ethical state unity should be established in reality in and through the freedom of all, rather than in and through Nature, in spite of Hegel's dismissal of this as a "shallowness of thought." With a nobility based purely on merit, an elective monarchy is necessarily implied, whereas a hereditary monarchy naturally aligns with a nobility based on birth, though it remains conceivable even without it. Lassalle is right to view the hereditary nature of the Crown as the last feudal privilege that has intruded into the state and has managed to persist to this day. The state, as the collective spirit mediated through free will, must bring forth everything from a foundation of freedom. In our state, we have hitherto allowed every sphere, aspect, and authority to result from free election, so why make an exception for the head of state?

##### The Ministry

Since the functions of the executive power are not limited to acting as the representative of the people as a whole, but also involves the execution of the societal activities in its undertaking, the head of state finds themselves compelled to select ministers to conduct these affairs in their stead and in their name. However, the accountability of the ministry does not absolve the head of state of accountability, for both must be held accountable. The head of state bears an even greater responsibility than the ministers themselves, for by virtue of having selected the ministers, they effectively assume a share of their accountability as well. Furthermore, the ministers cannot be chosen by popular election, precisely because the head of state is the one responsible for the execution of policy. Herein lies the distinction between administrative officials and executive officials. It goes without saying that the head of state will seek ministers who possess the requisite insight into the specific sphere of societal activity entrusted to their execution. Specifically, there are as many ministries as there are departments within the Senate, though this does not preclude the grouping together of related fields, such as Military and Naval Affairs, the Arts, Public Health, Science, and so forth. The Ministries do not constitute collegial bodies, as the Senate does, precisely because the Minister is personally accountable for the execution of the Senate's decisions. However, in the state of reason they are bound even by these decisions, and it lies in the nature of the matter that the manner of their execution depends entirely upon them and the head of state.

##### The Military

The final element embodied within the executive power is constituted by the military, the collective mass of the people that lends the material means and actual force to the will of the executive. If Plato rightly demands of these "guardians of the law" a spirit of obedience toward those possessed of wisdom, so as to defend the whole against both internal and external enemies, then the armed forces must carry the negation of its own individuality in service of the common good to the very point of death, as well as positively manifest this obedience through subordination to a higher will, upheld by strict discipline. But if one were to draw from this *passive* obedience the conclusion that the military should not to be sworn to uphold the constitution, then such a state of inequality among citizens would be completely inadmissible. For if the military possesses and exercises the same constitutional rights as any other citizen, then they must also remain faithful to those very rights. Obedience to a commanding officer must never extend to the point of violating the constitution, and just as a soldier is under no obligation to obey a superior against their own moral convictions, such as if their superior orders them to commit a crime, so too are they under no obligation to obey orders against their own *civic* convictions, should that same superior command them to violate the constitution, which is also itself a crime. We fully subscribe to the principle that the armed forces must not engage in deliberation while under arms, their only function is to execute orders. However, making the head of state the absolute master and proprietor of large standing armies leads inevitably to absolute rule, as observed in Russia and in Napoleonic France.

The question of standing armies need not only be viewed in its relation to liberty, but it must be resolved on its own merit. Here we may assert that if the military constitutes a necessary element within the structure of social life, then this element must possess an independent, distinct, and permanent existence alongside the other elements, rather than recruited on an ad hoc basis to meet momentary demands. This much we are prepared to concede, but just as the choice of any vocation results from the free will of the individual, so too must the choice of this particular vocation depend upon the ability and passion for the vocation that flows directly from one’s own character. The moderate number of troops required to maintain internal order consists of the citizens drawn to this calling by their own taste and inclination, making them the most loyal soldiers, constituting the core around which the mass of the people rallies in times of war to defend the country. Thus, standing in counterpoint to the standing army is the people’s militia, of which the standing army is a necessary part. As a result, while the forces of reaction, seeking to transform the army into an instrument of tyranny, have posited the maxim: "Against democrats, only soldiers are effective," the state of the future has no need to invert this maxim: "Against soldiers, only democrats are effective", for within this State, the two are in fact one and the same. 

Regarding the organization of the military, the appointment of the highest-ranking officers of the standing army by the head of state may very well be the most appropriate course, to the extent that they are subordinate to the executive will. However, the administrators within the relevant departments of the municipal, provincial, and state councils will be directly elected by their peers. Should the entire people then rise up to save their own existence, they must be granted the right to choose their own leaders. However, the distinction between the standing army and the civic militia must not be construed as if the former were tasked with maintaining internal order and the latter were only responsible for combating the external enemy. On the contrary, we have already seen that, by virtue of the system of voluntary association, every municipality and every province, just like the state itself, forms its own standing force, whether it be a municipal guard, a district guard, and so forth, that is self-governing by nature. If it is argued that voluntary participation is impossible in the case of the militia, since the waging of war would then depend entirely upon the whim of individual citizens, we have already responded to this objection by asserting that a war lacking popular support should not to be waged in the first place. Because the head of state, notwithstanding their authority to determine matters of war and peace, cannot dispense with either the financial appropriations granted by the legislative power or the manpower provided by the population that rallies voluntarily to the cause, rendering "cabinet wars" impossible. General conscription and compulsory military service are institutions that ought no longer to exist in the ideal state, even if necessity permits them.

#### Executive Safeguards

Whereas the safeguards against executive power must be very robust in a constitutional monarchy, in our state, the institutions themselves completely prohibit the arbitrariness of monarchical rule. Even if a monarch, placed in a position of executive power by birth, is completely irresponsible, it is precisely the symptom of this constitution that the splendor of the crown and its immense wealth that elevates the power of this irresponsible authority to an immense magnitude, to which historical memories, reverence for the ancestral ruling family, and similar factors contribute their share. The guiltiest party is therefore frequently placed beyond the reach of punishment, as witnessed in the case of Charles X. Even the ministers, no matter how firm a will they may bring to their office, are blinded by the solar body of the crown and are drawn into the orbit of its way of thinking. Even the most liberal-minded men often become reactionaries as soon as they become ministers, and Bias was quite right when he exclaimed "ἀρχὰ ἄνδρα δείξει" (The office reveals the man). This change of heart and the moral erosion of even the most popular figures were vividly demonstrated on a grand scale in the history of the July Monarchy. The accountability of ministers, who shielded the inviolability of the King through their signatures, is a very feeble weapon against the ever-expanding prerogatives of the Crown. The English used to send unscrupulous ministers to the executioner's block, but since then, the state of ministerial accountability looks very bleak everywhere. Therefore, while in all matters unrelated to their official duties they are subject to the ordinary courts, like all other citizens, in our state, they are accountable to their constituents whenever the issue concerns their responsibility as members of the executive, much in the same way the head of state is held accountable. Thus, is entirely fitting that the general assembly presses the charges while the senate renders the verdict, whereas for members of either body, provided the matter does not fall under the purview of local law, they are strictly accountable to the association they were elected by, as a general rule.

Furthermore, the weak safeguard against encroachments by the military, taking the form in ordinary states as the annual determination of the size of the army and the funds necessary for its maintenance, has in our constitution, taking the form of the citizen's militia, has been transformed into the strongest possible safeguard. In other words, no specific safeguard is needed at all when the army consists of citizens trained in arms. For in this case, the people hold the future of the state in their own hands, whether through the election of their leaders or through collective action to resist any form of arbitrary rule and overthrow it, wherever it may rear its head. 

## International Law

As much as the head of state acts as the representative of the people in their external relations, therein lies the basis for the state's relationship with other states. At first, it might have seemed as though we could conclude our inquiries with the study of domestic constitutional law. What remains to be considered beyond that point, once we have constructed the state of reason in all its aspects and unfolded every activity of national life in the spirit of freedom? However, since the limitation inherent in the standpoint reached thus far lies in the fact that the individual state embodies the Idea of ​​Freedom subject to a specific constraint, that it remains bound by its own national character, so we cannot reach the true conclusion of our philosophical inquiries until we have portrayed the transcendence of this final barrier as the ultimate goal of world history. To this end, the individual state must relinquish its rigid isolation and self-sufficiency. Just as the legal person of private law is free only by entering into a relationship with another legal person, and just as the family member becomes a truly realized person by supporting and complementing other family members, so too does the same principle now apply to individual states, which are not called upon to renounce their distinct national character and individuality, but rather to socialize and complement them. This constitutes the process of assimilation as represented in the second figure (U-I-P). Thus, superimposed upon the internal union of state life, there arises a higher union of nations that attain their full and complete status as recognized entities only through this process of mutual association. It is only where a plurality of independent states exists that a truly free relationship can exist among them, for a universal state would inevitably stifle such freedom. Firstly, we consider the diplomatic recognition of states as persons and property owners, secondly, we set forth their contractual relations in the form of treaties, and finally, proceed to the subject of international justice and its rectification as the law of war, which will constitute the transition to cosmopolitan law.

### Diplomatic Law

Just as the first principle in private law was to be a person and respect others as persons, so too do we begin here with the mutual recognition of states. However, whereas constitutional law concerns the internal freedom of individual citizens, in international law we only consider the aspect of freedom which various nations possess in their external relations with each other.

#### Non-Interference

The first rule of international law is not to interfere in the internal affairs of another state—the principle of non-interference. However, just as in private law, international law also initially lacks an impartial judge to adjudicate between individual parties. As a result, the provisions of international law constitute a mere *ought*, thereby leaving the individual state, as a sovereign entity, the free choice of whether to follow them or not. Thus, interference and non-interference depend upon the discretion of nations. We must view this as the specific historical-philosophical development of international law, i.e. how this fluctuating *ought* gradually transforms itself into firm rules.

The arbitrary exercise of international law need not—even at its very inception—float entirely groundlessly in thin air, rather the very impulse towards intervention rests upon the principle of non-intervention itself. The following example may serve to elucidate this seemingly paradoxical proposition. Since the obligation of recognition is founded upon reciprocity, a state whose neighbor suddenly alters its constitution might view this alteration as a peril threatening its own security. It might perceive in this constitutional change next door a tacit refusal to recognize its own constitution, and thus discern from this a legitimate ground for intervention. Thus, it is argued, a certain affinity between constitutions constitutes an essential prerequisite for the mutual recognition of states. It was on this basis that the Eastern Powers of Europe deemed it a violation of their own sovereignty, and as a result, a justification for intervention—when the French beheaded their king and established a republic. England had no right to take such a stance, having itself proceeded in precisely the same manner on its own soil. We, however, categorically deny any nation the right to regard a neighbor’s internal constitution, or even its internal political crimes, as a legitimate ground for intervention, given that international law is concerned exclusively with the external relations existing between states. Furthermore, if the European family of nations has historically been able to tolerate, without intervening, various republics as Venice, Holland, Switzerland, and others, why should it not likewise tolerate a French one? 

However, if any interference whatsoever is deemed reprehensible, then this applies especially to such interference seeking to obliterate not only the certain actions of a neighbor, but their entire personhood. Thus, even if in individual cases the question of what constitutes interference or non-interference remains open to doubt, interference must under no circumstances extend so far as to abolish their sovereignty, the very personality of a people, and to incorporate it into another, as was done to the Polish. The independence of a people is an inalienable and imprescriptible right,to incorporate one people into another is to enslave it. This has occurred continually throughout history, and the Romans in particular practiced it on a grand scale. In this regard, it must be noted that what constitutes an injustice from the standpoint of international law, from the higher standpoint of world history, may become a right. The very essence of historical progress lies in the fact that every people which, once its historical moment has passed, must perish in its specific national distinctiveness and cede the dominion of the world to its successor. But once the ideal of humanity has been realized and the ultimate goal of world history attained, all the former nations that have perished, been trampled underfoot, or torn asunder, having submerged their limited national distinctiveness into the universal concept of humanity, may once again re-emerge from it to manifest the inherent nature of their spirit, now as a distinct facet of the universal human spirit. All the peoples of the earth shall form one great family, and infinity shall flow to humanity—in Hegel’s words—from the chalice of this realm of spirits.

#### Territorial Integrity

Secondly, just as the individual belonging to a foreign state must be recognized as a person, so must the the property rights appertaining to this person also be recognized. Just as the person exists only to the extent that they have property, so too must the sphere of this external existence of freedom be secured for a people. The primary and indisputable property of a people is its territory, for it is through the nature of this territory—its climate, geographical character, and so forth—that it has its distinctive national identity. This territory is inviolable, so a people has the right to refuse passage to foreign nations. Conversely, however, the right of passage may be granted to a nation’s armed forces along so-called *étapes* or transit routes. For this reason, the rivers that flow through the territory and the sea coast upon which it borders also belongs to the people. Even if, under Roman law, these were considered common property for the individuals *within* any given nation, they are nevertheless regarded as the specific property of the nation as a whole. Furthermore, since the seas constitute the common property of nations (whether inland seas shared between nations or the open seas shared by all nations) it is unjustified for any nation to deny passage through straits, even to warships, given that these straits now constitute a universal passageway. It must further be argued that, given the principle of absolute freedom of trade between nations, all tolls levied on land and water routes are be rejected by us as irrational.

While we may not wish to deny a people the right to acquire ownerless land, the sole question that arises in international law is this: what constitutes ownerless land? Initially, one might answer: uninhabited islands or tracts of a foreign mainland. However, as much as these islands are geologically connected to a larger landmass, this raises another question as to whether distant seafarers who set foot upon such land possess the right of acquisition, or whether, conversely, the territory in question rightfully belongs, by virtue of geographical contiguity, to the peoples of the adjacent mainland. The English, to be sure, took possession of North America, paying no heed to the legitimate owners, through sheer conquest, and they proceeded no differently with Australia and New Zealand. However, if a nation is situated nearby, it is fully entitled to lodge an objection—just as the United States have done through the Monroe Doctrine, prohibiting foreign powers not only from acquiring territory on American soil, but also from interfering in the internal affairs of any American state. 

As much as the personality of a state extends to the entirety of its property, ships are also not excluded from this. Not only when the ship lies in port, but also on the high seas beneath its flag and upon its deck, it constitutes the very property of its people. Just as the miller appropriates the wind as their own property, so too does the ship appropriate the furrow of the sea through which it passes and occupies at any given moment. Hence, English laws prevail aboard an English ship, and for this very reason, the Southern States of North America dreaded the approach of an English vessel to their ports, for any slaves who had escaped to such a ship were rendered free. If the English evinced a greater degree of sympathy for the Southern rebels than might have appeared compatible with their professed abhorrence of slavery, it was likely their desperation for cotton that was to blame for this infidelity to their own principles.

But even if the open sea constitutes the common property of all nations, this does not preclude the possibility that the portion of the sea immediately adjacent to the coast may be deemed the property of the coastal state by virtue of "formative accession". It follows therefrom that, while navigation and fishing on the open sea are indeed permitted to all nations, fishing in coastal waters and coastal shipping remain the exclusive property of the respective nation, and may be opened to other nations only by means of a treaty. For instance, oyster beds are deemed part of the coast, so they must be reserved exclusively to the owner of that coast. Natural law, however, cannot definitively prescribe the precise number of miles seaward to which this coastal zone ought to extend.

From the right of free navigation also follows the right to unimpeded passage—the right not to be halted or searched by any warship of a foreign nation. In times of peace, this right is generally recognized. We shall address the subject of war later, when considering the rights of neutral parties. Although the English have often—and unlawfully, by virtue of the overwhelming power of their fleet—arrogated to themselves the authority of maritime policing, they have invariably taken great offense whenever anyone has attempted to exercise such a right of supervision against their own vessels. Admittedly, some form of police order does seem necessary even upon the high seas, lest pirates be free to harass innocent travelers. Thus, international law establishes a consensus that every vessel must fly its flag, be furnished with papers proving its national identity, and so forth. But what use is a flag if a ship carries several false ones in its hold? Even this policing restriction upon the seas eventually falls away, as much as passport controls on land have begun to waver. Furthermore, since no permanent judicial authority extends its jurisdiction across the high seas, every traveler must defend themselves against pirates. Every nation is free to secure its own safety against freebooters by providing naval escorts for its merchant vessels.

#### Right of Legation

The personality of a state, however, must be recognized not only in its internal activity, nor only in relation to the external existence of its freedom, but equally outside its own territory, wherever the state appears as a person, and thus even with a foreign people. The personality of the state must be *at home with itself* in the other state, and only in this way does it truly acquire its *being-for-itself*. From this principle follows, thirdly, the right of legation. Whether a people has occasion to send any message to another, or maintains a permanent envoy in a foreign land, that envoy is to be regarded as sacred and inviolable. Since the head of state represents the sovereignty of the people in its external relations, it is entirely fitting that envoys be appointed by them. The envoys serve as their executive agents in external affairs, just as the ministers serve their executive agents in internal affairs. Thus, the envoys are the immediate representatives of the people's sovereignty to other nations, just as the head of state is to the nation itself. The inviolability of envoys does not cease even in times of war, they must be granted safe conduct and provided with passports should hostilities break out.

As much as one people is *at home with itself* in another through the envoy, there follows from this their extraterritoriality—that is, the right to be regarded, while in a foreign land, as if they were within their own. An envoy is therefore exempt from the tax laws of the foreign country in which they reside. The envoy lives under the laws of their own land, and their residence in the foreign country is regarded, in a sense, as the property of the nation. Thus, envoys are entitled to hoist their nation's flag over their residence. As much as an envoy appears in a foreign land as the presence of a nation's sovereignty, they serve as the natural protector of all their compatriots residing there, whether they are travelers or individuals staying for an extended period for the sake of their livelihood. Thus, these representatives of the head of state are dispatched even to the most distant lands, and when their primary duty is to safeguard the welfare of their trading compatriots, they are designated as consuls.

With the establishment of diplomatic law, the relationship between nations—and their mutual intercourse—is thus inaugurated. Just as in the sphere of private law, we proceed here from the consideration of persons and property to contracts, i.e. treaties.

### Treaties

Since each nation is recognized by other nations as an absolutely independent entity, the particular content of their will, whereby they enter into closer legal relations with one another, is determined by the arbitrary will of each party, for this is precisely the nature of a treaty. As states possess a far greater degree of independence, each constituting a self-contained and self-sufficient whole, the content of these treaties are less comprehensive and narrower in scope than is the case in civil society, where the more developed that society becomes, the more the individual becomes dependent upon all others.

#### Trade Agreements

Firstly, treaties can possess the very same content as contracts in private law, i.e. to serve human needs. As much as one nation produces goods of a certain kind, and another nation goods of a different kind, they are able, through mutual exchange, to satisfy their respective needs in the most useful and effective manner. If free trade constitutes the most conducive and rational means to this end, then there is no need for trade agreements between various nations. Such treaties merely serve to loosen the restrictive barriers of protective tariffs, import duties, and the like, with respect to a particular nation without dismantling them entirely. Just as the one extreme of free trade requires no treaties whatsoever, so too does the other extreme of Fichte’s "Closed Commercial State" require none. But whereas the Closed Commercial State erroneously locates the sound principle of state autonomy in monastic isolation, free trade more naturally perceives it in unrestricted exchange. Trade agreements pertaining to societal needs are therefore only necessary in the intermediate situation wherein nations oscillate between free trade and import bans. Among such restrictions were the English Navigation Acts, which placed significant impediments in the path of foreign shipping traffic. Just as those Acts were eventually repealed, so too were their kindred measures of the Corn Laws, which permitted the English aristocracy to supply bread to the impoverished masses only in exchange for exorbitant sums of gold, all to ensure that the nation would not become dependent upon the Continent for its sustenance. As if the high price of grain in England and the exclusion of foreign grain from English ports was not just as detrimental to the English themselves as it was to foreign countries, for the more grain the latter are able to sell in England, the more English goods they are able to purchase. Under a system of free trade, the only remaining subjects for treaties of a specific nature would likely be those already mentioned: agreements regarding transit routes, coastal shipping, fisheries, and the like. In every instance, only the outer shell of protectionism has fallen, the underlying principle itself is far from being eradicated. 

Even if this first type of treaty is of a highly specific nature, it is nonetheless always the state, acting as a free legal person, that concludes it. Thus, the very act of concluding even the most trivial of such treaties inherently entails that recognition of a state's sovereignty. As a result, the recognition of a new state is often limited to the fact that established states conclude commercial treaties with it. States that conclude a treaty with a seceded colony are generally not particularly hesitant to do so, partly due to the exhaustion of the motherland, and partly in order to secure commercial advantages for themselves. This occurs particularly in instances where new states secede from older ones, as was the case when England’s North American colonies and Spain’s South American colonies broke away from their motherland. After the war had continued for some time, and the rebels had sufficiently demonstrated their independent strength, foreign powers concluded trade treaties with them. Admittedly, this constitutes an act of war on the part of the motherland against the nation concluding the treaty. And had England or France, during the war between the United States and the insurgent South, concluded treaties with the latter, thereby recognizing its sovereignty, the North would likewise have viewed this as grounds for war. 

#### Federations

Secondly, just as treaties may indirectly have as their object the recognition of the independence of another people, this independence and its preservation may also constitute the direct purpose of the treaty. In this latter case, they are termed alliances. Since it lies in the interest of a militarily weaker people to seek protection wherever it may be found, they will align itself with a more powerful one by entering into a defensive and offensive alliance with them. Such an overture will be welcome to the more powerful party as well, for while the stronger power protects the weaker, the latter’s support nonetheless serves to render the powerful state even more formidable. Yet, such alliances are themselves subject to the vagaries of chance. For precisely because treaties are concluded and dissolved arbitrarily, there is no guarantee of mutual recognition. Moreover, it remains doubtful whether a weaker people will find a more powerful ally to safeguard its independence, for a powerful neighbor may well be tempted to form an offensive alliance with another acquisitive neighbor for the purpose of conquering the third party. The contingent nature of these alliances is further revealed by the specific terms on which they are concluded, terms which are themselves conditioned by the mutable interests of the states involved. This mutability will progressively diminish as the constitutions of states become freer, as much as a free constitution serves to curb any potential territorial greed pursued by a head of state.

Alliances shed this character of contingency and preclude arbitrariness in the relations between free states, thereby abandoning their natural isolation and establishing a juridical order of states. This alliance forms a *federal state*, which is distinguished from the *unitary state* only by the fact that, within the hierarchical structure of the division of powers, it possesses one additional tier. Just as the administrative provinces relate to the state, so too do the individual states relate to the federation, serving as their central political hub. However, if one asks why nations sometimes form unitary states and at other times form into a federal state, the reason for this lies in the divergence of their historical development. Wherever the individual families comprising a nation became conscious of their unity at an early stage, they easily relinquish their separate autonomy and bring to full realization an undivided national spirit, as for example, in Spain and in France (even more so in the latter). Conversely, wherever the various families of a nation exhibit a greater degree of distinctiveness and uniqueness, the formation of a federal state proves difficult or even impossible, as observed in arduous struggle of Germany and Italy.

We witness the formation of the earliest federal state in the seven provinces of the Netherlands, for these provinces, notwithstanding their individual autonomy, were compelled during their revolt to forge the closer bond of a federal union to withstand their powerful oppressor and safeguard the precious jewel of their liberty. Hence, they also adopted the motto that unity makes strength. The need for a federal state is even more powerful among nations which, by virtue of their geography, climate, customs, and so forth, are conscious of their underlying unity while being comprised of different peoples, such as Switzerland and the United States. Because this diversity precludes the formation of a unitary state, the free development of each distinct national identity is only possible within a federal state. Most powerful of all, however, is the need for such a structure among nations which, not by nature or by their own will, have been forcibly combined into a single state out of diverse national elements (as was the case in Austria), for becoming a federal state is their only option of attaining freedom. As a result, the government of the Imperial State labored under a profound delusion when it imagined that it could satisfy the dual imperative of freedom and national self-expression among its various constituent parts by means of a constitution for unitary state. The attempts at reviving superseded ethnic particularisms by maintaining a general confederation, as well as negotiating with the Hungarians regarding a dual focal point for the state’s elliptical structure, served only to exacerbate Austria’s predicaments and rendered its internal turmoil ever more intractable.

However, it is precisely the federal state to which an hereditary principality submits with such reluctance, whereas its establishment proceeds with such great ease within free states. The reason for this lies in the fact that, within a federal state, where the highest stage of political liberty attained thus far has been reached, the monarch finds themselves stripped of their all-encompassing, all-devouring power of ultimate decision. While in a federal state, the individual constituent members possess greater autonomy, retaining their internal sovereignty, and the federal government concerns itself solely with external sovereignty (its primary domain being military power and foreign affairs), whereas the monarch seeks to be the sole possessor of this dual sovereignty.

A pivotal institution within the federal state is the Supreme Federal Court, for it is precisely this institution through which nations transition from the "state of nature" characteristic of international law into the civil and legally ordered realm of cosmopolitan law. In international law, states acknowledge no higher arbiter above themselves, leaving the question of right or wrong entirely to their own arbitrary discretion. Conversely, in cosmopolitan law, a federal court adjudicates disputes between citizens of different states, disputes between a citizen and a state, disputes between different states, and indeed, even disputes between an individual state and the supreme federal authority, such as whenever the interpretation of the constitution be in doubt, or in related matters. Since the Supreme Federal Senate is comprised of the specialized delegates of the individual states, just as the legislative chamber is comprised of delegates representing the entire populace of all member states, the juridical division of this Senate also serves as the Supreme Federal Court. 

#### Confederations

In our depiction of the ethical coexistence of human beings, if we have proceeded ever onward, moving from one voluntary association to another, ascending ever higher from the family up to the federal state, we have not yet reached the very pinnacle of the law of free association. This law was founded upon the principle of overcoming the natural one-sidedness of human beings by having them complement one another through shared activity. To the specific forms of one-sidedness associated with age, gender, education, profession, and ethnicity, added to this is the one-sidedness inherent to entire nations, a quality that finds its very expression in their distinct national character. If Napoleon I once asserted, "What one nation hates is another nation," then true international law must transform this hatred into love, ensuring that each nation not only establishes itself as a federal state, but as a third step, allows its own particularity to merge into the broader union of its shared culture. Thus, the various branches of peoples across the world may join together in a general form of association to attend to their common interests, thereby forming a confederation of states among themselves. In this context, the objective is no longer the creation of a unified state entity, but convening of an assembly of delegates at a designated federal seat, which must be constituted not merely by the executive branch of the respective states, but by all three branches acting together, and crucially, without its resolutions in any way infringing upon the sovereignty of the individual states, neither in their external relations nor in their internal affairs. The delegates remain bound by the instructions of those who commissioned them, essentially bearing the character of a treaty under international law. 

If the subject matter of the assemblies of delegates consists of the shared interests of a particular region, then we ultimately conceive that all regions of the world are capable of focusing their attention upon the collective interest of the entire globe and of the humanity that dwells upon it. The goal is to advance the affairs of mankind through the path of calm deliberation, and thus—as Ancillon puts it—to bring the "I" of the globe to conscious realization, acting as a visible Providence. Above all else, however, such a system would require a federal tribunal, one charged not only with arbitrating disputes specific to Asia, Europe, or the Americas, but also with settling conflicts arising between different regions, such as when the English and French intervened in China, or when Napoleon III launched his campaign against Mexico. Such a federal tribunal for humanity would, in turn, arise from the delegates of the individual federations of states, and would serve primarily to supplant the prevailing state of war among mankind with an era of perpetual peace.

This concept, pursued for a century and a half, from the Abbé de Saint-Pierre down to Kant, has already taken several steps toward its realization, even if humanity remains far from its end goal. Following the colossal wars that ravaged Europe at the beginning of the 19th century, the great powers of the continent sensed that it was high time to transition from this "state of nature" in international law to a truly juridical order. Thus, following the downfall of Napoleon I, the "Holy Alliance" was concluded, an alliance that sought to give serious effect to the modern ideal of humanity: the universal fraternity of nations. Initially, however, it was only the monarchs who convened at these congresses, such as those held at Aachen, Laibach, Verona, and elsewhere, to avert war through peaceful deliberation whenever the political life of the European nations, be it in Spain, Portugal, Piedmont, or Naples. The five great powers arrogated to themselves the role of arbiters, summoning weaker states before their tribunal and subjecting them to their rulings. This procedure supplanted the doctrine of the European Balance of Powers which had prevailed in the 18th century, under which the final decision had effectively been left to brute force. Christian charity was intended to displace force, but this all too soon degenerated into self-interest and the suppression of national liberty. Even earlier, Charles V, Henry IV, Louis XIV, and Napoleon I had all attempted to establish a universal monarchy—a system that would completely annihilate all other national identities of the individual members of humanity.

Owing to the ever-diversifying relations of the European Great Powers with Asia and America, several of them—England, France, and Russia—have not confined their influence to Europe, but have extended it across the entire globe. If we add North America to their number, then these four—acting as world powers—hold the destinies of the world in their hands, just as the European Great Powers hold those of Europe. The question is how to transform this *de facto* position of power into a *de jure* state, thereby helping international law advance from its raw state of nature to a rational order, transforming the "ought" into an "is." However, since this unconditionally rational state is not yet the existing reality, natural law must highlight whatever is rational within the currently given circumstances in order to guide it toward that unconditionally rational ideal. However, the irrational element of international law that was not merely in the formal principle that treaties *ought* to be observed, but also in their very *content* that direct followed from arbitrary will. Thus, since the origin of treaties is to be found in arbitrary will, it is conversely rational that such treaties are simply regarded as things that *ought* to be observed. For since the general welfare constitutes the supreme principle and guiding star for the conduct of any nation, a nation will uphold a treaty only as long as doing so appears advantageous to it, and will breach them the moment its own interest so demands. Moreover, since the ultimate arbiter of that interest is the arbitrary will, the fulfillment of treaties becomes an act of arbitrariness no less than their non-fulfillment. An international treaty is a promise given with the immediate, tacit intention of breaking it. Statecraft and morality are thus still at odds in our time, and diplomacy is a sophistical art.

But even if nations commit a wrong when they break treaties, the nature of such wrongdoing is not as simple here as it is in the sphere of private law. Instead, in the wrong committed against another people often lies the fulfillment of the highest ethical duty towards one's people, as we already know the philosopher king's statement that a ruler must even sacrifice their morality if the welfare of their people demands it. We do not wish to advocate for a "double standard" of morality, between one governing the relations between nations, and another governing the relations between individuals. As we did in our discussion of the theory of duties, we may speak here of a "collision" of duties, one that is lamentable to the highest degree. The historical development of nations may be advanced through immoral means, but this does not absolve the individual who committed the act of their personal responsibility for it. The laurel wreath placed upon the hero’s head, because they advanced the common good through a crime, does not serve to conceal their moral guilt. Likewise, if in the dealings between nations, cunning or violence has served as a means to advance the progress of humanity, this does nothing to absolve a nation of its guilt.

The sole task is to determine how such a collision of duties might be rendered impossible. If Kant envisions the attainment of this goal in the establishment of "perpetual peace," he proposes to bring about this peace by ensuring that political prudence and morality *coincide*—a union that is ultimately conditioned upon the adoption of free constitutions by all states that constitute the universal federation of humanity. For if all states were to set as their common objective the free  of humanity, then nations would no longer have any conflicting interests, and thus would have no reason to harm or take advantage of one another. Of what concern would a bordering territory to any single nation be, even if its inhabitants speak that nation's language? A nation is scarcely likely to embark upon conquests if its political organization coincides perfectly with its national identity. Nor will any nation seek to secure commercial advantages through warfare once absolute free trade has become the law of the land. But where such a state of affairs does not yet exist—where conflicting interests clash directly and without mediation, or where existing federal tribunals have rendered their arbitral verdicts only to be met with disobedience by the parties involved—there remains only the vengeance of nations as a means to cast off the actual or perceived injustices they have endured—war.

### Law of War

Here, we have to consider the origin of the war, its duration or conduct, and its end. However, all three moments are united by the standpoint that right prevails even in this context, just as in the sphere of private law we examined injustice in order to trace its transformation back into right. This applies all the more to war, as much as the injustice inflicted upon another people already inherently contains an ethical purpose in the restoring the well-being of that people. In this instance, the transformation of injustice into right proceeds far more evidently than it does in the sphere of private law.

#### Origin of War

The commencement of war must only be undertaken for the sake of right. Hugo Grotius, in his famous work *De Jure Belli et Pacis*, vigorously refutes the view of those who assert that law ceases to exist in wartime and argues that war can have no other purpose whatsoever than the restoration of violated rights. However, if right is the ultimate end, then the means employed for this purpose cannot be unjust, but must itself be lawful. Therefore, the primary principle is that no war may be undertaken without a just cause (*causa justificata*). To this end, a nation must first have exhausted all other means of securing its rights, and it is only when all else has failed, neither negotiations, nor the mediation of other states, nor any of the preliminary measures that precede actual hostilities, only then are the cannons, which are for this very reason, termed the *ultima ratio regum*, permitted to unleash their thunder.

The further the education of humanity advances, the closer it draws to its ideal archetype through the strengthening of a global council of nations, the sooner that wars will be nipped in the bud and the storm clouds gathering on the horizon of history be dispersed without recourse to armed force. Thus, where international tribunals already exist, the formal legal process must first be pursued to determine whether the opposing parties will submit to the dictates of rational judgment. Should even a court of international peace be lacking, a body acting as an arbitral authority that can resolve many disputes through amicable settlement, then the path of astute statecraft serves as a substitute for the sober course of law. But nowadays, at least in an effort to avert conflict, congresses, protocols, and diplomatic correspondence among individual powers precede hostilities, whereas in earlier times, intervention consisted of sporadic interference by uninvolved parties who undertook mediation at the behest of one adversary or the other.

However, even if all these negotiations and mediations have failed, this does not necessarily mean that war has broken out on that account, since the adversarial parties, left to their own devices, have only begun to take independent action against one another. The party with a grievance first recalls its own envoy and issues passports to the foreign ambassador. Neither nation maintains a presence within the other any longer and both retreat into its own stark isolation. However, even if one nation has recalled its envoy, the other, should it be averse to war, is under no obligation to follow suit. Further measures, taken before actual hostilities commence, involve countering hostile acts with countermeasures (reprisals). For instance, if one nation prohibits the ships of another from entering its ports, the latter may exercise retaliation. Commerce between the nations is suspended, where the ships of one nation, currently situated in the ports of the other, are placed under seizure (embargo). During the Continental System, Napoleon I employed such coercive measures against England on a massive scale, but a crucial distinction was that, given the impossibility of naval warfare and the impracticability of a landing in England, an actual war did not immediately follow in the wake of these measures.

It is self-evident that, in the case of all these measures, arbitrariness remains the decisive factor, particularly where the very basis of these steps, the justification for war, is concerned. Strictly speaking, there is only one just cause for war: the violation of a state’s independence or sovereignty. But in how many cases might a nation not perceive its rights to be violated? Much as in a duel between individuals, where a mere slight to one’s honor or an accidental physical contact can suffice, for war is a duel of nations (*duellum*). A nation may feel itself aggrieved by the smallest trifle, and it is arbitrariness that decides whether the offense is of a magnitude to warrant war. In the Middle Ages, a war once broke out between England and France solely over the killing of a single sailor, though it is arguable that this served merely as the immediate trigger that finally brought to the surface the deeper underlying causes of their enmity, such as England’s possession of the French coastline. If one asserts that war is justified only once an actual violation has occurred, the reply must be that it is already too late if one chooses to wait for the attack to commence. When an adversary fortifies their strongholds, outfits their fleet, and mobilizes their armies, a neighboring state cannot simply stand idle. "Every hammer-blow," said Cobden in a speech to the Peace Congress in Paris in 1849, "struck in Cherbourg, echoes in Plymouth; and if the French increase their fleet, the English must do likewise." Against France's colossal armies, the English fielded a large voluntary citizen army. Massive armaments can even serve to prevent war, in accordance with the maxim: *Si vis pacem, para bellum* (If you desire peace, prepare for war). Nevertheless, all political considerations, dependent as they are upon arbitrariness and indeterminacy, constitute no more of a just cause for war, from the standpoint of international law, than does the aim of advancing world history.

Even if a war is arbitrarily declared, it must never be commenced until it has been clearly and explicitly declared, both to the adversaries and to the uninvolved parties. The onset of hostilities must therefore be preceded by a formal declaration of war that sets forth the underlying reasons and enumerates the grievances. Only in this manner will the aggressor possess, at minimum, the semblance of right on their side. The party attacked, for its part, will likewise endeavor, through official state documents, to demonstrate that the right lies with them. Thus, the principle is indisputable: wars can only be waged solely for the sake of right, never for the extermination or annexation of other peoples. So even after the outbreak of hostilities, the principle of right remains the enduring basis of the conduct of war. Indeed, it was likely a sentiment of humanity that led to the omission of a formal declaration of war, as if one were unwilling to fully acknowledge the state of war, and remained ready at any moment to settle the dispute.

#### Conduct of War

As soon as war begins, all treaties previously agreed upon between the parties must be regarded as having lapsed, for the simple reason that the existing legal relations no longer satisfied the parties involved. However, the conduct of war does not thereby excuse lawlessness. Instead, following the shattering of previously existing rights, the nations now seek a better and more rational law. Herein lies precisely the rational-legal nature of war: that a higher form of law is meant to evolve from it, even if the war was initially undertaken solely to enforce the execution of a violated treaty.

The right that must be preserved during war above all else is the very personality of each individual nation. Therefore, a war of extermination (*bellum internecinum*) must never be waged, and constitutes the primary principle of international law in this context. For instance, the Turks once attempted to wage such a war against the Greeks. However, through this barbaric act, they were excluded from the sphere of European international law. The European nations put an end to this conduct through the Battle of Navarino, where a *triumvirate* (*triarchia*) comprising France, England, and Russia usurped the judicial authority, taking the place of the *pentarchy* (*pentarchia*). Why then, did another triumvirate, consisting of France, England, and Austria, not put a stop to the very same barbaric practice of the Russians against the Polish? Just as diplomatic envoys are inviolable in peacetime, so too must be the envoys of war during hostilities. Moreover, the internal institutions of a nation must not be violated, for it is precisely within these institutions that the nation's personality primarily resides, unless the deficiency of these institutions was the cause of the war. However, whether or not the cause is also a *just* one is questionable.

International law further demands that individual and family rights be respected, as much as combat is directly conducted between armed combatants, not against citizens or the defenseless. While earlier international law still permitted the plundering of a fortress taken by storm, even this last vestige of barbarism has largely fallen into disuse. Therefore, the enemy property liable to attack is exclusively public property. However if the property of individuals serves the enemy in the conduct of war against their adversary, then that property may be seized by said adversary, though the individuals concerned are entitled to compensation once the war has ended, much as is the case in civil wars. Such compensation must also be rendered in instances involving monetary contributions, the provisioning of enemy troops, the supply of goods, and similar exactions demanded of individuals or communities.

As a general rule, the question of what is or is not permissible in war must be judged by the overarching standard that the state is entitled to neutralize the enemy and nothing else. Ruses of war are not excluded from this scope. Any individual who surrenders should not be killed, but taken prisoner. However, the question arises: if prisoners cannot be conveyed to a place of safety, if there exists a risk that the enemy might liberate them, is their execution permissible? In this regard, Ségur observes in his *History of the Grand Army*, referring to an incident in which the French killed several thousand Russian prisoners *on fut cruel par nécessité* ("cruelty born of necessity"). To what extent the security of the state might be jeopardized were one to refrain from such an acts of cruelty is a matter that, in such circumstances, can only be left to the dictates of the commanding officer's own sense of humanity. The filling in of wells and the burning of supplies, much as the Southerners in North America did with their cotton during the last Civil War, were acknowledged as permissible acts. However, the poisoning of wells, arrows, and the like, is forbidden, even though such measures would undoubtedly inflict the greatest damage. One must regard the latter as constituting "dishonorable" weaponry, just as is the firing of chopped lead, whereas the new types of projectiles, which increase the severity of a wound, are deemed permissible. For this represents an improvement in weaponry that both parties can employ with equal fairness, whereas any party that refrains from using poison would find itself at a distinct disadvantage. Admittedly, everything ultimately depends upon the customs of the various nations, and barbaric peoples have no qualms of fighting with poisoned weapons.

In accordance with this principle, just as persons and property must be spared as much as possible during war, so too does war proceed to admit specific treaties and agreements between the belligerents within its own sphere, even though its commencement entailed the abrogation of all prior treaties between them. It is here that we see most clearly how war contains within itself its own opposite. To this category belongs armistices, agreements regarding the surrender of a fortress or a military detachment, the demarcation of a dividing line between opposing army positions, the exchange of prisoners, safe conduct for negotiators, and so forth. To breach any such agreement is contrary to international law, so the rule of law must remain valid even during war. Though, precisely because the ultimate adjudication of right is staked upon the point of the sword, such violations of international law during wartime remain entirely at the mercy of the belligerents' arbitrary will, and wherever they occur, they do not serve to advance the resolution of the legal dispute, but only provokes further retaliation that engenders greater bitterness in the conduct of the war.

If the belligerents themselves are not devoid of rights in their dealings with one another, but are also bound to hold property and treaties, even with respect to the enemy, how strictly must this principle apply to states that are not involved in the conflict? This brings us to the rights and duties of non-belligerents during a war. Since it is solely their impartiality that secures these rights for them, they are not permitted, for instance, to grant passage through their territory to any belligerent party, nor to provide them with equipment or supplies of any kind whatsoever. But even here, arbitrariness ultimately remains the decisive factor. A government turns a blind eye, claiming it cannot forbid the trade of its subjects, and permits assistance rendered by individuals, provided it is secretly favorably disposed toward one of the belligerent parties, as the English were during the American Civil War. However, toward those to whom a government wishes ill, it casts a sharp eye.

The rights and duties of neutral parties are of far greater importance in naval warfare than in land warfare, because in the latter, the theater of war is much easier to survey and much more readily delimited. Specifically, the belligerents must not encroach upon the territory of neutral parties, but must confine themselves to each other's territories. As a result, neutral parties often station border guards and watch the conflict with arms in hand, a stance that has been termed "armed neutrality." However, since the sea is a domain shared by all nations, a place where neutral parties and belligerents come into direct contact, it becomes more difficult to draw the line and prevent the subjects of a neutral power from participating in the war. For this very reason, the most intense disputes have historically erupted among the powers regarding this issue. Among the memoirs of Napoleon I written on St. Helena is a highly meritorious treatise on this very question, titled *Neutres*, and in the wake of the Treaty of Paris of March 30, 1856, his nephew made a significant contribution toward bringing this concept one step closer to realization in this sphere as well.

At sea, the barbaric mode of warfare, which targeted not only enemy combatants but also individual citizens, persisted for a much longer period than land warfare. Peaceful merchant vessels belonging to the enemy were routinely seized, along with their crews and cargo. Indeed, since neutral merchant vessels are encountered within this shared domain, they find themselves in the precarious position of being exposed to the belligerents, thereby becoming objects of suspicion, given that the belligerents' own vessels may hoist a neutral flag in order to evade the enemy. For the flag is merely a symbol, whereas solid ground and soil constitute an unmistakably given reality. Thus, ever since the Peace of Westphalia (1648) and the Peace of Utrecht (1712), it was established that the warships of belligerent states possessed the right to search neutral-flagged vessels and were obliged to recognize such a flag as valid only if the ship’s commander and at least half of its crew belonged to the neutral nation. As a result, the principle that "the flag covers the goods" prevailed, though subject to the exclusion of war matériel, military personnel, or other supplies serving defensive purposes. In short, if war goods (contraband) were destined for delivery to a port effectively blockaded by enemy vessels, in such instances, the ship would be seized and condemned by a prize court of the belligerent state.

By virtue of the immense naval supremacy that England attained, despite all of Bonaparte's efforts, it was able to alter these regulations in its own favor and gradually secure its recognition by other nations. Thus, England searched neutral vessels even when they were escorted by a warship of their own nation, a situation in which territorial jurisdiction was unequivocally indicated by the flag. Thus, it seized enemy goods found on neutral vessels, thereby overturning the principle that "the flag covers the goods," and engaged in similar practices. Consequently, Napoleon I exclaimed in exasperation: "The seas have now been surrendered to England as its private property!" Without tracing the various developments of this body of law in greater detail, England's dominance in the intervening period infringed upon these legal norms. This was until, through the Treaty of April 16, 1856 to which nearly all European and American powers acceded, it was established that not only does a neutral flag protect enemy goods, but even an enemy flag protects neutral goods.  

The Americans are willing to renounce privateering, a practice whereby enemy vessels may be seized not only by state warships but also by private vessels (privateers), only on the condition that all property belonging to belligerent nations, with the obvious exception of contraband, be exempt from seizure by enemy warships. In other words, they seek to establish for naval warfare, in accordance with the demands of Napoleon I, the same humane conduct of war that is already recognized in land warfare. This eminently reasonable principle has foundered against England's resistance. Nor can it be said that moderation lies on England's side, for in the matter of the search of the British vessel *Trent*, which was carrying enemy envoys and dispatches, the Americans made concessions, even though they would arguably have been within their rights to bring the entire ship before an American prize court. However, they were formally in the wrong, for instead of seizing the vessel, they allowed it to proceed unhindered, taking only the envoys as prisoners.

#### End of War (Peace)

When the conclusion of peace brings about the end of a war, whether because one side runs out of money and manpower, or because both are weary of the conflict, the conditions that the victor imposes upon the vanquished serve either serves to restore old treaties or to establish a new legal order, depending on the interests of the victor. However, this legal order is invariably established through by their own arbitrary will. Nevertheless, precisely because nations relinquish their one-sided biases against one another and draw closer together after the struggle, war serves as the means, as Kant observes, to eventually lead them, through mutual weariness and exhaustion, into a more civilized, juridical state. Thus, the very arbitrariness of the victor serves to advance the ultimate purpose of world history: the perfected freedom of both individuals and nations. Under the assumption that the victor, notwithstanding the arbitrary nature of their actions, has achieved their victory through the power of the spirit, the content of their arbitrary will advances the higher right of reason to which the individual must submit. Therefore, it is an injustice to Cato when Lucan has him declare: *Victrix causa Diis placuit, sed victa Catoni.* (The victorious cause pleased the gods, but the vanquished cause pleased Cato.) Just as vengeance, within the sphere of private law, may be just in its substance, even if it is never just in its form, so too does the same apply to war. Although, such actions can certainly exceed their bounds and prove to be unjust even in their content.

Just as the injustice that the vanquished is deemed to have committed may be finite and measurable, so too can it be infinite, for crime itself possesses this dual nature. In private law, if murder constitutes this infinite violation of freedom, then the infinite injustice committed by a nation is the violation of *humanity*, for its actions has placed it in a state of regress, whereas another nation, one that has already attained a higher stage in the development of humanity, is in a state of progress. Such a nation enjoys the favor of arms, for the spirit compensates for the disparity in numbers, as the Greeks demonstrated at Marathon in their victory against the overwhelming forces of the barbarians. From this victory follows the right of conquest for the victorious nation. But with this right, we have transcended the standpoint of international law, for the latter demands the absolute recognition of the free personality, even that of the vanquished nation, as well as the ratification of existing treaties or the conclusion of new ones. Territories may indeed be ceded, and monetary indemnities paid, but the entire nation must not be annexed, nor the national identity of the vanquished fused with that of the victor. We still frequently observe the contrary throughout world history, particularly in antiquity, where wars were struggles to the death. Even the great Carthage vanished, and ultimately all distinct national identities were absorbed into the spirit of the Roman people. Since this violates all international law, it would constitute an absolute injustice, unless there is a higher law stands above international law. In truth, this is the right of the World Spirit, to which nations must sacrifice themselves, just as every individual must sacrifice himself to his own nation. This higher standpoint, which we must now proceed to examine, is the standpoint of cosmopolitan right and world history. It is only in the unfolding of world history that the supreme archetype of freedom is ultimately realized within the spheres of law and the state, and even wars serve this very purpose. Because humanity is not by nature a participant in this supreme freedom and rationality, it requires a process of development through time in order to attain this standpoint. It is for this reason that all the blood in history has been shed: so that humanity might secure for itself, not only in reality, but also in consciousness and in thought, its supreme freedom. The nations that prove incapable of meeting this demand perish and are defeated by the nations that have come to partake in this higher concept. Thus, we may rightly designate conquerors as instruments in the hands of the World Spirit, just as Attila was called the "Scourge of God", because the Roman State was destined for destruction in order to make way, upon the stage of history, for the Germanic nations, as the bearers of a higher standpoint of freedom. World history is the world’s tribunal. All nations are at the mercy of this judgment, whether to undergo their own purification, or to be consumed as instruments for the sake of progress.

Yet even earlier nations, having paid their tribute to history and sacrificed their independence to the cause of humanity, thereby acquires the right to rise again as members of the World Spirit, and to have their national character rejuvenated from a higher vantage point. Thus, older nations that were conquered never lost their national identity, and likewise wrested themselves free from the clutches of their conquerors. The world has since transformed, nations change along with their customs, languages, and habits, they outgrow their former selves and shift their borders in the process. The universal brotherhood of all nations is destined to become the very heaven of the restoration of all things, no nation shall be abandoned to the hell of annihilation, rather each shall merely pass through the purgatory of history to enter into paradise.

## Cosmopolitan Law

If nations have cast off their natural one-sidedness through war and have united into one great union through peace, then man is completely restored from the fragmentation into which he had fallen through civil society. The individual who proves themselves to be the active agent of all ethical relationships ranging from the family to human society, embodies the species within themselves. Just as the human being was formerly an unconscious and necessary manifestation of the species by nature, so now, through free self-knowledge, they stand as the perfected exemplar of that species. By each person developing this character of the species through generalized education, the highest goal of historical development is achieved: the Eternal Personality of the Spirit, which presents itself as the Archetype of Reason in the individual. Firstly, when this archetype is placed directly before the senses as a work of human creation, it is Art. Secondly, when this archetype is conceived as a transcendent reality that has assumed personal form and is envisioned within the realm of faith, it is Religion. Finally, when this archetype is re-created within the thinking spirit of the individual, it is Science. It is these three spheres that weave a single, unifying bond around the entire human race. Even though Art may initially have been a specifically national expression, the Beautiful, in all its manifestations across all nations, becomes the common heritage of all mankind. In the sphere of religion, the universal, triumphant Church weaves a bond of fraternity around all mankind, even though the struggle among the various national churches has proven tenacious. Finally, science unites all peoples into one great commonwealth (the *république des lettres*), wherein all national distinctions are destined to vanish entirely. Thus, the rights of world citizenship will manifest themselves, beyond the Federal Tribunal of Humanity, in a universal art, a universal religion, and a universal commonwealth of the sciences. The representatives of these domains, drawn from the corresponding sections of the Federal Senate, shall sit in the Areopagus of Humanity to advance the common interests pertaining to these fields, just as the Federal Tribunal of Humanity is charged with determining the law in disputes arising across all nations.

### World Art

#### Subjective Art (Design)

The first moment of world art manifests itself as the art of the useful. If man reveals the finitude of his nature through his sensory needs, then we have seen, in terms of luxury and in ever-advancing industrial diligence, how bare necessity clothes itself in ever more beautiful forms, and how the functional and the agreeable correspond increasingly to the archetype of the Ideal. Thus, even within common necessity, a higher, spiritual need is satisfied, man is guided from the gratification of his natural needs to his need for beauty, and where the fragmentation of existence emerges most sharply amidst the infinite multitude of needs, man is restored as a unified whole. Art ennobles the human spirit, drawing it away from the merely natural aspect of necessity when man contemplates and finds beauty even in the most commonplace of implements. For this reason, the Greeks were such a highly cultivated people because they clothed their entire environment, vessels, lamps, and weapons in the raiment of beauty: just as Odysseus, with supreme craftsmanship, personally fashioned his own marriage bed. As metropolitan cities serve as the driving force behind the advancement of beautiful forms, fashion spreads anew across the entire globe. World Exhibitions, such as those witnessed in London, Paris, Dublin, New York, and elsewhere, serve to make the achievements of every nation the common property of all others. The prizes awarded at these events, instead of being distributed by a random assortment of delegates from various nations, should, in the future, be bestowed by the artists of our Areopagus.

#### Objective Art (Fine Art)

Works of fine art correspond not only to the form of the archetype, but also represents its content, not merely a natural need. Architecture forms the transition from the beauty of utility to the beauty of the archetype. On the one hand, architecture still serves practical needs, such as residential dwellings, public institutions, and religious needs, until it finally rises, independently and in its own right, to become a work of fine art in itself. Just as the costumes and customs of nations draw ever closer to one another as they elevate themselves into the forms of the beautiful, so too do their works of art become accessible to all. Travelers from across the globe admire the pyramids and obelisks of the Egyptians, the Parthenon of Athens, and the Greek temples at Paestum, recognizing in them the creations of the human spirit. The art of every nation belongs to all, works of fine art are entities that increasingly enter into the realm of common property. As a result, the limitation of the scope of individual ownership will invariably lead to an expansion of the concept of human freedom in general. Whoever rediscovers the archetype of beauty rises above the limitations of their national culture and embraces the whole of humanity. Art schools, art academies, and art exhibitions are, once again, matters falling under the administration of the Areopagus of Humanity, although federations, states, and even municipalities can maintain departments dedicated to this sphere of human activity.

#### Absolute Art (Culture)

However, it is insufficient for man to merely clothe his external possessions in the form of The Beautiful, nor to view this form as an independent entity detached from himself. Finally, man must transform *himself* into a work of art, presenting himself as the embodiment of living beauty. On the stage, actors—whom the Greeks so aptly termed *hypocrites* (ὑποκριταί)—present to us the semblance of The Beautiful, so that the individual, returning to life from the spectacle, can strive ever more earnestly to realize this presented archetype within their own existence. Hence, Schiller rightly designated the stage a "moral institution", even if this is not necessarily *the* highest purpose of art. Furthermore, this category encompasses *tableaux vivants*, singing, improvisational poetry, and dance, or in short, what is known *refined sociability*. If we demand that the worker find repose in their later years, thereby entering into a contemplative existence in which they can enjoy the fullness of their humanity within the sanctuary of their own personality, then every single day must serve as a mirror of this entire life. Following the arduous toil of the day, every worker enters into a state of repose in the evening, exchanging the gravity of business for the realm of play, which is also, according to Schiller, an essential facet of art. It is for this reason that, at this particular hour of the day, we seek out activities such as theater, dance, and social gatherings.

In modern times, society increasingly assumes the character of *public* sociability, and alongside residential life, the broader community arises from it. While the cooperatives, dedicated to the earnest purpose of labor, found their unifying center primarily within the muncipality, the community—where citizens associate with one another through their common humanity—serves as the natural home for refined sociability, existing alongside the political sphere within the general community association. Neighbors gather at the clubhouse not only for mutual instruction, but also for recreation, where they have access to a reading room, a game room, a ballroom, and the like. Voluntary associations for communal meals, sports, and excursions naturally align themselves with this framework, in these contexts, the community as a whole organizes and plans such festivities and in the management of the association’s domestic affairs. While the Spartans were compelled to eat their "black broth" in common, ensuring that their underdeveloped individuality remained subservient to the general will of the state in all matters, the modern individual voluntarily transforms the satisfaction of their particular needs into a communal experience, thereby enabling them to enjoy the universal, i.e. The Beautiful, in the most immediate and personal manner. Within such public societies, activities such as singing, recitations in both verse and prose, theatrical performances, *tableaux vivants*, dancing, and the like are undertaken. It goes without saying that, in our state, public affairs should be touched upon even during these social gatherings, so that every participant is animated by the very breath of public life.

### World Religion

While in art, the archetype appears as the work of man himself, the individual nevertheless recognizes himself as inadequate to this archetype. Thus, even though the archetype belongs to him, it detaches itself from his individuality to become, in his imagination, one or even several transcendent figures. This is the standpoint of religion, hitherto the highest expression of liberty, equality, and fraternity, and thus of human rights, as much as all individuals rediscover themselves, within their universal essence (which they call God), as one and the same being.

#### Religious Life

Religious life, conceived as the inner disposition of the individual, constitutes the innermost foundation and core of civic life. When all citizens recognize one another as brothers of a common origin, the mutual love shared by all transforms into a love for the nation, where one stands for all and all for one. It has therefore been said that no laws are prescribed for the pious, since the inner disposition of the religious standpoint takes the place of external forms. Only in this sense can it be said that the state stands upon the ground of divine authority. However, it is precisely the distinctive nature of the state to stand with its other foot firmly planted in reality and finitude, and to unfold this infinite *in-itself*, this inner sanctity, into external existence and the manifold distinctions of appearance. If this the true sense in which religion is made the principal bond of the state, there are nevertheless plentiful, and altogether pernicious, modes of relating religion to the state. Even the Romans, who gave the word its name, intended to bind people's minds (*religare*) so that they could force them into obedience to the nobility through superstition. In the Middle Ages, religion was henceforth employed by the Pope even to undermine the cohesion of political life. Religion was intended to divert people from the interests of this earthly existence toward a yearning for the hereafter, and to preach a supposed "cosmopolitan right", defined as the subjugation of all nations to the authority of the Papacy, whereas true cosmopolitanism upholds all distinct nationalities within the broader sphere of humanity. In modern times, absolute monarchy has utilized religion as leading strings for the citizenry, seeking to create a counterweight to nascent political liberty, thereby causing the political struggle between priestly and royal power to unfold, in which each sought to instrumentalize the other for the sake of its own domineering ambitions.

In antiquity, the essential unity that bound all citizens together remained inextricably linked to, and thus conditioned by, the natural circumstances of birth and national identity. Only an Athenian felt a unity of essence with another Athenian, or at most, a Greek with another Greek, at least to the extent that they placed universal deities above their particular, local divinities. Athena was the goddess *and* the state of Athens, and Hera was that of Argos, even though Zeus, the supreme civic deity, remained common to them all. Thus, even among the ancients, a sense of fraternity did not yet exist between one nation and another. Indeed, even people residing within the same land were not accorded equal rights if they belonged by birth to a foreign tribe, a circumstance from which the strict laws against foreigners and the institution of slavery originated. The reason why the wars waged in antiquity were struggles to the death lies precisely in the fact that thought had not yet progressed from the concept of nationality to that of humanity. It was Christianity that first replaced the religion of the nation with the religion of humanity. Feuerbach is entirely correct in observing that Christianity conceived of the nature of the divine not as a natural object, but as the essence of man, for Christianity teaches that God became man. This is also why Voltaire aptly remarked that, if God created man in His own image, man certainly returned the favor.

As much as Christianity, though itself a particular religion, conceives of the divine not as a particular national spirit, but rather as the universal human spirit, it is precisely capable of being the universal religion of humanity. Admittedly, this can only be true if it does not cling to its strict and limited dogma, not as the various denominations have ossified it, but instead, as W. E. Channing puts it, strives to weave the common bond of love around all the peoples of the globe, notwithstanding the differences among these denominations.

If religious disputes—and the bloody wars that have sprung from them—are drowned out in a "religion of reason," as Kant envisions, then the true relationship between religion and the state arises spontaneously from the principles of rational law. Just as little as we want to destroy national identity and lump all peoples together, so too must religious faith, whether of the various Christian denominations or the denominations of other faiths, be left entirely free. In other words, there must be no state church where only the adherents of a single sect possess civil rights, for such an arrangement fosters the dominance of a single party. In our state, not only must the inner sanctuary of conscience not be intruded upon, but we are also not satisfied with the maxim of Frederick the Great, who permitted everyone to seek salvation in their own way. Instead, by ensuring that every individual, enjoying complete freedom of conscience, is entitled to practice and express their faith without hindrance, whatever it may be, they shall, even if they outwardly subscribe to no specific creed, enjoy precisely the same rights as all others. Nor shall the state indirectly coerce a religious confession from its officials, whether through favoritism, discrimination, or similar means, for such actions serve only to foster hypocrisy. The best means of avoiding such things, however, has already been specified in the archetypal state, according to which the administrative officials are not appointed by the executive power, but are elected by the workers themselves. Even if church councils, which are themselves the product of elections within religious congregations, prove to be intolerant, then then the electors have no grounds for complaint, as the fault lies with them, and moreover, the resulting harm is not so great, for the church councils do not have the power of the state behind them.

#### Church Organization

By virtue of the relationship that religious life exists within the state, it must also enjoy a legal existence therein. This constitutes, secondly, the church—an institution which, like all institutions, must have freedom as its foundation. The church must be formed as a free association, in which those who share the same religious convictions may join. In this context, historically established denominations can claim no precedence over those that have recently formed. Furthermore, one cannot be inducted into such an association by others—such as parents, guardians, or teachers—nor is birth a determining factor. Instead, once one’s powers of judgment have matured, the free decision as to whichever community one wishes to belong rests entirely with the individual. From this, the equal entitlement of new religious communities vis-à-vis those already in existence follows as a matter of course. As a result, these new religious communities must not be denied the rights of association, for otherwise they would be unable even to acquire a meeting house in their own name, should the local community refuse to grant them the use of a clubhouse.

Self-government prevails here to an even greater degree than in all other associations of civic life, for it is precisely here that the complete equality of all members is preeminently realized, for as the saying goes, there is no respect of persons before God. There can be no question here of supervision by state authorities, nor of patronage by the nobility, the cities, or the state itself. Each congregation bears the costs of divine worship and freely elects its own speakers, who may even arise spontaneously within the assembly as the Spirit moves each individual, much as is the case among the Moravians. The solicitation of financial support from the municipality, the province, or the state—a practice we generally acknowledged as permissible elsewhere—would only serve as a lingering reminder of the old state with which the church remains outwardly intertwined. Just as it elects its speakers, the congregation also chooses its elders who administer the church’s assets and attend to all other ongoing affairs. In this purely democratic constitution, they represent an "aristocracy of merit," whereas the speaker holds the executive authority. But throughout this system, no rigid hierarchy or chain of command prevails, the sole authority and influence resides in the power of the Spirit. Named after the elders (*presbyteroi*), this initial, primordial structure has come to be known as the presbyterial constitution. In the apostolic era of Christianity, it was the sole form of governance, and it has invariably arose wherever the church was left to its own devices, proving itself to be the constitution most suited to the Church’s nature, precisely because it is the most democratic. Such was the constitution of the early Christians during the Roman persecutions, of the Huguenots in France, and of the Presbyterians in Scotland. 

The two other forms of church polity that have evolved throughout the history of Christianity are incompatible with its spirit and its constitution grounded in natural law. The second of these—that which took shape during the Catholic Middle Ages—is the hierarchical or episcopal constitution, which continues to prevail in the Old Church to this day. This priestly autocracy, inextricably linked to the doctrine of papal infallibility, stands in direct opposition to freedom of conscience. While the Pope was once bound by the authority of church councils, these assemblies eventually fell into disuse. Throughout this entire period, the dominion of the higher clergy over the lower, as well as the priesthood over the laity, remained an undisputed reality. Just as this church exerts an undue internal influence upon the consciences of its adherents, even though it is this conscience that ought to be the very source of faith, so too has it become secularized in its external aspect. The church was transformed into a state governed by priests, reverting the Christian world to the very infancy of its cultural development. It is to be hoped that our own era will very soon witness the dissolution of this unnatural union of secular and ecclesiastical power.

Once the medieval struggle between state and church had been fought to its conclusion, and as Protestantism gave rise to a church independent of the Papacy, these churches embraced a presbyterial constitution only in lands where the religion of the ruling prince—which coincided with the state religion—remained Catholic. Conversely, the princes who broke away from Catholicism placed themselves at the head of their peoples, who in turn accorded them the status of bishops of their church within the framework of Protestant ecclesiastical law. Thus, the church became entirely fused with the state in a third form of organization—the consistorial constitution—whereby the prince, acting as the supreme bishop of the church, administered its affairs through his secular authorities. As a result, a form of state tutelage over the church had appeared, standing in direct opposition to the church's own self-governance. Although the political upheavals of 1848 guaranteed the autonomy of *both* the Catholic and the Protestant churches, it essentially remains a contradiction to attempt to facilitate this self-determination by means of a maneuver in which a specific division of the Ministry of Ecclesiastical Affairs is detached in name only from the jurisdiction of the Minister, only to remain subject to the influence of the Head of State in their capacity as the "foremost member of the Church."

But should this imply that there should be absolutely no connection whatsoever between church and state? As a free association, the church is a distinct entity from the state, but by virtue of its substantive content, it has a more universal character. As a legal entity existing in the external world, it must enter into the sphere of the state. However, the legal relationship between church and state can be none other than the very same relationship that other individuals and associations have with the state. The church council of our state is a senatorial department that is just as independent of the executive power as are all other councils. If a secular prince simultaneously acts as high priest, or a high priest simultaneously acts as a secular prince, then we would have a theocracy. In such a system, the innermost essence of doctrine and the faith of the individual conscience are turned into matters of external command. Protestant churches have never gone to such lengths, they have never directly punished unbelief with chains and fire. However, the Protestant state has sought to punish those of differing faiths indirectly by withdrawing their civil rights, and to foster doctrinal orthodoxy by granting secular priviledges. Even if they believed they had left individual consciences free and refrained from encroaching upon inner convictions, it nevertheless arrogated to itself dominion over every external manifestation of religion, that is to say, the freedom of worship. This prerogative was termed the *jus sacrorum* of the Protestant sovereign. It was put into practical application in Prussia, for example, when Frederick William III—with the assistance of his Protestant bishop, Eylert—devised a liturgy and recommended it to the congregations for adoption, and when he encountered resistance, he added the caveat that he possessed the authority to issue a direct command. However, if public worship is the expression of public doctrine, it follows as a matter of course that the public must conform to that doctrine. As a result, the authority charged with determining the doctrine must likewise provide it with its appropriate form. Thus, in the dispute of that time between Marheineke and Pacificus Sincerus (Schleiermacher), we must side with the latter who challenged the sovereign’s liturgical authority.

The relationship between church and state, as determined by the principles of natural law, can therefore be none other than this: that the state intervenes through its courts wherever individuals or associations infringe upon private or public rights. The religious confession of the head of state remains entirely irrelevant in this context, as the state is bound to exercise this authority impartially with respect to all confessions. As a result, such restrictions upon the church cannot be designated, as is customary in Protestant ecclesiastical law, as *jus majestaticum circa sacra*. This authority is in no way distinct from any other exercise of judicial power, nor may it be allowed to degenerate into a form of paternalistic police supervision. Furthermore, if the state is not empowered to prescribe religious doctrine in the first place, neither may it pass judgment upon the inherent danger or harmfulness of specific religious concepts. Thus, with regard to doctrine *qua* doctrine, the state has no authority whatsoever—not even the power of prohibition—even if it were technically capable of exercising such power. Instead, the state may intervene only when a doctrine, by manifesting itself in the external world, infringes upon existing rights. In such instances, the objects of intervention would invariably be the specific *actions*—whether originating from the doctrine itself or occurring during acts of worship—and the adjudication of such matters would fall exclusively within the jurisdiction of the ordinary courts of law. However, should the administrators or spokespersons of a religious community be found to have acted in contravention of that community’s own internal ecclesiastical regulations, they must—like members of any other professional association—be held accountable before the court of honor of their respective professional division within the municipal, provincial, or state council.

#### Ecumenical Council

Thirdly, the synodal constitution joins the presbyterial constitution as its complement. For just as the administration of ecclesiastical affairs common to various lands and regions must be exercised by divisions of the federal courts and the Areopagus of Humanity, so too must the general, enduring institutions of the church—such as the formulation of doctrine and worship—be deliberated upon by legislative assemblies arising from elections held within the individual congregations. These are the synods, ranging from the provincial and state synods up to the general church assembly encompassing the entire globe. It goes without saying that these assemblies are not exclusive to the clergy—as is the case in the Catholic Church—but that freely elected laypeople participate in them as well. The distinction between clergy and laity is destined to diminish ever more within the Protestant Church, and ultimately to vanish entirely. In this regard, it will depend upon the will of the individual congregations whether they choose to deliberate in synods composed of congregations sharing the same confession, or whether mixed synods, disregarding differences in creed, convene to deliberate upon matters common to all confessions.

The shared nature of all religious life, constituting the ultimate goal of the general church assembly, leads us back to the very root and origin of religion. If this is rooted in the premise that all human beings are merely modes and manifestations of the One infinite Being. If this is the doctrine that is common to all religions, then it follows that the true goal of all religions is the unity of the spirit, i.e. the universal love for humanity, wherein all saints recognize themselves as One Saint. If distinct national identities should not dissolve into a vague uniformity, then neither should religions. Despite the distinctiveness of religions, they should remain conscious of their mutual kinship. The truly universal, triumphant church must present itself as the common bond uniting all creeds—a bond that is far better suited than the archetype of Art to fully awaken the self-consciousness of humanity. Unlike from the standpoint of Art, the self-consciousness of humanity is not recognized within a single, sensuous manifestation, but as a universal, non-sensuous essence: the collective individual whom we might term *Sanctus Humanus*. However, from the standpoint of religion, the Eternal Personality of the Spirit still appears as a unity of multiplicity rather than a unity of pure thought. That this final barrier to human self-consciousness should now fall, that the principle of universal citizenship should grasp the unity of human nature through the medium of pure thought, constitutes the standpoint of science: the discipline tasked with guiding humanity toward this supreme unity through the power of education.

### World Science

Through science, the distinctions between nations have been entirely transcended, and their specific national characteristics have merged into a universal humanity. There is no such thing as French or English astronomy, geography, natural science, and so forth. At most these distinctions appear in philosophy, specifically the Greek and the Christian traditions, acting as the representatives of the ancient and the modern eras. But even these distinctions—as well as the contrasts that have arisen within the Christian world regarding the philosophies of different nations—are now in the process of dissolving. It was, however, in philosophy that national characteristics were most likely to manifest themselves. For philosophy deals with fundamental principles that must ultimately be reconciled into a unified whole, whereas in the empirical sciences, these fundamental principles tend to recede into the background. Within the sphere of science, freedom has attained its highest pinnacle. As a result, the state should intervene the least of all—and certainly should not exercise paternalistic guardianship—in the process of cultivating individuals toward this freedom. It is here that self-governance is most fully and appropriately in its element.

This leads us to consider the contrast between public education and domestic education, and while Aristotle (in *Politics* VIII, 1) grants precedence to public education, this might appear to contradict the premises we have just established. One often hears it argued that patriotism tends to vanish wherever public education is absent. But when one observes the dominant political faction in contemporary public life employing public education as a political instrument—seeking to indoctrinate the youth with their own reactionary views for generations to come—one would be compelled to opt for private, individualized education for this very reason. Although, this objection can be countered in that the same party does not always hold power, and moreover, that family upbringing exerts an even greater influence, especially upon one’s civic disposition. If we have advocated for public education (which is, after all, indispensable for academic instruction), then the question arises: could a compromise not be struck between public and domestic education—one in which public instruction and self-governance no longer stand in opposition to one another?

Above all, the paternalistic guardianship of the state over education must cease. One may view compulsory schooling as historically justified on the one condition that not every family possesses the intellectual and moral cultivation necessary to realize that they should send their children to school. In the rational state, where this realization prevails universally, compulsory schooling naturally falls away of its own accord. All schools arise from educational associations in which every family who wishes for their children to receive instruction contributes to the school tax in proportion to their income. The municipal, provincial, and state councils administer education in precisely the same manner as they do other spheres of labor. Thus, we may designate the instruction resulting from this principle of voluntarism and self-governance as "public" instruction, by virtue of its communal nature, but equally as "private" instruction, to the extent that it is funded by voluntary associations of families. Within the municipality, family associations will collectively establish the general education necessary for all citizens, whereas specialized vocational education will be entrusted to worker associations. Finally, higher education, encompassing the universities and academies, falls under the purview of the state, the federal governments, and the Areopagus of Humanity, with experts selected from the various circles of educators, forming the administrative departments at these various levels.

#### General Education

Just as in organic life, the internal process of formation is constantly supported by the external process of assimilation, whereby the latter continually supplies the former with fresh material, so too is the internal intellectual life of the state strengthened by a process of assimilation. We have already observed the first of these: the process through which individual nations exchange their products and ideas, their customs and institutions, thereby constantly supplying one another with fresh material. The internal generation of material, however, consists of the education of the newly rising generation. This perpetual preparation and appropriation of new material, whereby the youth are elevated to the general spirit, constitutes, in the first instance, public education. It is particularly essential for parents that their children are not left to fend for themselves when they are away at work during the day, but that they are entrusted to the care of the community through the agency of family associations. Falling under this category is the initial care of children, ranging from the most tender age up to early childhood, such as infant nurseries, infant care centers, kindergartens, and other institutions of a similar nature. All such matters are to be treated by the local community, for instance, by locating the kindergartens within the premises of the community association hall.

For the period of childhood, the elementary school then takes over as the institution where formal instruction *as such* is imparted, and where the other two facets of education—discipline and instruction—are added to the physical education provided by the institutions mentioned earlier. Physical education is continued in the form of gymnastics, laying the foundation for the general defensive capability of the nation. As for the subjects of elementary instruction, they are those that cultivate the human being *as a human being*, quite apart from any specific social station, for a person must first assimilate what is *human* before proceeding to the specific requirements of a professional vocation. Therefore, one might argue that religious instruction is entirely appropriate within elementary school, particularly Christian instruction, given that Christianity is intended to be the religion of humanity. However, since religion is exclusively a matter for the church, provision for such instruction must be made exclusively by the religious associations themselves. The state and society must refrain entirely from interfering with this domain to avoid any semblance of a state religion and to ensure that the school does not become a denominational institution. A church congregation is free to provide educational services if it so desires, but the church must not exert the slightest influence upon public schools (which are also conceived here as free associations), which implies that no member of the clergy may serve as a school inspector. Let the state or society be labeled "godless" by the Church on this account (as in the maxim *la loi est athée*), it is by no means godless to refuse to regulate the doctrine of God through external state institutions. In addition to the rudiments of the natural and historical sciences, the scope of this instruction encompasses basic writing, arithmetic, and similar subjects, and only these fall within its proper domain. For man is a human being if and only if as he is also a particular individual belonging to a specific people. 

Ultimately, however, the adult citizen should enjoy the benefits of knowledge and instruction throughout their lives from the very beginning. Not for a moment should they be excluded from the intellectual life of the nation, but through the medium of science—no less than through art and religion—they should be elevated above the limitations of their particular social station and raised to the level of universal humanity. As a result, the distinct drive for knowledge—for an accurate understanding of nature, of civic institutions, and the like—manifests itself across all stations of society. This need for general education is met partly by local communities, incorporating the mutual instruction of one association by another, and partly by the establishment of public libraries, an initiative into which existing lending libraries can be readily integrated. Also falling under this category are vocational and religious schools, as well as public lectures—which are not the exclusive prerogative of universities—delivered to the general public either by individuals, by scientific associations, or by the municipality itself, without the need for police interference or permission. Here, science appears in its noblest guise as knowledge applied to life. Its supreme triumph lies in rendering itself universally intelligible to the people rather than shunning the masses. Finally, mention must be made of the distribution of popular literature—though certainly not in the narrow sense of religious advertisement which disseminates religious tracts among the populace for the sole purpose of reviving a stagnant orthodoxy. But in the spirit of universal liberty, we would be the very last to seek the suppression of such associations.

#### Vocational Education

Just as the purpose of elementary education is to elevate the individual as such to the level of general civic enlightenment, secondly, so must the people organize themselves according to their internal distinctions and select from the general body of assimilated knowledge the specific instruments suited to their particular needs, mediated through the free choice of the individuals themselves. This constitutes vocational education, or intermediate instruction. To this category belongs agricultural schools, trade schools, commercial schools, art schools, seminaries, and academic schools. Since the administration of these institutions are much more comprehensive than elementary schools, they are best entrusted to the hands of the provincial or regional subdivisions of the populace. Thus, whereas elementary instruction falls under the purview of the local community, with the designated experts in elementary education holding seats on the municipal council, those responsible for intermediate instruction within the municipality take their place within the corresponding departments of the provincial administration.

Even if specialized vocational training is ruled out, it still must be taken into account to the extent that the youth becomes conscious of their future vocation. If educational associations possess the necessary financial resources, including property, they may be able to discern the aptitudes of the youth early on. Thus, teachers and pupils of vocational schools can live together, forming a kind of small society or large family, much as the Pestalozzi Foundation does. For the vocational school is intended to serve as a preparation for life within society, presenting the image of public life in miniature with its attendant rewards and punishments. The apprentices in training are engaged in work, thereby earning a portion of the funds required for their specialized education. 

#### Higher Education

Finally, higher education leads the individual arising from the fragmentation of specialized vocational training back to the realm of the universal, general education, but at a higher level. Higher education now demonstrates how all the sciences originate from a common source: the science of sciences—philosophy. If this is the mission of the university, it derives its name not from the fact that it teaches *everything* (though it is true that one can learn just about anything at a university), but from the fact that it teaches *all* the sciences. The specialized faculties are nothing other than the specific sciences that are realized in the spirit of universal science. Therefore, the rigid medieval segregation of subjects into distinct faculties must be abolished, and the privilege of lecturing across all faculties must be extended to every instructor within the institution. At the same time, it becomes evident that access to the university must not be restricted on the basis of wealth, nor to those have attended preparatory schools, nor merely to those aspiring to profession that requires certification. Anyone who intends to dedicate themselves to agriculture, industry, and similar fields must also be granted the opportunity to examine their own specialized disciplines—chemistry, physics, technology, medicine, botany, commercial studies, and the like—from this highest possible vantage point.

As the highest stage of instruction, the university links the two lower stages together in the following manner. While primary education is concerned primarily with the education of the whole, undivided human being, without yet distinguishing specific spheres of activity, secondary education focuses on specialized disciplines (which, as empirical sciences, deal with given and existing subject matter), marks the advent of true intellectual training. Here, a specific aspect of the external world is singled out and made the object of human *representation*, a faculty that relates passively to its object and is thus directly related to a human need or activity. In higher education, philosophy—as the science of reason—makes the differentiations of the intellect into their own intellect. By drawing upon the full richness of his education, man neither leaves what he learns as it is given, nor merely passively absorbs and memorizes them as individual objects of his consciousness, but that he generates them from within himself through the power of thought—a power which he simultaneously apprehends as the very core of the external world itself.

If the highest freedom unfolds most unhindered within science in general, we ascend once again to its very summit in philosophy—the quintessential science of the university. Academic freedom, as the supreme imperative of constitutional law, is particularly indispensable to higher education. Academic freedom has become so proverbial, the first state constitution to recognize this supreme expression of liberty in plain terms was the German Imperial Constitution of March 28, 1849—followed by the Prussian Constitution: "Science and its teachings are free." These documents thereby revealed themselves, in a manner worthy of the name, as the constitutions of the intellectual state. Here, it is primarily a matter of applying Luther’s maxim: that one must allow minds to clash with one another. The notion that universities should remain bound by a specific creed, much like the old English institutions of Oxford and Cambridge, is entirely contrary to the very concept of a university. By contrast, the founding of the independent University of London, as well as the plans that emerged in Germany in 1848 and similar initiatives, are truly worthy of commendation.

The administration of the universities is once again their own affair, specifically as it concerns the internal workings of the institutions themselves. We must restrict academic jurisdiction to matters pertaining to the university as a community, specifically regarding discipline and instruction. However, in matters of general civil law, students must be referred to the ordinary courts. With regard to external administration and the relationship between various universities, their representatives must sit within the education department of the state council, where they attend to the common affairs of all the state's universities. They also constitute the higher appellate bodies for secondary and primary education, which also must have their own representatives within the state council. The universities integrate themselves into the state as a necessary constituent element, for they may still be regarded as institutions capable, through their faculties, of meeting the needs of the state. Henceforth, the faculties must not exist as closed guilds of specialized scholars, rather they must merge into a general community of all teachers—a body in which each individual, according to their specific aptitude, lectures on this or that particular branch of knowledge that rests upon the foundation of universal science. Just as the administrative officials of the university receive commensurate remuneration from their respective associations for their services, so too shall university teachers receive their honorary stipends. This can also include a share in the proceeds derived from the university's assets, should the institution possess such property. Teachers who attract no students will be compelled by this system to abandon their academic careers and pursue a path that is better suited to their abilities.

If science is still perceived as a means to an end within the universities, due to its relationship to the purposes of the state, science, then in the academies, even the last vestige of this perception has vanished. In the academy, science exists purely for its own sake. According to Schleiermacher, the masters gather in the academy for their own sake, whereas in the university, the student enrolls in a class taught by a teacher for the purpose of instruction. In the academies, by contrast, instruction is no longer a purpose at all. The academicians instruct neither one another nor the general public, they simply advance the frontier of science and establish, before the entire commonwealth of scholars, the extent of its progress. As a result, the sphere of the academies’ connections is no longer restricted to unitary or federal states, but extends to confederations of states and to humanity as a whole. The academies form scientific associations spanning from nation to nation, convening scientific assemblies encompassing the entire globe and aligning themselves with the Areopagus of Humanity. In such gatherings of scholars delegated from every corner of the earth, the highest level of activity unfolds within the commonwealth of sciences, as participants engage with the classification of the sciences, meteorological observations, geographical measurements, and other scientific endeavors that pertain to the entire globe. Here, humanity comprehends itself most purely as unified self-consciousness of the globe, precisely because it does so in the very form of thought itself.

With this highest rung of freedom, we conclude our exposition of the archetype of right and the state, having traced its development from the freedom of the individual person to the freedom of humanity as a whole. The mutual assimilation of all peoples has, within the realm of science, elevated itself into a genus process, one in which the Law of Reason and the State of Reason have taken shape as the very insight that apprehends the entire world as the edifice of Reason. It constitutes the positive representation of humankind, wherein all peoples consciously strive for peaceful union with one another, whereas war was the negative aspect of this genus process—a phase in which peoples certainly recognized one another, but out of hatred and girded with the sword, ready to deal death to one another within that iron embrace. For even if national identities are purified and resurrected through this death, ultimately enter into the family of nations encompassing the entire earth, it is only their *first* life that has come to an end, and only by aligning themselves with that aforementioned insight are they resurrected and elevated to the life of humanity. Yet it is insufficient for rational insight to simply acknowledge the Law of Reason, as we have presented it, as the supreme ideal to be realized in reality. The keystone of this inquiry, serving as the true test of our scientific discoveries, is the investigation into how much of the path towards this archetype has been traversed by peoples over the course of their historical life, i.e. the philosophy of history.
