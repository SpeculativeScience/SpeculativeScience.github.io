# Pneumatology: The Immortality of the Soul

1. The immortality of the soul is merely a negative quality thereof—the absence of mortality; yet this asserts nothing whatsoever regarding the positive nature of the soul.

2. That the soul cannot die signifies only that it cannot, like the body, be decomposed into its constituent elements—precisely because it is not corporeal.

3. Thus, in order to predicate of the soul a present, positive quality—rather than one that is merely negative and prospective—we must first define its nature. It is Spirit in its dependence upon Nature—the natural spirit—subject to Nature’s vicissitudes rather than holding dominion over them; from which it follows, as a direct consequence, that it suffers sympathetically in the death of the body.

4. Consequently, everything by virtue of which the Spirit constitutes a *soul* perishes: distinctions of race, sexual differentiation, temperament, and so forth—for all these qualities derive from corporeal determinations.

5. In general, individuality cannot preserve itself in death; for the principle of individuation is Matter—that medium in which Thought (being the Form of the Species, identical in all human beings) becomes embodied in diverse ways, owing to the heterogeneity of the material substance.

6. With individuality, consciousness also falls under this category. For consciousness is merely the form of subjectivity by means of which the individual distinguishes himself from his objects; without this distinction between subject and object, no consciousness is possible.

7. That spiritual element which remains constant amidst all the distinctions of individuality and subjectivity, I call *personality*.

8. In the legal sense, personality is the abstract concept of the free spirit, which, as a subject, possesses immediate existence within an external object; yet in such a way that the external scope of this sphere of freedom remains entirely indifferent for each individual—consequently, both the equality and the inequality of persons remain mere possibilities. The legal person is, therefore, merely a formal concept.

9. The higher content of the person lies in his ethical activity—whether as a member of a family, a member of a community, or of the State and the Nation; and finally, as a citizen of the world within the unfolding process of history.

10. Those deeds performed in the spheres of artistic practice, religion, and science must likewise be viewed from an ethical standpoint—insofar as, in each of these, the Good, the True, and the Beautiful are striven for as the ultimate end of the world, guided toward their consummation, or represented as having been attained.

11. Only that which is accomplished by the individual in the service of the Absolute Idea constitutes his true personality; everything else that he does in his life belongs to his individuality and subjectivity.

12. All deeds of ethical persons converge toward a single goal. In all of them, it is solely the absolute will of the World Spirit that becomes personified. What individuals accomplish in free self-determination—acting as ethical persons—are manifestations of that absolute will.

13. This will is the true Person, which constitutes the true essence of all individuals. I call it the eternal personality of the Spirit. 

14. Thus, in place of the immortality of the soul, I view—as the truly affirmative principle—the eternity of the spirit; a spirit which, in whatever individualities and among whatever people it may reside, manifests itself both theoretically and practically within their ethical, aesthetic, religious, and scientific spheres.

15. In this realm of the spirit, everything belonging to individuals and subjects that is in any way worthy of immortality lives on—namely, the spirit itself.

16. The Mnemosyne (memory) of history is the place where even individual immortality finds its proper abode. Through the deeds and thoughts that Plato, Aristotle, Alexander, Christ, and Raphael continue to awaken—daily and hourly—in the minds of so many people, their very bodies, too, celebrate their eternal festival of resurrection.

17. Upon another star—severed from their national, telluric foundation—individuals lose all foothold and ground, and are reduced to mere specters.

18. They are received into the bosom of the Absolute, and there they find rest from their individual struggles. Yet the eternal personality of the spirit is not itself an individual distinct from all other individuals; it is not a subject standing in opposition to an objective world; nor is it a legal entity formally relating to other atomic individuals; rather, it comes to manifestation solely *within* them.

19. The eternal personality of the spirit is that consummation of the Absolute—existing in and for itself—which belongs to the "third epoch of the world" (the ultimate end of all things); a consummation which, through the cultivation of all human spheres into the form of Reason, now operates—in full conjunction with their own consciousness—within all those individuals who have attained immortality therein; whereas, in the "first epoch," these same individuals bore this knowledge and being of the Absolute within themselves unconsciously, as mere feeling, intimation, and inspiration. 

20. In this new social life of humanity, the current of the Absolute Spirit permeates all individuals so powerfully—and all hearts beat so unanimously for the common good and the ultimate purpose of the world—that the Communion of Saints is realized: a state in which all spirits are One Spirit, which never dies and, amidst the changing of its vestures, ever remains true to itself. The future life is the life of the future.

21. The more the individual, in the course of his life, brings this eternal personality of the Spirit within himself to full expression, the more transfigured does his body already rise again in the here and now; the less of him perishes with physical death; and the more does he himself—in his wholeness and fullness of life—pass over into another mode of existence of this eternal personality.

22. In the sense indicated above, eternal life is a transmigration of souls and a palingenesis of the Spirit.

## The Doctrine of Eternal Life

In answering our first question—namely, that concerning the personality of God—the answer to the other question—that of the immortality of the soul—is, in truth, already contained. As we have already noted in our introductory observations, these are indivisible moments of one and the same whole. For if the infinite Spirit, in order to be truly real, must eternally particularize itself and appear as a distinct individual—then this very individuality is thereby elevated to the status of the Infinite and Eternal. Although, to be sure, this is only to the extent that it has transformed itself into the Universal, not beyond the realm of temporal existence, but in the very midst thereof. Let us therefore examine our task today from the perspective of this human personality—just as we investigated it in the last two lectures from the perspective of the divine personality—so that we may subsequently place ourselves at the very center of this inquiry and, by synthesizing both moments, come to recognize the eternal personality of the Spirit, to which—by way of concluding observations—the vindication of our interpretation of these two doctrines may then be readily appended. 

If our metaphysical inquiries into the relationship between time and eternity confirm our doubts raised at the very outset, concerning an immortality that begins only after death and is merely extended into infinite duration, and if conversely, they compel us to posit eternal life—with apodictic certainty—as something that realizes itself within time itself, then nevertheless, to restrict this eternal life solely to the temporal existence of the individual would constitute yet another form of one-sidedness, given that eternity—as we have seen—encompasses temporal duration as a *moment* within itself. Therefore, we must first consider the eternal life of the individual from the abstract perspective of its duration—a perspective which, being the lowest mode and mere appearance of eternity, is characterized by a lack of consciousness. Secondly—in accordance with the absolute concept of eternity—we must conceive of the eternal life of the individual as the *temporal consciousness* of an individual who acts in conformity with his own concept, and who thereby eternalizes himself. Thirdly, this true concept of eternity is once again brought into connection with the duration of time, thereby effecting the transition into the eternal personality of the spirit. Insofar as I recognize myself—in the present moment—as acting in a manner commensurate with my own concept, I simultaneously possess therein the consciousness of my imperishability within time.

Man—precisely because, as the pearl of creation, he incorporates his unity with the infinite Spirit into his consciousness—knows a life other than, and higher than, that which is confined to this sensuous existence. The animal lives between birth and death solely amidst sensuous pleasures and sensations; beyond these, it remains impervious to any impression. Thus, with death, the animal's existence vanishes without a trace; indeed, even during its lifetime, only the individual moment ever holds value for it. Man, however—as the consciousness of the universal Spirit—endows his sensuous life with a higher, spiritual significance, for within it he brings about, in his own unique way, the realization of the universality of the eternal Spirit. And because sensuous life is thus neither the essential—let alone the sole—aspect of human existence, that which is essential remains preserved even after the death of the natural body. By this "essential," we do not here refer merely to the absolutely universal Spirit—that Spirit which becomes personal within every human being and thereby enjoys an eternal life. This form of immortality—posited by Aristotle, the Stoics, and others—is merely the immortality of the universal Spirit. However—inasmuch as it is, in accordance with the Christian standpoint, the fundamental principle of the modern world to ascribe absolute significance to the individual himself (as we observed in our earlier exposition of the eternal Incarnation)—the question now arises, firstly: in what respect does the individual, as such, persist beyond the lifespan of his sensuous body? For precisely because the individual constitutes a temporal manifestation of the Universal and the Eternal, he is—to a certain degree—thereby immersed within the very stream of eternity—even if, on the other hand, it must straightway drink from the floods of Lethe in return.

The entire presence of the departed individual has left a spiritual impression upon others. It is a spirit that has acted upon other spirits, and is preserved within their memories. Even in a physical sense, the traces of that individual existence have not vanished entirely with death. The survivors visit the place where the remains rest—not because the spirit of the deceased still maintains any connection to this spatial existence, but because, at this site, the memory of the departed confronts the living with heightened vividness. Thus, if the place where our remains are to rest in death is not a matter of indifference to us—and if, as Goethe observes in *Elective Affinities*, we are so eager to leave behind traces of our individual existence in the physical world—it is because we hope thereby to anchor the memory of our individuality within the minds of others. Spatial survival, therefore, is not an end in itself, but rather a means for ensuring the spiritual presence of the deceased among the living. Now that Napoleon’s name is no longer proscribed in the political life of the French people, the desire has naturally arisen—and has ultimately found fulfillment through the current Thiers ministry—to bring the remains of this hero, with whom a glorious chapter of French history is inextricably linked, from the oblivion of his ocean-girt island home into the very heart of his capital city. What, after all, are these remains to the French nation but a catalyst for conjuring up, with ever-greater vividness, the deeds of the Empire?

I would call this remembrance of the dead by the living the first mode of the individual’s unconscious persistence; and the more manifold the tokens of remembrance of an individual that exist, the more assured will he be of this immortality. This is, to be sure, still the lowest stage—the one to which the Egyptians, the first people to embrace such a belief, confined themselves in their cult of the dead; yet it constitutes, in some sense, a persistence of individuality that has been wrested from the realm of spatial existence and thereby elevated into the eternal kingdom of the spirit. Inasmuch as art preeminently accomplishes this spiritualization and eternalization of sensuous being, we must turn to it in order to attain such immortality. While the urn or the burial site merely serves as a general reminder of the individual, an image created by art calls back to us the very features of the departed. In the gaze of his eyes—in the features of his face, which the genius of art has fixed in eternal permanence, lifting them from the fleeting cycle of transient phenomena—the entire character of the beloved departed one, now elevated into the realm of the ideal, unfolds before the eye of our mind. That gentleness, that benevolence, that spirit—they live on when, in our remembrance, we reflect upon the times when the individual’s deeds flowed forth from this character, and when we watched his work with blissful inspiration. Centuries and millennia have vanished, yet the venerable countenances of a Christ, a Socrates, or a Heraclitus still gaze upon us with looks full of meaning. Roch’s mouth is open—the source from which words of eternal life have flowed. Such figures stand there more beautiful than they were in life; no longer bound to this single point in space, they appear instead in infinitely multiplied manifestations.

Yet it is not merely this character in general that endures in the minds of the survivors; rather, the memory of his individual manifestations—that is, the deeds that sprang from him—lives on eternally within the human race. Soon, rising directly before our eyes in the magnificent center of his royal capital, will stand the equestrian statue of the great King—he who, in the previous century, while seated upon his throne of intellect, made room beside himself for that very faculty, upon which he built his entire state, and which he knew how to implement with the courage of a warrior. The embellishments of the pedestal will visually depict his deeds in both war and peace; and for the unassuming strollers of the royal residence, this monument will call to mind the entire life of Frederick—for all eternity, and ever anew. Thus does a people encircle its physical realm with the monuments of the past, wherein every significant deed—every act conducive to the public weal—remains indelibly commemorated.

Through their deeds, the individuals who accomplished them live on immortally in the consciousness of the people. And is not this life—existing in the memory of others, in honor, in a good name among posterity, and in the blessings of one’s descendants—a distinct, higher form of life than the sensual existence into which our physical birth brought us? Whether this sensual life is truly a good remains doubtful; it depends entirely upon the use we make of it—and for how many does this very use become the cause of the judgment they pass upon themselves, and the source of the weariness that drives them to flee from this life? Honor, however, is something no one would ever voluntarily cast aside. Thus, for the one who devotes the span of time allotted to him to the service of an ideal, his works shall follow after him, just as the Scriptures declare. He has erected for himself a monument in the hearts of his fellow human beings and of future generations—a monument he would not relinquish, even for the most precious of prices. Admittedly, honor and posthumous fame do not touch me; and if, with death, all comes to an end, they can be a matter of indifference to me. Thus speaks only the sensual man, who regards pleasure and wealth as the highest goods. Not so acts the nobler character—he who willingly sacrifices pleasure and wealth—nay, life itself, the very epitome of all earthly goods—not merely when he envisions the starry crown of honor descending upon his head in return, but also within the more modest spheres of human society, whenever he cannot preserve his life without henceforth walking in disgrace among his fellow citizens. We shall possess no consciousness of our honor or shame once we are dead; yet this very thought—of how we shall be judged when we are no more—constitutes that higher life of the spirit which we strive to keep unsullied. Honor, therefore—however fleeting it may seem—remains nonetheless an enduring, inalienable good; within it, even without conscious awareness, we lead an eternal life—a life which, even amidst our sensual existence, lies supremely close to our hearts and is held by us in higher esteem than that very sensual life itself. Thus, we do indeed—in accordance with the common belief—postulate a life distinct from the sensual one; yet this life does not stand in isolation from the earthly realm, of which we have, here on earth, developed only the lowest stage.

But what am I saying? It is not merely through the memory of our individuality and our deeds that we live on immortally in the consciousness of others; rather—and secondly—we endure through our deeds themselves within the realm of reality. For if every individual—as we stated a few weeks ago in the psychological section of our reflections—is essentially the series of his deeds, and if these deeds did not have merely sensuous, transient interests as their aim, then man becomes eternalized through them within the realm of the spirit. This enduring influence constitutes a truer immortality than the mere honor of a name, for within it, the entire substantiality of the spirit is preserved. This becomes most evident in the noblest deeds—those which, from the very moment of their inception, possessed their existence wholly and solely within the realm of the spirit. What, for instance, has truly perished of Plato and Aristotle? Nothing but the accidental nature of their individuality—that they lived in this or that city, stood within these or those family relations, or ate this dish today and that one yesterday—along with the consciousness of the continuity of their "Self" amidst the flux of these accidental states. Yet of their thoughts—through which alone they are truly Plato and Aristotle, and which, like thought in general, constitute their innermost essence—not a single iota has been lost to us. These thoughts, acting as the teachers of the human race, live on in every thinking mind; and their originators celebrate their resurrection within the spirit of every student whose love for knowledge is kindled through the study of their writings. Thus, the philosophers of the past are preserved within those of the present. This is the only transmigration of souls—in the realm of Spirit—that we can accept.

The very same holds true for deeds—those deeds fashioned from coarser material, which spring forth from the soil of transience upon the field of political history. While these deeds do not persist in their original form—the very shape in which the creative genius first cast them—they live on nonetheless through their effects and consequences. Thus, when Miltiades triumphed at Marathon with ten thousand men against many hundreds of thousands, the painting itself—which once hung in the *Stoa Poikile* to immortalize the memory of that feat of arms—has indeed vanished. Yet the deed itself has not merely been enshrined within the more enduring halls of history, within the temple of Mnemosyne; it is a deed whose fruits the European West continues to savor, day in and day out, to this very hour. Of this deed, too, holds true what Cornelius Nepos said of the feat of Themistocles at Salamis: "Through it, Asia succumbed to Europe." Through the agency of such men, the European spirit liberated itself—once and for all—from Asiatic despotism, in whatever guise that despotism might choose to appear; and it was through this victory of Greece that free political life, free art, and free science were able to be poured forth across the Western world. Is not all of this, in its entirety, the work of those heroes—a work in which they remain present even now (albeit without conscious awareness or distinct individuality)—precisely because they poured their entire personalities into it, and staked their very selves upon it?

I shall not dwell at greater length here upon the deeds of Christ—whose work it is—whereby a Christian, in faith in the Redemption, reconciles himself with the Divine Spirit, and thus Christ rises again within him. For this belongs just as much to the doctrine of the Personality of God—where we have already considered it—as it does to the question of the individual immortality of the man Christ. I touch upon this only because it is precisely the point where it becomes most clearly evident that both doctrines, in their new formulation, are identical; for nowhere does the interpenetration of the Divine and the Human present itself to our conception so immediately as in the manifestation and deeds of Christ. For just as we saw initially how the persistence of the person already pertained to immediate individuality—as a higher mode of personality, serving, as it were, as *objective* personality—and how, subsequently, the deeds of the individual presented themselves: so a third mode of personal persistence unites the first two, and thus asserts itself as the most perfect form of this initial, unconscious immortality—an immortality that belongs solely to the mode of manifestation of Eternity. This is the persistence of the individual within the family—an immortality to which Aristotle, too, had already drawn attention. In his children, man lives on—both as an individual and as a type. The features of the father are replicated in the son. And amidst the wistful awareness of his waning strength, the father—beholding the blossoming vigor of his son—retains the steadfast confidence that he will find rejuvenation in a second self, and continue to live on in his remotest descendants, long after the plowshare has perhaps passed over his silent grave. Everything the son achieves that is enduring and destined for eternity is, in truth, the deed of the parents who bestowed this upbringing upon him; and if it has been said that every great man owes his greatness to his mother, does not her domestic devotion then bear all these fruits for the welfare of humanity—even if not every mother, like Cornelia—renowned no less for being the mother of the Gracchi than for being the daughter of the Scipios—succeeds in immortalizing her name for posterity?

If thus far we have described the mode of appearance of eternal life merely as a duration extending beyond natural life, then immortality does not yet constitute a present life for us, but rather only the hope of a future one—nothing actual, but a mere possibility. It depends upon certain conditions whether I can preserve my individuality beyond death. These conditions, to be sure, reside within me. The more I have glowed with fervor for the True, the Good—for the Eternal in general—the more deeds I have also accomplished for eternity, and I live on in them. Thus, true eternity is, even here, the very ground of immortality—although the finite, as such, can likewise force its way into such duration; and just as the lowest formations of nature endure, so too can the worst and most mediocre of human beings secure for themselves this immortality of their name—and of their infamy! The names of Thersites, Herostratus, and Ephialtes are still uttered with contempt or loathing. And in the case of Herostratus, his heinous deed actually had posthumous fame as its very objective—just as the audacious Fieschi, in a similar vein, sought to direct his "infernal machine" against Louis Philippe solely to transmit his reputation as a regicide to posterity. If, therefore, immortality is dragged into the realm of the accidental, then—precisely by accident—it falls to the lot of the unworthy. Indeed, in the case of great and noble deeds, does not everything depend upon their becoming known? And even if they continue to exert a beneficent influence upon posterity—albeit anonymously—the specific immortality of honor we have described is nonetheless lost to their author.

>Many brave men lived before Agamemnon;

>But all are pressed down, unwept and unknown,

>By the long night—for they lack a sacred bard.

Is it not left, then, to mere chance whether a particular individual happens to stand at precisely the spot where—seized by the force of circumstances—he can accomplish a deed for the benefit of humanity? Rather, the one whom Fate favors and has chosen is the one who carries out the immortal work to which many were called. How diversely, indeed, are the gifts of Nature distributed among mankind! And shall the less gifted—he who is unable to leave behind anything memorable in history—go away empty-handed in the matter of immortality? Eternal justice would thus be perverted were this first form of immortality the only one that existed.

All these difficulties vanish if we return to the concept of eternity that we discussed earlier. Although—as we stated—eternity finds its mode of manifestation in infinite duration, it is nevertheless not exhausted therein. True eternity was, for us, rather the consciousness of the correspondence between reality and concept. Whenever I have performed an act through which I have satisfied the demands of the moral law within my inner self—wherever, in my thought and action, the Idea of ​​the species has risen to consciousness, and I view myself as a serving member, as a useful instrument for the well-being of humanity—and I know: in all these moments, I dwell in eternity; for it is not I, but the divine Idea that lives within me then. This—says Schleiermacher in his *Speeches on Religion*—is the manner in which every pious person bears within themselves an immutable and eternal existence. For if our feeling nowhere clings to the particular, but rather finds its content in our relationship to God—a relationship in which everything particular and transient is subsumed—then surely nothing transient remains within it, but only the Eternal; and one may rightly say that the religious life is that state in which we have already sacrificed and relinquished everything mortal, and truly enjoy immortality. Religion strives entirely toward the end that the sharply defined contours of our individuality should expand and gradually dissolve into the Infinite—that, as we become conscious of the Universe, we should also become, as far as possible, one with it. Should we not strive to transcend our individuality even here, and to live in the All-in-One? He who has learned to be more than merely himself knows that he loses little when he loses himself. The goal and character of a religious life is not that immortality which lies outside of time and behind time—or rather, not merely *after* this time, yet still *within* time—but rather that immortality which we can possess immediately, even in this temporal life: to become one with the Infinite amidst all finitude, and to be eternal in every single moment.

Here, no objection can trouble us any longer—not even the argument that this second form of immortality, much like the Homeric conception, is restricted to a select few "favorites of the gods" who dwell in the Elysian Fields. It is not merely great and eternal deeds—deeds I might perform for the very welfare of humanity—that alone grant me this intuition of the eternal World-Spirit. True piety teaches us—as Schleiermacher once again asserts—to constantly deny ourselves and to merge with the entire universe, insofar as we are capable of reaching it. Thus, Schiller aptly observes in his *Philosophical Letters*: All spirits strive toward a state of the highest, freest expression of their powers; all possess the common impulse to expand their sphere of activity—to draw everything toward themselves, to gather it within themselves, and to make their own whatever they recognize as good, as excellent, or as beautiful. The intuition of the Beautiful, the True, and the Excellent constitutes the instantaneous appropriation of these very qualities. Whatever state we perceive, into that state we ourselves enter. In the very moment we conceive of them, we become the possessors of a virtue, the authors of an action, the discoverers of a truth, and the inheritors of a state of bliss. "We ourselves become the object perceived." Even the humblest and least gifted person—provided he possesses not a spirit neglected by poor upbringing, but rather one receptive to the Beautiful, the Good, and the True—can, in this way, savor eternity in every single moment. And just as religion does, so too does art enable everyone to achieve this.

>For it is the striving of art

>To lift each soul beyond itself.

It opens up the intellectual realm to us; and not only does the artist himself—who gives form to his ideals—become a truly great and good human being (the very image of which he sketches out for us), but every reader, listener, or spectator finds themselves in the exact same position. The infinite allure that draws all people toward the contemplation of art is, therefore, not merely a yearning for enjoyment and pleasure. We remain entirely disinterested observers of the great deeds and misfortunes of a hero; and no advantage or disadvantage accrues to us therefrom. Yet what nonetheless draws us irresistibly toward art is this very feeling: that within it, we become fellow citizens of a higher world—that the eternal ideals of humanity rise to life within our own breasts, and that we experience their eternal life within our own spirits.

>From out of life, two paths lie open to you:

>One leads to the Ideal; the other, to Death.

>See to it that, while there is still time—and while you remain free—you set forth upon the first,

>Else the Fates, by force, drag you away along the second.

But if, through the recognition of the Eternal that surrounds us—within whose bosom we live and which permeates our very being—we already enjoy the Eternal in an imperishable way, then this holds true in the realm of action as well. For however insignificant our deeds may seem—however powerless we may be to significantly advance the well-being of humanity—they have nonetheless grown from the soil of this Eternal, and they eternally spring forth from it. The slightest thing we do intervenes in the organism of this Whole; and the smallest pin in a clock—were it to go missing—would bring the entire mechanism to a standstill. Thus, every deed—provided we view it not as an end in itself, but as a subordinate member of a higher Whole—occupies its necessary place; it is a moment in the realization of the eternal Idea, and is thereby itself made participant in eternity. And through this, we ourselves—in the very act of doing—attain the consciousness of our own eternity. In patriotism and the deeds born of it, the citizen takes not himself—his own fleeting individuality—as his object, but rather the Idea of ​​the Whole: the substance of the State, which is eternal. Equally eternal is the Idea of ​​the Family, with which the individual finds himself intertwined. The feelings of a mother, as she gazes upon the infant in her lap, constitute a source of eternal bliss for her; and in this consciousness of her own eternity—as Schleiermacher observes in *Christmas Eve*—she must recognize Christ anew. And just as every human personality is the Divine embodied in flesh, so does the mother seek out, in every movement and stirring of her child, the traces of that higher spirit which—within him, as within every pious soul—is destined to awaken to consciousness.

However, the individual in this life possesses the present consciousness of his own eternity—not merely in the performance and recognition of the deeds of world history, nor in the fulfillment of his duties as a member of a state and a family; rather, religious consciousness is, truly and primarily, the seat of this eternal life. This is the place—the context—in which the passages from the Gospel of John, which we cited in our historical reflections, are to be understood in a higher and truer sense. For when it is said: "If you believe in me, then you have eternal life," eternal blessedness is thereby presented not as a longing or a hope for a future state that is to befall us after death, but rather as a present blessedness—mediated through our own activity—as the individual’s being filled with the Divine Spirit, and as the unshakable consciousness that "my Redeemer lives within me." The highest expression of this eternal life of man is the Lord's Supper—which, as we recently observed, serves equally as the supreme symbol of the eternal Incarnation of God. And here we already perceive how the personality of God and the immortality of the soul—conceived as the Incarnation of God and the eternal life of man—converge within the mystery of the Lord's Supper. The passages from Paul cited in our historical introduction—along with other writings of this Apostle—likewise attain their true significance only within this very context. For behold—in the enjoyment of the body of Christ, and through the presence of the Divine Spirit within the temple of the believing heart—the old, natural, sinful, corruptible man dies, while the new, spiritual, reborn, incorruptible man has risen again; thus, from now on, Christ lives in my life—not I in myself. The fragmentary ceases, and the Perfect begins—where we shall no longer behold God through a mirror, but face to face. Therefore, that which I must offer up to Nature in death—in order to repay my debt to her—I have already offered up while yet alive; "I die daily," such that the strangler, Death, finds nothing left in me to consume, and I, while living, am filled solely with the consciousness of the Eternal. Then it is time to exclaim: "O Death, where is your sting? O Hell, where is your victory?" "Now the sting of death is sin." And: "Whether we live, we live unto the Lord; or whether we die, we die unto the Lord; so that, whether we live or die, we are always the Lord's."

Due to the difficulty for ordinary consciousness to grasp the concept of eternity as absolute presence—and to expunge from it the notion of mere duration—religious representation inevitably introduces the idea of ​​a perpetual duration of this very consciousness of the Eternal. In truth, however, the eternity of a state does not depend on the duration of time; for if infinite time were a prerequisite, such eternity could never be truly real. Religious faith attempts to resolve this dilemma by asserting that eternal blessedness consists simultaneously of present enjoyment *and* infinite duration—as if the continuity of temporal succession (which, after all, we cannot trace prior to birth) could be maintained after death; and as if infinite duration in general—which, strictly speaking, can only be reckoned from the moment of birth—could amount to anything more than simply a very long stretch of time. For anyone born today cannot possibly live for an infinite span of time, since they must inevitably lack that entire segment of time which elapsed prior to their birth. Yet would not annihilation be all the more dreadful the longer we had grown accustomed to the enjoyment of that life? If, conversely, we annihilate that which is perishable *right now*, then annihilation can no longer harm us in the slightest. We need no longer seek the temporal manifestation of eternity merely as a dim trail trailing behind the true "star and core" of real eternity; rather, we must grasp both as a single, integral, present, and real life. That "other life"—the life of the senses—has revealed itself to be the true and sole substance of the latter. Yet for those who cannot liberate themselves from these conceptual distinctions of the intellect, we must say, along with Schiller:

>Their faith was the happiness allotted to them;

>They could consult their sages. 

>What one has spurned in a single minute, 

>no eternity can ever bring back.

And thus, contrary to their own will, the belief in a blissful future becomes the present reality of an eternal life; for in this faith, they enjoy—with unwavering confidence—the absolute presence of a Higher Power within themselves.

But if we have designated the modes of manifestation of eternal life as the unconscious persistence of individuality, and its true substance as the present consciousness of the Divine within us—and if, in accordance with our principles, we are forbidden to separate essence from appearance (since we were compelled to reject precisely this separation—and the merely external linkage of the separated elements—as a one-sided reflection of the intellect)—then we must now, in a third form of immortality, fuse consciousness and unconsciousness, infinite persistence and absolute presence, into a single whole, and thereby bring our doctrine of eternal life to its conclusion.

For if the deed that corresponds to the concept of the Good is an eternal one—and if the consciousness of having accomplished it becomes the very enjoyment of the blessed life itself—then this essence of eternity must not lack its corresponding mode of manifestation. If a human being becomes eternal by living within the knowledge or the accomplishment of the Eternal, then, in that very instant, he encompasses the entire future as well! For the Eternal—which now reveals itself within the world of phenomena—cannot perish in times to come; and the doer is so intimately interwoven with this Eternal that he may anchor his own existence to it. I can illustrate this consciousness of an infinitely enduring—albeit in a single moment concentrated—immortality to you in no better way than through the words of Schiller—words that may well be applied to Christ: 

>"Imagine a truth, my Raphael, that brings blessings to the entire human race across distant centuries. Add to this: that this truth condemns its proponent to death; that this truth can be proven—can be believed—only if he dies. Then imagine the man possessed of the bright, all-encompassing, sun-like gaze of genius; of the fiery wheel of inspiration; of the entire sublime capacity for love. Let the perfect ideal of that great impact rise up within his soul; let all those whom it is destined to bless pass before him in a dim premonition; let the present and the future converge simultaneously within his spirit. And now, answer this question for yourself: Does such a man require the promise of another life?" 

He lives—even now—in this so-called "other life," in this realm of the intelligible, which endures in imperishable constancy and grants an immutable existence to those who bear it within themselves and recognize it as the very substance of their spirit.

"The sum of all these sensations," Schiller continues, "will merge with his personality; it will fuse into one with his *I*. The human race, which he now conceives in his mind, is himself. It is a body in which his own life—forgotten and expendable as a drop of blood—floats. How swiftly would he shed it for the sake of that body's health!" In a single moment, he anticipates the enduring felicity of all mankind and partakes in its enjoyment. All true immortality—or eternity—of the individual thus rests upon expanding his own self into the universal Self of humanity. And since it is impossible—contrary to what the conceptions of ordinary consciousness have hitherto attempted—to cram the infinite fullness of divinity into one's own tiny *I* in order to attain immortality, it is far more natural and rational to conceive of oneself as a mere reflection of the Whole, and thereby to lead an eternal life within it.

>You recoil in fear before death! You wish to live on immortally?

>Live within the Whole! For long after you have passed away, *it* remains.

The essence of moral action remains the same in all ages. What changes are solely the external circumstances, the motivations, and the names. These vanish, just as do the individuals who performed the deeds. The deeds themselves, however, are handed down through the ages as eternal examples worthy of emulation; they are reiterated in the actions of future generations that accord with a moral ideal, and the awareness of their imperishability bestows upon their authors themselves the sense of enduring permanence. Leonidas lives on in every deed by which a citizen courageously faces death for the salvation of his fatherland; and it was in this blessed consciousness of his own eternity that he died.

>He who has served the noblest of his time

>Has lived for all eternity.

And it is not only the great deeds of world history and the realm of thought that bestow this consciousness of infinite duration; rather, even within the more limited sphere of domestic life, the fulfillment of its duties grants us this blessedness. As a mother traces the footsteps of the Divine within her child, she recognizes this very maternal love as the imperishable heaven that unfolds within her own bosom. This sentiment transcends individuality—which vanishes within it, willingly sacrificing itself for the child's well-being. This sentiment is itself the Eternal; it constitutes the very substance of the individual and thus—reverberating through a thousand mothers—emerges as the unbroken continuity of this eternal blessedness, in which the mother who harbors these feelings participates and knows herself to be preserved, precisely because she is wholly absorbed into this sentiment.

Eternal life—the description of which was the task of today's lecture—is, therefore, indeed unattainable without sacrifice. We must cast aside all the trappings of the earthly realm—all self-serving interests—if we aspire to mature into citizens of the spiritual world. Thus, with the dissolution of individuality, the eternal personality—which is born into earthly existence within every human being—sheds its final vestment; and it repeats this process until everything that the eternal Idea encompasses has fully unfolded within the actual manifestation of the human race. In this race, God has become just as absolutely personal—or human—as the individual is assured of an immortal, eternal life. This absolute unity. We must now, in our subsequent considerations, subject both of these doctrines—specifically the concept of the eternal personality of the spirit—to a more detailed examination.

## The Eternal Personality of the Spirit

If, on the one hand, it is true that God attains to personality only in man—and if, on the other hand, man enjoys eternal life only insofar as he immerses himself in the divine personality—then we seem to have arrived at the sheer contradiction of a double negation, from which no path could be found leading to the truly positive and affirmative. In seeking to realize God, we lose Him either in the totality of the human race, or in one or several individual human beings, or even in the spirit of humanity itself; and this constitutes the first negation. If we then ask what that is in which we worship God, it proves to be precisely that very being which—in order to know itself as substantial—must, for its part, lose itself in God; and this constitutes the second negation.

We can most easily escape these labyrinthine paths of dialectic through the following reflection. Insofar as God, through the Incarnation, steps forth from Himself only to lose Himself in a being which—in order to apprehend God as its innermost substance—must lose itself, God thereby loses Himself in that very act of "losing oneself in God"; that is to say, He finds Himself once again. It is precisely in this that the realization of God consists: that in this act of self-loss—in that wherein He is destined to appear—He negates that which is merely the negative aspect of Himself; such that, as this negative element is sublated through the manifestation of God within it—and is thereby revealed as the non-essential—God, conversely, asserts Himself therein as the sole affirmative and essential reality.

Here we see most clearly that God is a God of the living, not of the dead; that He does not sit enthroned in blissful repose—like the Epicurean gods—beyond the spheres (which would be a veritable slumber of the spirit); but rather that He is process, activity—specifically, through the negation of the negative. And in this way alone is it possible to recognize the eternal personality of the Spirit. We may assert that, for this eternal life of God, the human race is necessary. Would He otherwise have brought it forth, had it not been necessary? And since God is the sole necessary Being, would the human race be necessary if it were not necessary *to Him*—if it did not belong to the very circle of divine revelations, indeed, if it did not crown and complete that circle, constituting the perfect emanation of the divine essence? In this sense, Angelus Silesius says:

>God matters as much to me as I matter to Him;

>I help Him sustain His Being, just as He sustains mine.

Or, in another distich:

>That God is so blissful, and lives without desire,

>He has received as much from me as I have from Him.

While Novalis views all of creation as a continuous revelation of God—in which God eternally becomes stone, plant, and animal—Christian dogma, by contrast, rightly restricts this revelation of God to His Incarnation; whereas among the pagans, to be sure, the sun, moon, mountains, rivers, trees, and winds were indeed personifications of God. Yet these serve to conceal the Deity rather than to reveal it. The preeminence of the human race—as the crown of creation—lies, conversely, in the fact that within it God attains to self-consciousness and recognizes Himself as Spirit in man. This does not imply that God has become an angel—precisely because the concept of the angel constitutes merely a particular idea (that is, a specific moment) of the absolute Idea of ​​God Himself—an Idea within whose ideality the creative forces of the universe are encompassed, and which appears as autonomous, light-bearing figures surrounding the throne of God only to the faculty of imagination, not in actual reality.

The human race—and the tiny speck in the universe that it inhabits—are, therefore, not so contemptible and insignificant in comparison to the rest of creation. The Earth is, to be sure—as Klopstock says—merely a drop in the ocean of the Infinite; yet the infinity of space is by no means the true measure of God's greatness. To form a worthy conception of the personality of God, one might imagine every star—and in the Milky Way, they are strung together like grains of sand by the sea—as a sun like our own, around which revolves a planetary system—like ours, or perhaps an even more beautiful one. God's eye, then, surveys these countless worlds and guides them by His will—not merely predetermining their orbital motions from the very beginning through eternal laws, but rather directing the spiritual development of the beings upon them, and gradually initiating them into the mysteries of creation. Thus, just as the human race constitutes a revelation of the divine Spirit, so too may higher, more perfect, or equivalent revelations manifest themselves upon our sister planets and upon the stars of other solar systems; and God's self-consciousness encompasses them all within His own personality. In this way, the entire heavens are populated—partly by the souls of departed humans, who there enjoy a higher state of perfection than they did on Earth and ascend ever higher from one stage to the next; and partly by beings of an originally higher nature—those very beings who have traditionally been designated as angels.

However much brilliance and allure Fontenelle’s speculation regarding the plurality of worlds may seem to possess, it is nonetheless—on the one hand—unphysical, and—on the other—just as irreligious as it is unphilosophical. Moreover, it would completely destroy the concept of the eternal personality of the spirit—the very concept we are currently seeking. For the divine self-consciousness would not be perfectly mirrored in its object, but would instead exist across the many spheres only as something absolutely fragmented. We must, therefore, refute this view today from these three perspectives, so that—following this initial, negative examination—we may subsequently undertake a positive discussion of the question at hand.

The reasons Fontenelle adduces for the habitability of the planets are exceedingly weak and scarcely merit serious refutation. The planets, he argues, are of the same nature as the Earth. Why, then, should the Earth alone constitute an exception and be inhabited? Fontenelle thus reasons by analogy. The question arises, however, whether the attribute of being inhabited belongs to the Earth simply because it is a planet, or because it possesses certain specific characteristics that distinguish it from all other planets. Similarly—and with equal uncertainty—it is inferred from the analogy existing between the Sun and all the fixed stars—namely, that they are self-luminous bodies—that the stars, like the Sun, are each the center of a planetary system. Yet, the shared quality of self-luminosity by no means implies a shared possession of all other properties. For that centrality belongs to the Sun not by virtue of its general classification as a fixed star, but precisely because it is *this particular* star. The analogical inference is therefore flawed, as it takes into account only the nature of the general category while disregarding the particularities of the individual entities.

An equally unconvincing argument of Fontenelle’s is the impossibility of conceiving of any other purpose for which the planets might have been created—furthermore, the fruitfulness and splendor of Nature, which supposedly produces nothing in vain, and so forth. Yet how many islands are scattered across the ocean that are merely bare, uninhabited bodies? What purpose do they serve? we might ask. Are the stars not likewise bare bodies of light, scattered across the celestial ocean? When we ask what purpose something serves, we merely relate it to something else in order to demonstrate its utility for that other thing, rather than viewing it in and of itself. However, every being in Nature—at the particular level it occupies—is an end in itself; and we must reject such an external teleology as a subordinate mode of contemplating Nature, for it fails to engage with the innermost concept of things. Yet this very concept of the starry heavens is—as we observed earlier—precisely this: the moment of abstract, motionless duration, existing as the mere, lifeless mode of the manifestation of eternity; and thus, the fixed stars possess their own distinct character—one that we must not allow ourselves to obscure through analogical extrapolations drawn from other domains.

If we now examine more closely the physical arguments against the plurality of worlds, the following points emerge as the principal objections. First, only planets could be habitable—not the Moon as well, as Fontenelle would have it. For it is generally conceded that the Sun, by virtue of its luminous nature, cannot be inhabited. Yet while modern views assert that the Sun is the generative source-point of the planetary system—and thus, in terms of inherent dignity, superior to the Earth—the ancients, conversely, regarded the Sun and the stars as products of the Earth. The notion that one side sprang from the other in a temporal sequence is false, regardless of which side one chooses as a starting point. For since the Idea of ​​Nature has eternally emanated from God—and since the existence of *all* its constituent moments is essential to its complete realization—these natural formations are, in fact, simultaneous; indeed, the bodies of the periphery would possess no significance without their center, nor the center without its periphery. In this respect, however, the ancients undoubtedly stand closer to the truth; for the Earth does, in reality, hold priority in dignity over the Sun, and constitutes—if not the *sensible* center—then certainly the *spiritual* center of the system. For while the Sun represents the abstract principle of light; while comets are merely tenuous, easily dissipated masses of vapor; and while moons are rigid, burnt-out volcanic masses possessing scant atmosphere and devoid of meteorological processes—the planets alone embody the concrete synthesis of physical elements: the intermingling of solid and fluid matter which—warmed by the Sun—is alone capable of sustaining higher organic forms of life. It follows, therefore, from the very nature of the planets themselves, that they alone could be habitable. Among these, however, those closest to the Sun approximate the burnt-out state of the Moon, whereas the more distant ones are either still entirely covered with water—as Schubert has demonstrated in the case of Jupiter—or possess atmospheric processes too overwhelming for the ordered course of terrestrial life to be established there amidst such a wild, cometary state.

Furthermore, the Earth is the most beautiful focal point not only of the planetary system but also of the entire starry firmament. For if the other planets possess no inhabitants, the fixed stars do so even less, seeing as they are abstract bodies of light, much like the Sun. And just as light appears within the Sun as a mere moment of a larger system, this very moment asserts its independence within the host of stars. As the moment of abstract duration—devoid of life or internal process—they constitute the infinite expansion of light throughout space. The Sun, as a moment within a whole of greater corporeality, must—without thereby needing to relinquish its inherent luminosity—itself partake in this corporeality; for it is subject to the force of gravity. The fixed stars, conversely, appear to be of a more ethereal constitution. Were they, too, to possess planets gravitating around them, one cannot conceive why the telescope should not reveal a single one to us—for the infinite distance of the fixed stars is, after all, acknowledged even by astronomers themselves as a mere hypothesis. Yet, just as clearly as we observe—among the vast number of double stars—one revolving around the other, or both orbiting their common, invisible center, so too would we discern other wandering stars revolving around them. For what is a double star, if not the imperfect inception of a planetary system? How, then, could we fail to perceive a fully developed system far more readily than one still in the process of formation? These double stars thus demonstrate to us that, while the stars indeed aspire toward a planetary nature (and their motion, moreover, conforms to Kepler’s laws), they have not yet attained that state which Nature has reserved exclusively for our own planetary system. The double stars are, therefore, the intermediate link between the solar system and the stellar heavens—a realm that, within them, has advanced only halfway toward its full realization. Thus, it remains evident that the most perfect element in the nature of the stars is not to be sought outside our own planet, and that beyond it, no trace of a spirit is to be found.

Nor does the claim—that a general motion of the Sun, and consequently of the entire planetary system, within space has been observed—invalidate our assertion. For whether the whole system of stars revolves around a central sun—or, what seems to me the more probable scenario, this infinite host of stars, together with our own solar system, rotates around an ideal center (that is, around the axis of the cosmos)—the spatial center of the universe need not, for all that, be the spiritual center of the cosmos; just as the true center of the planetary system, in our own case, resides in a single body situated at the periphery. Thus, God’s personality does not dwell in the physical center of the world, but rather reveals itself most fully precisely where the spiritual blossom of creation unfolds.

That this is indeed the Earth is further confirmed by our religious traditions. For if God so loved the world that He sent His only-begotten Son to redeem it, then the Earth has been, in the most preeminent degree, the object of this infinite love. The Earth—precisely because it is the dwelling place of the Spirit—manifests within itself this very process of a soul struggling free from nature and soaring upward into the infinity of the Spirit; and it does so precisely because the infinite Spirit Himself deigned to descend to it and become one with it. Were other planets also inhabited by spirits—since the nature of the Spirit is one—this same process would necessarily have had to repeat itself there. Now, since God does not possess multiple Sons to impart to other spirits, Christ would have had to come to them as well, and to live through the same history an infinite number of times—that is, to allow Himself to be crucified anew, time and again, in every solar system of the Milky Way, in every one of the infinitely many nebulae (which some regard as so many distinct Milky Ways, and others as nascent planetary systems), and indeed on every single planet orbiting every single star, in order to reconcile those spirits, too, with the Father. The imagination falters far more before this infinite succession in time than it does before infinite extension in space. For here, in the latter instance, "bad infinity" is in its proper place—though certainly not within the realm of the Spirit. Furthermore, God would no longer have revealed Himself to the human race in an undivided manner had He also appeared in corporeal form everywhere else. The infinite multiplicity of the Indian incarnations of God would, furthermore, necessarily displace the Christian faith—and the latter would have to surpass the fantastical nature of those religious conceptions a thousandfold. Moreover, belief in the unity of the divine and human natures would cease to hold true if God were also one with other spirits unknown to us—perhaps even more perfect ones—meaning that we would be merely a part of the divine revelation, incapable of recognizing the whole from within ourselves alone. Yet were everything merely a perpetual repetition of the Incarnation on Earth, such a process would be entirely futile and contrary to the very nature of the Spirit, which does not remain mired in the ceaseless monotony of Nature.

This reference to the nature of the spirit leads us to the philosophical impossibility of such a conception; for the nature of the spirit can only be determined philosophically. Yet, before proceeding, I beg leave to offer the following remark regarding the religious arguments. Should anyone seek to accuse the conviction we have put forward—and which is supported by all the arguments presented thus far—of being irreligious—on the grounds that it encroaches too closely upon the greatness of the Creator, diminishes the scope of living creation, and confines the spirit within such a small space—then we must return the accusation. The plurality of worlds rests solely upon an abstract intellectual premise—one that regards infinite progress as the ultimate good—and which not only devalues ​​the worth of Christian Revelation by implying that it does not yet contain the highest stage of the spirit's perfection, but actually annihilates it entirely; for its fundamental dogma—the unity of the divine and human natures—would be corrupted by that very intellectual category which serves as the linchpin of shallow rationalistic views. Moreover, it is this very notion that encroaches most closely upon the dignity of God—the notion that He should manifest Himself as something fragmented across infinitely many worlds, and thus, within the very image and likeness of Himself, be unable to attain to a unified self-consciousness. However, as regards the smallness of the space to which the spirit might be confined: firstly, the spirit is in itself that which constricts space and renders it ideal; and therefore, furthermore, the quantity of space is absolutely indifferent to the manifestations of the spirit—which often delights in enclosing the greatest wonders within the smallest compass.

As for the arguments finally derived from the maturity of the spirit, the absolute ideality of all distinctions, which we previously termed the maturity of the spirit, meant to us more precisely that it begins with the separation of these distinctions in nature, in order to coalesce, through their negation, into the infinite unity of all its determinations. The spirit is thus only a process and progression through several stages of development, each arising from the next: so that each subsequent stage attains consciousness of the higher one, until the absolute consciousness of the unity of finite and infinite spirit is reached. That the development of the human spirit on earth is only one stage compared to other stages that have remained unknown to us is impossible. For the nature of the spirit is precisely to possess consciousness of its earlier stages of development. Since humankind does not possess this consciousness, no stage has preceded it; thus, the development of the mind begins anew from the cradle through reason. However, humankind cannot merely be a first stage of development, upon which higher stages of spiritual development would then follow, which, however, would have remained unknown to us, since we were only aware of the self and the nature of itself. For humankind achieves the highest stage of being and of cognition when the individual, received into the spirit of the community, knows himself to be absolutely one with the divine essence and participates fully in the highest blessedness.

Since, therefore, the human race is in itself the complete development of Spirit—from its unity with Nature to absolute freedom—there remains only the possibility that this same development of Spirit might repeat itself either in time or in space. In both cases, however, we fall into an infinite regress, which is in reality nothing but the false semblance of the Spirit's eternity. For if we assume, like Origen, an infinite succession of worlds—each new one representing a distribution of creatures according to their merit—then the question arises as to which is the first; for in that world—since no distribution based on the merit of a *previous* one could yet exist—one would be compelled to assume a merely arbitrary distribution by God. Thus, the infinite regress is already implicit in Origen's assertion: we can never arrive at a *first* world, because doing so would lead to the contradiction of a destiny devoid of merit; and, by the same token, we cannot arrive at a *last* world, because in such a world we would be left with unrequited transgressions and merits that would no longer be punished or rewarded. Each of Origen's worlds, therefore, presupposes the merit of the spirits in a *previous* world, and this merit, in turn, presupposes the existence of a *subsequent* world. In this succession, therefore, only the formal nature of Spirit is observed—namely, its unfolding in time. The *content*, however, is not to be found therein; for the issue here is not the Idea and the progress of the Whole, but solely the moral merit of the individual—an individual who would remain forever trapped within this ceaseless cycle of his own infinity.

In both views, however, the essence of Spirit is violated—namely, that it consists in the absolute continuity and mutual interrelation of its various moments of development. In the view of Origen, every world constitutes a new beginning; Spirit pours into the sieve of the Danaids. In the view positing the simultaneous existence of infinite worlds, the very same process begins everywhere at once; yet these developments—which are absolutely identical to one another (for the nature of Spirit is everywhere the same)—nevertheless fall strictly outside one another. Thus, according to Origen, one world does indeed spring from another, but only by virtue of the constitution of its individual parts, and not in accordance with the specific destiny that would properly belong to it as a whole. According to the other view, all coherence has vanished, and the worlds are strung together atomistically, side by side, like the trees of a forest or the stars in the sky. In a word, Spirit would be relegated to the standpoint of Nature—whose forms exist outside one another without any mutual awareness, and are bound together only unconsciously, in their inner depths, by the bond of necessity. It is precisely for this reason that Nature is the realm of the impersonal: because, out of the boundless multiplicity of its forms, it is unable to gather itself into a conscious unity. In the case of Spirit, however—precisely because it is the absolutely personal—its distinct stages do not lie unconsciously outside one another; rather, Spirit knows itself as that which remains identical with itself throughout its various stages of development. It thus remains preserved as a self-consciousness that recognizes itself in all these stages—a self-consciousness that attains its consummation in the highest stage, by therein achieving absolute fusion with its object.

Were one, however, to assert that God is a central consciousness—resting at the absolute center of the universe—which, as it were, acts as the absolute center of gravity of the world, moving and guiding everything around it; then those infinitely numerous spiritual worlds—standing on an equal plane, whether arrayed side by side in space or in succession through time, and themselves knowing nothing of one another—would be no better than the many points of light that revolve unconsciously around the axis of the world. For just as the stars know nothing of the center around which they circle soullessly, so too would each of these spiritual realms remain distant from the absolute consciousness of God—which, in this scenario, would possess for them no greater value than that of a blind natural necessity. Thus, just as the inhabitants of our star would feel little honor in knowing themselves placed outside of this divine centrality—relegated merely to an isolated point on the periphery—so too would they pay little honor to a God who, by the same token, would dwell outside the living consciousness of all those other hypothetical spirits who, likewise, appear banished to such an isolated point on the periphery. Therefore, just as surely as God is the eternal personality of the Spirit, so surely is there but one single development of the Spirit in time—a process in which it arrives at the full revelation of its own essence. And in the stages of this development—which emerge successively—they are related to one another, in that the higher stage constitutes the consciousness of the earlier one, and the final stage is the comprehensive self-consciousness of all the preceding ones; in it, the Spirit elevates itself to the perfected self-unfolding wherein the imperfect transformations that preceded the stage of completion are sublated—whereby time is thereby annulled—and out of this semblance of temporal development, the primordial, timeless Being of the Spirit—existing in and for itself—is fully brought forth into manifestation.

The development of the Spirit, as we know it, is therefore the only one, because God has fully imparted Himself to it; and the eternal personality of the Spirit is that self-consciousness of God which comes to awareness within the human spirit—a self-consciousness which, as the universal heart beating throughout the entire human race—as the universal Man—constitutes, regardless of the stages of development through which it passes, nevertheless but a single Individual, who knows himself to be identical with himself across all these stages. This universal Man is the hero of the eternal epic which, as Schelling says, the Divine Intellect composes, yet simultaneously places right into the midst of the prose of reality. This development falls within the realm of Time, for Time is, in general, the medium of the manifestation of Eternity. Yet the Spirit itself is not thereby dragged down into Time; for it is precisely the totality of those very moments which distribute themselves across Time—moments which, having thus emerged successively in Time and side-by-side in Space, subsequently—within the most perfected consciousness of the final form—sublate this succession and juxtaposition, and recognize themselves as the fully realized form of the Spirit—a form corresponding to its Concept, and therefore eternal. Insofar as this knowledge encompasses true Being within itself, Schelling is right to assert that, at that moment, God *will be*.

To discern this form, we must now examine the *becoming* of the eternal personality of the Spirit—within temporal consciousness and for the sake of temporal consciousness—through the process of world history. This history, however—acting simultaneously as the World Judgment—constitutes the process of purification for the finite realm, and has as its ultimate goal the absolutely realized image of the eternal personality of the Spirit, brought thereby into full consciousness. We must now set forth this movement and this result of world history in order to grasp the eternal personality of the Spirit—secondly—from its positive aspect as well. Specifically, we shall proceed by confining ourselves to this *becoming* of the eternal personality of the Spirit within history, reserving the consideration of its *completed* realization for a third inquiry—namely, the question concerning the End of All Things. This positive aspect of our inquiry—viewed from these two perspectives—shall constitute the subject of our next discussion. (Since these words were written down and delivered, the first volume of Strauss’s *Christian Doctrine of Faith* has appeared)

Thus, what has actually changed within world history is merely the *manner* in which individual consciousness has apprehended its relationship to—and its unity with—the Absolute Spirit; it is not the substance of this unity itself. This holds true, even though—as a matter of course—the entire condition of the nations and their political life were necessarily modified in tandem with this evolving consciousness. Yet the various modes of this relationship between the individual and the Substance are themselves, in turn, the very *moments* of the Absolute Spirit—moments which, within the consciousness of individuals, unfold and differentiate themselves under the guise of temporal development, even though they have existed from all eternity in inseparable wholeness.

The moments of this self-knowledge are simply those in which—firstly, by virtue of the immediate unity of the finite spirit with nature—this spirit knows itself to be immediately vanished and dissolved into the infinite Spirit as the universal Soul of nature. This is the standpoint of the Eastern peoples, among whom the absolute personality of God is so far from failing to attain self-consciousness that, on the contrary, nothing *but* this entirely universal Being possesses personality. Human individuality is there of no significance whatsoever; and through prayers, acts of self-mortification, the recitation of mystical formulas, and abstract contemplation, every individual can immediately elevate himself to the state of being God, and have nothing but this entirely universal Being as the content of his consciousness. Here, the eternal personality of the Spirit beholds itself immediately—and still entirely undimmed—in every individual as that which is identical with itself. Eastern religion is this God-intoxicated vision, wherein the soul perceives the Godhead gazing forth, as it were, from every eye of natural and spiritual creation; and—because nothing is personal save this universal Being—it shatters every natural form as something indifferent, utterly incapable of sullying the pure crystal of the eternal personality of the One Spirit.

Secondly, in Greece, the individual attains consciousness of his freedom and no longer vanishes entirely into the general divine substance; yet, neither does he yet stand independently in opposition to it, but rather exists in beautiful unity with it. Everything he thinks and does is a human creation—something brought forth through the spirit's own free activity—yet it is equally the stirring of a divine element that comes into existence within him. The unity of divine and human nature—which in the East was merely an immediate fact—has now, as an immediate fact, risen to the level of consciousness. In the East, it was solely the divine substance that knew itself to be one with man—insofar as man, only through the rigorous renunciation of his own selfhood, gave expression to this consciousness of God, which was in itself entirely devoid of individuality. In Greece, by contrast, the individual himself—without surrendering his own particularity—has attained consciousness of this unity; for he perceives the deity as having descended into this very particularity—which he preserves—though only insofar as the subject itself is "naturally born" as such (i.e., possesses a particularity determined by nature), to which the divine immediately imparts itself. Thus, whereas in the East all men are slaves—because they can elevate themselves to God only through the annihilation of their sense of self—in Greece, a privileged few—favored above those "barbarians"—live in the immediate enjoyment of their freedom and godlikeness: namely, the Greek citizens; while those others—the less fortunate—remain in a state of slavery, deprived of this self-consciousness. Consequently, the Eastern standpoint is also preserved within the institution of Greek slavery. And in Greece, the only addition that subsequently emerged was that some—namely, the masters of those who are compelled to live out their existence solely for the sake of others—also perceive themselves as being immediately one with the Divine Essence.

Thus, the eternal personality of the Spirit has manifested itself in these first two forms in a manner that is quite similar. For since individuality—and its indeterminate multiplicity—is a matter of absolute indifference to divine self-consciousness (inasmuch as not every individual, as such, is necessarily destined to attain consciousness of the divine substance), God has, broadly speaking, merely manifested Himself as personality; it matters not whether all individual points dissolve into the universal substance, or whether some remain therein, illuminated by the sunbeams of the divine light. In either case, individuality itself is not yet present in its fully realized form. The substance—or the universal—is already present as personality even within the vanishing individuality, and thus acts as its eternal bearer. However, for individuality to become, in its turn, the bearer of the universal Spirit, it must cease to be merely a fleeting nothingness or a transient phase of the universal. It must, therefore, no longer linger in that passive proximity to the East, nor in that cheerful interplay and enjoyment of existence characteristic of the Greek world; rather, it must—thirdly—undertake the arduous labor of conforming itself to the universal substance through its own active exertion. This transition occurs in the Roman Republic through the act of the individual sacrificing himself for the welfare of the State, by negating his own natural inclinations. Roman virtue consists in freely choosing death, so that Rome may conquer the world. In this self-surrendering individuality, the individual recognizes himself as a member of the eternal *Nomos*—and thus as a mere moment within the self-movement of God—since the World-Dominion is itself precisely that Divine essence which the Romans revered as their spiritual substance. For the Capitoline Jupiter is, in the final analysis, nothing other than the power of the sacred *Valentia*—that mystical force of the public Roman Fortune (*fortuna populi Romani*) which steps into the place of every other deity, summoning them all unto itself. The spirit of the Roman World is this substance of all the gods—an individuality which, though fragmented and gathered within a Pantheon, comes to full consciousness within every noble Roman.

Once Rome had, through this energy of individualities, conquered the world, these individuals revealed themselves—now that the State no longer required their service—at the end of this epoch as, in fact, the absolute bearers of the State. Thereby, however, they themselves became absolute entities, of which the World Spirit is conscious; and what previously—in the Orient and among the Greeks—was merely the *doing* of the Substance (the act of recognizing oneself as the absolute personality within the individual) is now the *doing* of the individuality itself. Every individual—and gradually not only all Romans, but since Caracalla also all foreigners, and finally even the slaves to whom Justinian granted freedom—recognizes itself within the Empire as a subject of rights: that is to say, everyone possesses the consciousness of absolute personality and legal capacity. Everyone knows himself, as this specific individual, to be infinitely free, possessing the absolute right to dispose of his person and his property at his own discretion—a principle that was already established in the Twelve Tables: *uti pater familias super re sua legassit, ita jus esto* ("As the paterfamilias has ordained regarding his property, so shall the law be"). Notwithstanding the contingent nature of the particular sphere of property that each individual possesses, he is, as a person, equal to every other; and it is in this equality before the law that the consciousness of divine personality is kindled—a consciousness present with equal power in every single individual, since before God, too, there is absolutely no respect of persons. The ethical substance of the State has, indeed, vanished: every person is an abstract, infinite atom that recognizes itself, within itself, as such. But through this, the absolute personality—the absolute Personhood of God—has become truly real. It does not emerge merely through the dissolution of individuality, as in the East; nor is it, as in Greece, bound solely to a few accidental individuals. Rather, wherever a human countenance reveals itself—everywhere—there is something absolutely worthy of respect to be found: the reflection of the divine essence. In all individuals, it is only pure personality that counts—not specific content; and the eternal personality of the Spirit has here come into existence most vividly, in that it reveals itself entirely unveiled in every individual, and is thereby poured out into all of creation.

The abstract person—without its specific fulfillment—is, conversely, at the same time the object of the State. For we see it here surrendered to destruction through the arbitrary will of the Emperor—an individual who, for himself, stands as the representative of universal personality and as the ruler of the globe: 

*Divisum Imperium cum Jove Caesar habet.*

Yet, firstly, this infinity of the person is inverted once again, such that the Emperor—raised to the throne by the arbitrary will of his legions—is deprived of his life through the intrigues of his own court. Secondly, however—after he has become, in death and as a corpse, a rightless thing—it is only then that the divine substance within him is recognized and divine honors are paid to him. Conversely, the person—who was rightless in relation to the Emperor during his lifetime—is so honored in death that the Emperor grants him the choice of the manner of his death, and—as *pretium festinandi* (the price of haste), as Tacitus puts it—respects the person's final will; this is particularly the case, however, when the Emperor has been appointed as a co-heir. Thus, after the individual had surrendered himself into the Substance—and had thereby re-emerged from it as an entity possessed of absolute rights—the cycle is concluded with that very annihilation of the subject which, standing outside the Substance, conceives of itself as infinite. Just as the individual, in the previous instance, posited both himself and the Substance as the Absolute, so here we witness the counterpart to that which we observed in the discerned in the first two standpoints, where it was Substance that—either through the negation of the individual or in a positive manner—recognized itself within that individual as the sole reality.

All moments regarding the Idea are found to follow one another in their pure sequence; and history has had no other task than to grasp the absolute principle of the eternal personality of Spirit, as it knows itself in the totality of its moments. This grasping initially took place within religious representation. and must ultimately take shape as philosophical knowledge, from which will then result the realization of this very principle throughout the entire existence of the human race as the outcome of world history. For Devotion—to which the human race became ripe only within the Roman world—is nothing other than the gathering together of the moments of the Absolute that had been dispersed throughout the entirety of previous world history—moments which, even then, already constituted the entire existence of the eternal personality of Spirit. For Substance, as such, must at some point come to know itself as inseparably personal within the natural individuality of the individual. The true pinnacle of this knowledge is Christ, whose appearance Schelling therefore terms the fulfillment of Greek consciousness—inasmuch as all that was previously dispersed now gathers itself together within this one natural, personal individual.

This descent of God to man, however, constitutes but one aspect of Christian consciousness; the other—namely, that the individual, just as in the Roman world, grasps himself absolutely within himself as an infinite being (that is, as finite and fallen)—likewise sublates this very infinitude existing outside of God, and sacrifices his entire individuality to the divine substance; whereby he completely regains his substantiality in God, and has thereby elevated himself to become the totality of the moments of the eternal personality of Spirit.

Because the communal history of the Church—a history that constantly repeats itself within its congregation—has given way to the history of the individual’s spiritual conversion, individual human consciousness has now become the vessel for a living, divine personality. As Hegel says: a new epoch has dawned in the world. It appears that the World Spirit has now finally succeeded in shedding every alien, objective form of existence, and in at last apprehending itself as Absolute Spirit. The struggle of finite self-consciousness against Absolute Self-Consciousness—which had previously appeared to the former as something external to itself—now ceases. Finite self-consciousness has ceased to be finite; and thereby, conversely, Absolute Self-Consciousness has acquired the concrete reality it previously lacked. Indeed, in the celebration of the Eucharist—in the reconciliation of the Christian with God—where the sacrificial flame of prayer has consumed every trace of selfishness and ungodliness within the individual, and where, rising from these ashes like a phoenix, divine self-consciousness emerges as the Holy Spirit of the congregation: at that moment, this individual—in conformity with the divine Idea that constantly repeats itself in every real person—has himself become the eternal personality of the Spirit, which alone remains as the ultimate reality. If, then, the divine Spirit breathes within every individual, where, one might ask, has the distinction of the individual gone? Could one say that the consciousness of the place is fragmented within a single soul, simply because it resides in many? The hard shell and barrier of individuality have softened; and through all hearts and minds, only one thought seeks passage—a thought that, amidst this multitude, remains a unity. Much as an army acts like a single man—and at the commander's word, all move as one body, animated by a single spirit—so, too, is the entire community but one body with its many thousands of members; and the Spirit of God, having made Himself personally present within it, governs it with the utmost ease—just as we set our own hand in motion.

Just as in the time of Christ this eternal personality of the Spirit emerged as religious consciousness within a single individual—and thereby emerged into the world at all—and just as the new doctrine subsequently spread rapidly throughout civilized humanity; so, too, in our own day has the philosophical realization of this consciousness come to pass—a realization which, though initially confined to a single individual, has already found the widest dissemination and recognition among the scientifically educated. Yet it is not sufficient that the struggle of world history be waged solely within the consciousness of thinking minds, or that it merely result in such consciousness. Rather, it must transform itself into the victory of the eternal personality of the Spirit—a victory achieved as this Spirit permeates the entire state of reality and molds every aspect thereof in accordance with this principle—such that the entire realm of the Spirit appears as nothing other than the very body of God's self-realization. Following an expression used by my friend Cieszkowski in a letter to me, we may term this the *dénouement* of world history (the *dénouement de l'histoire*); or, alternatively, we may designate it as the approach of the Kingdom of God—a time when a new heaven and a new earth shall come into being, and when, with the end of all things, God shall be All in All. 

We must now elucidate, with the utmost precision, the speculative significance of these aforementioned religious concepts, thereby bringing to a close our exposition of the doctrine of the eternal personality of the Spirit.

In our times, a change has occurred in the relations between nations—a change that has given the entirety of history a transformed character. Whereas previously brute force in war decided the fate of nations, and passion often seized upon the most trivial pretexts—such as an insignificant commercial interest, a point of honor slighted, and so forth—to ignite the tinder of the greatest conflicts; we now observe nations, whose constitutions rest upon entirely opposing principles, drawing closer to one another and reaching mutual understandings through the medium of diplomatic negotiations. Our era has, therefore, been termed an "Age of Transactions," in that conflicting interests and principles are now reconciled through the amicable channels of negotiation. To be sure, we are still far removed from the moment when Providence might abandon its transcendent, inscrutable workings and step fully into the conscious awareness of the very actors involved; yet, wherever a peril arises that threatens to set Europe ablaze—wherever a nation is consumed by internal strife—diplomacy now seeks to intervene, to offer assistance, and to mediate a settlement. Dispassionate deliberations now guide the course of world history; and history itself is beginning to emerge no longer *a posteriori*—from the struggles of nations—but rather *a priori*—from the realm of thought. One can foresee the moment—however distant it may still be—when, perhaps after just one more war—but that one the last—diplomacy will be compelled to abandon its duplicity, its secretive machinations, its airs of self-importance, and the like; and when a Congress of the nations of Europe—nay, even of the entire civilized world—will convene to guide the affairs of the globe, openly and freely, as the supreme authority.

The more—according to Kant in *Perpetual Peace*—reason, within the internal organization of states, develops itself as a representative constitution into the edifice of human freedom, the more will it also prevail in the relations of states to one another, and likewise determine the actions and omissions of individual human beings. Thus, Schiller’s pronouncement will become ever clearer:

>World history is the world’s tribunal.

For reason itself brings the deeds of men to judgment and condemns everything that does not correspond to the concept of reason. Then, too, will Christ’s promise be fulfilled—that He would return to judge the living and the dead. But how can He return other than as the universal Divine Spirit, who is with us until the end of the world? This Holy Spirit will thus become the World-Judge, who will lead us into all truth. The more He comes to active expression within each individual, the more will He manifest Himself in the general order as a personal presence. The decisions of the Amphictyonic Council of Nations—the seat of which some French socialists have already, in spirit, relocated to Constantinople—will breathe the spirit of love, which binds all individuals and nations together into a single, universal consciousness.

If it is no longer necessary to view the universal Spirit as realized in one or more specific personalities—precisely because it inhabits the very core of the individuals themselves—then the "new heavens and the new earth" that were promised to us have arrived. Scripture speaks explicitly of a *return* of Christ; thus, the realm of the Spirit need not be conceived as a dwelling place hovering high above the earth. Rather, the "new heavens" that we, as Christians, are to await consist of this indwelling of the absolute personality of the Spirit within the hearts of individuals—hearts which are thereby transformed into the very throne of God. The "new earth," however—that which is to be bestowed upon us—is the complete subjection of nature to the purpose of the Spirit, so that nature may serve His ends: in a sense, the rediscovery of the lost Paradise, as human art and industry increasingly extend man's dominion over nature, reshaping it into a garden for human endeavor. Is the network of railways—which at this very moment is being partly projected and partly executed across Europe—not a subjugation of the most recalcitrant element in nature: the infinite spatial separation of things? Does this not promise, for the future, an infinitely more manifold communion of spirits than even the art of printing was able to grant us four hundred years ago? Through this greatly facilitated communication, the pulse of the spirits will beat with ever-increasing regularity; and ultimately, a single beat will stir all hearts, a single Spirit will animate all members of the great family, and a single collective deed will spring forth from the will of all.

Once this is achieved, the eternal personality of the spirit awakens to consciousness within the individual. Notwithstanding the inequality of individuals that stem from their diverse stations and faculties, all are nevertheless, in having brought this universal purpose of humanity to conscious awareness, One Body and One Soul.

>All the saints are but a single Saint alone,

>For they are one Heart, Spirit, and Mind within one Body.

The Self of the Globe has, within them, become conscious of itself. And if this Self—as the Microcosm, in contradistinction to the Macrocosm (i.e.,the entire natural universe)—has thus been articulated: then that spiritual realm—though spatially confined to a single point in the cosmos—is nonetheless of infinite extension; and true infinity is attained only here, not in the infinite progression of the spheres of the sensible world. These latter never correspond to their own concept, because their infinity is never complete or actual. Conversely, if God is the active principle within every individual, then the innermost concept of humanity has been realized in each one, and so the human race then enters—as Fichte puts it—the sphere of eternity. Finally, if we wish to contemplate the human race—as the ultimate purpose of world history—in this its eternal form, we must not depict this as though eternity itself were then to begin, rather it is only the *consciousness* of eternity that will then dawn upon humanity. Likewise, God will not then begin to be the active principle of the natural and spiritual universe itself, but the human consciousness of this sole activity of God shall come to pass. Thus, it is not with fire and sword, with thunder and lightning, with crashing and earthquakes, that the new heaven and the earth shall descend like a bride adorned. Instead, the fire of the Spirit shall seize the souls of men, and consuming the wood of vanity, sending forth the breath of God’s peace from the ashes.

With this altered consciousness, however, the entire position of the human race has undergone a transformation; and this, in turn, presupposes that—even in reality—the human race has already assumed a higher, more refined form. In order that the consciousness of God may awaken within each individual, everyone must recognize himself as permeated by the spirit of divine love, rediscover the divine essence in the humblest of his fellow human beings, and, through this love, embrace the entire human race. Love will thus be that which—as the Apostle Paul affirms—endures eternally, and is consequently higher than all else. This love—precisely because it is the Eternal—is not merely a human sentiment; although it manifests as the love of one human being for another, it is in truth the divine bond itself that unites spirits with one another. Yet this love is not merely the unconscious medium of the realm of spirits; rather, it attains to full consciousness within every individual. Each individual is not merely a self-seeking "I," but contains within himself a "Thou"—the universal "Thou"—and recognizes himself as identical with it, just as this great "Thou" constitutes, in and of itself, the very foundation of every "I." If, then, this awareness of the unity of persons is what we call love, it is entirely correct—as Spinoza asserts—to say not only that man experiences an *intellectual love of God*, but also that this intellectual love of man is, conversely and simultaneously, God's own *intellectual love of Himself*—the very love with which He loves Himself within man. For the universal *Thou*—which comes to consciousness within man—does not love the particular *I* in each individual, but rather the universal essence of that individual, which has become reality within him: the Son of God, who has been born in the heart of man. This intellectual love of God is, therefore, the love of the Father for the Son; and this eternal contemplation of the universal within the particular constitutes the eternal personality of the Spirit—which remains the same in every individual, notwithstanding his other particularities.

>Mark well: in the stillness of the soul, a Child of God is born,

>And once again, that which Adam lost is restored.

>If your soul is still, and night descends upon the creature,

>Then God becomes Man within you, and all things are restored.

The restoration of all things is, therefore—according to Angelus Silesius—not the recovery of our garments, our hair, or our physical bodies, and so forth, nor is it the restoration of other finite relationships of a higher order. Instead, it is the restoration of that which can never  be lost: the Divine Essence itself, and the Spirit of His Love—that Spirit which, even now and forevermore, chooses the human heart as its birthplace.

>Ah! If only your heart could become a manger!

>God would once again become a Child upon this earth.

To this end, however—as Angelus Silesius maintains—all that is natural must be consigned to the night of oblivion, yet this does not prevent all things from being restored. For whatever belongs to the creature *as such*—when severed from the Creator—is, in truth, nothing but pure Void:

>The sinner exists no more.

>"How? Do I not see him standing there?"

>Had you the true Light, you would see him vanish into air.

In this sense, therefore, every individual who—through the reception of the Divine Essence within themselves—has cast off sin, is eternal. For whenever, throughout the whole course of his earthly life, he imitates the example that Christ has left him—and as often as he does so— God is born anew within him; for the human being then transforms his heart into that very manger, ready to receive the Divine Child within himself.

This, then, is true the palingenesis of which every individual is all the more assured the more purely he has, within himself, embodied the Divine. For what significance lies in all that other, inessential tinsel which we must willingly return to the earth, just as we received it from her? Yet everything born of the spirit lives on imperishably within this eternal personality of the Spirit of Love. All we are left with is to address the objections that might be raised against our formulation of these doctrines—or, in other words, add an apologetic section by way of conclusion.

## Apology for the Existence of God

It cannot escape our notice that the manner in which we have conceived the personality of God will give rise to the most varied misgivings and objections; and we do not wish to wait until these are raised against us, but rather to anticipate them, so that we may address them immediately. To conceive of the divine governance of the universe by analogy with a human leader and ruler lies so close at hand—we have been so accustomed to it since childhood, and, so to speak, have imbibed it with our mother's milk—that we find it difficult to abandon this mode of conception. Upon closer examination, however, it proves to be an inexcusable anthropomorphism, rooted, on the one hand, in that intellectual laziness which rests content with tradition and accepts unquestioningly whatever the nurse, the mother, or even the catechism has taught us. Another reason why this anthropomorphism persists—indelibly, as it were—in the minds of men is human egoism: the tendency to measure everything by one's own standard, to seek oneself everywhere within the universe, and to project one's own modes of thought and action onto the nature of things. Just as the childlike and poetic outlook—characteristic of the infancy of the human race—ascribes human feelings and actions to all inanimate objects (and, in the case of animals, such attributes are still frequently ascribed even by adults today)—thereby elevating that which is beneath us up to our own level—so, too, do we draw down that which is above us into the sphere of the familiar and the known. A witty Frenchman [Voltaire] was therefore right to observe that, if God created man in His own image, man has certainly returned the favor.

According to the doctrine we have posited regarding the unity of the divine and human natures, this would be nothing at all objectionable. However, if we examine more closely the conceptions held by ordinary consciousness regarding the personality of God, the following objections to our formulation of this doctrine might be raised, stemming from those anthropomorphic prejudices: 

1. "A distinct personality of God—standing over against the
universe and possessing an extra-mundane consciousness—is
necessary," it is argued, "for three reasons: points which
cannot be abandoned without the gravest consequences for morality,
and without which it would also be impossible to regard the
world as a harmonious whole ordered by reason. For if
God possesses no personal consciousness, then, firstly, He
cannot have created the world according to a rational
plan born of eternal goodness; rather, it must have
emerged unconsciously from chaos. Or, more precisely,
it is itself eternal; it is the only God we can acknowledge.

2. "Secondly, whence comes the admirable order that we continually observe in the world? Surely we cannot ascribe it to—or credit it to—mere chance? But if no governing Providence presided over the world at its inception, how, then, can it be sustained by ordering wisdom?" Indeed, all theologians agree that it is one and the same divine activity that created the world and that also sustains it; for preservation is merely continuous creation. 

3. "Thus, according to philosophical doctrine," one continues, "the world would nonetheless be left for all eternity to blind chance; for, just as—as previously noted—no benevolent God created it, so too now and forevermore could no wise God guide and govern it. Where, finally—and thirdly—is the Judge of the world, if we posit no almighty, personal Will to evaluate the deeds of individuals and nations with His spirit of justice, to mete out reward and punishment to each according to their merit, and to come on the Last Day to judge the living and the dead?"

These objections amount to accusations of immense gravity leveled against philosophy—accusations whereby it is charged with atheism, irreligiosity, and the like. I do not intend to engage here in metaphysical arguments—specifically, the argument that a God possessing a distinct consciousness, separate and apart from the world, would necessarily stand opposed to the world as its object, thereby reducing Himself to something utterly finite and limited. Regarding the metaphysical impossibility of such a premise, I refer back to points raised in earlier lectures. I could undertake the refutation of those objections simply by appealing to this principle: that once a metaphysical impossibility has been firmly established, then whatever consequences may logically follow from it, the premise itself remains inviolable. But it would be both ungracious and presumptuous not to give due consideration to those moral and popular objections as well. My task here, therefore, is to demonstrate that our own conception is the only one truly worthy of the Divine—and, conversely, that the ordinary mode of understanding (which, admittedly, offers not the slightest rational justification for its assertions) actually degrades the very concept of God. Thus, the charge of irreligiosity lies with that side and not with ours, to the extent that they have allowed the Christian dogma to be displaced by such superficial notions.

As for the first point, an individual consciousness of God—one that existed outside of and prior to the world—would signify nothing other than that God, much like a human being in his actions, had set a subjective purpose for Himself within His own intellect, with the intention of subsequently carrying it out. Such an assumption would necessarily imply that God deliberated upon the world—whether He wished to create it at all, or merely how He intended to realize this objective. Yet such deliberation requires time; indeed, the Arab sect of the *Medabberim* explicitly asserted the temporal nature of creation precisely on the grounds that God had weighed the existence of the world against its non-existence, and ultimately chose existence over non-existence. But does one not then realize that, from the moment deliberation began until the point at which a decision was reached, God did not yet know what He ought to do—that Eternal Goodness, therefore, did not yet know what was good: to create the world or not? Furthermore, if God possessed the option to create it or not, we would be compelled to attribute arbitrariness to Him; and inherent in this lies the implication that the deliberator could have decided in favor of either the good or its opposite. But if He indeed possessed that capacity, what assurance do we have that God actually decided upon the good? For since, during the process of deliberation, he remained for a time unaware of what was good—for that is the sole purpose of deliberation: to acquire the correct insight—it is possible that this remained hidden from him even after he had reached a decision; and thus, whether through error—or—and the pen recoils from writing down such blasphemy—through ill will, he may have failed to do what was good.

Every healthy mind will recoil from such conclusions; and yet they flow necessarily from the premise that God is the conscious Creator of the world. Rather, it is—and remains—an egoism of the individual to regard consciousness as the highest principle. Thus, one thing becomes clear at the outset: that God does everything He does out of necessity. The Idea of ​​the Good is not something existing outside of God—something upon which He might gaze, as upon an archetype, and by which He might choose to guide Himself or not. To make God follow such an external standard would be—as Spinoza rightly observes—to subject God to Fate. Rather, the Idea of ​​the Good is the innermost essence of God Himself; God is, therefore, nothing other than eternal Goodness, and everything that flows from it flows with necessity from the nature of God. God, therefore, cannot act otherwise than He does, because He is necessarily bound to do what is best. By this, we do indeed exclude arbitrariness from the divine essence, but not true freedom. For that is free which acts in accordance with the inner laws of its own being. Since these laws of the divine nature are immutable, the very same things flow—with immutable necessity—from God’s free essence at all times.
Herein lies the concept of an eternal creation.
Because God and His actions are immutable, He has not, strictly speaking, ever *begun* to act; rather, whatever flows today and tomorrow from the eternal laws of His nature has always flowed from them.

>God is creating the world even now. If this seems strange to you,

>Know that with Him there is no "before" or "after," as there is here.

The nature of God would undergo a fundamental alteration if He were to have begun to be a Creator at a specific point in time, having previously remained at rest for an infinite duration. In that case, however, His status as Creator would be something accidental within Him; and one fails to see why this attribute of God might not have remained dormant for many thousands of years longer, or, conversely, why it might not have manifested many thousands of years earlier. Yet, since the world has indeed flowed forth from the eternal Reason of God, it has *always* flowed forth from it; and it lies in the very nature of God to have eternally realized Himself within a world—such that no beginning of this activity could ever be posited. That which follows here, on theological grounds, from the nature of God—namely, that He has always been a Creator—had already emerged for us, in the course of our metaphysical inquiries, from the very nature of the Universal and of Time; and thus, in what has just been stated, it finds merely a final corroboration and confirmation.

The Old Testament is by no means unfavorable to the doctrine of eternal creation—a concept that Origen, among others, sought to discern within it. For quite apart from the fact that many theologians interpret the Mosaic account of creation merely as a *reformation* of the world—whereby it was rendered capable of sustaining the human race—the concept of eternal creation is, in fact, implicitly contained within the very opening words of this narrative. For if God created the heavens and the earth "in the beginning," then no time had yet elapsed *before* the heavens and the earth came into being; they were, therefore, originally existent. Were one to assert—following Augustine—that time itself originated only *with* the creation of the heavens and the earth, then the continuity of time (which we have already demonstrated to be impossible) would be disrupted. In that case, eternity would have existed *prior* to time, and time would have commenced only *after* that eternity; yet such an "eternity prior to time" would itself be nothing but time—specifically, time in the guise of one of its own specific moments: the Past—even if it were to re-emerge in the future, subsequent to the cessation of time proper. A *temporal* creation signifies, fundamentally, a creative act occurring at a specific moment in time; it therefore presupposes the existence of time—and, by extension, the existence of change within time—that is to say, the very world that is yet to be created; for there is no such thing as "empty time."

The conception of a God who, prior to time, dwelt within Himself and reflected upon Himself—existing in the immutable enjoyment of His own supreme perfection—thus collapses into nothingness. We need not insist upon the fact that Lessing, for instance, associated such a notion with a sense of infinite boredom that it caused him anguish and distress. We must, however, observe that this concept is fundamentally un-Christian, as much as Christianity teaches us of the eternal generation of the Son. Were one to argue that the generation of the Son is distinct from the creation of the world—as we ourselves previously conceded—and that, consequently, while the former is eternal, the latter is temporal: this is, in a certain sense, undoubtedly correct. We could not, however, admit that the realm of spirits—the sphere of nature’s creative forces, conceived as the locus of Ideas or as the eternal Son of God—existed *prior* to the sensible creation; for that would imply that these creative forces remained, for an infinite duration, inert and uncreative. Yet the individual forms of the sensible world are indeed temporal in their origin—and are, therefore, destined to pass away. Creation is, consequently, temporal in its individual parts, but eternal in the general Idea of ​​Nature. Thus, the eternal and the temporal aspects of creation do not contradict one another in the slightest; they are merely moments of one and the same organic whole—and each aspect, without the other, would be as incomplete as if Time and Eternity themselves were mutually exclusive.

Eternal creation does not, therefore, arise merely out of a necessity derived from the concept of eternity itself—for in God, it has already attained its completion. For if the Eternal is, in fact, that whose concept is fully realized—or whose reality corresponds to its concept—then the Universe is the reality that corresponds to the eternal concept of God. The concept existing apart from its reality is just as much an abstraction as reality existing apart from the concept. From this, it follows with equal certainty that God did not exist as a subjective entity prior to His reality, nor did Time exist as chaos before it had been ordered by the divine intellect. Were one to claim—in order to avoid these contradictions—that God’s resolve indeed required no time to realize itself (such that, in its very inception as the *Word*, it simultaneously created the *Thing*), and that, furthermore, He did not find the *matter* of the thing ready-made, but rather drew it forth from within Himself: then, while we would accept these tentative concessions, we would nevertheless still be compelled to protest against the very notion of a *specific* act of volitional resolve—as such—even if conceived as occurring without any loss of time. For a *resolve* presupposes a state of *non-resolve*—and a specific moment in time at which the resolve first came into being—presuppositions which we have absolutely no right to attribute to God. Thus, to borrow the words of Augustine, God created the world *sine speciali voluntatis decreto* (without a specific act of volitional resolve); and to those who find this phrasing too bold—and who might prefer to read *sine indigentia* instead—we may reply that, in this context, the two expressions amount to the very same thing. For every decision of an intelligent being—once formed in time—presupposes a struggle, driven by which the being acts in order to resolve it.

How are we ultimately to conceive of eternal creation? Matter—since it is eternally endowed with a form-giving activity—has never been mere chaos; rather, the realm of forms—as the various moments of God's creative activity—has always been at work within it. These generative forces of nature constitute the very inner essence of nature itself—an essence which has thus eternally come to manifestation within matter. Therefore, there is no need to fear that the heavens and nature will ever cease to exist, as Aristotle rightly observes; for they have always remained the same—otherwise, potentiality would be elevated above actuality. To conceive of God, namely, as the abstract realm of forces prior to their active operation would be to reduce God to a mere potentiality—to a consciousness that has not yet realized its own purpose. Yet a consciousness that seeks to fashion its object only *ex post facto* would be confronted by an unordered raw material which it would first have to shape. Thus, the order of the universe would be made to emerge from disorder—a notion which Aristotle rightly deems absurd; to say nothing of the fact that, were Chaos independent of God's consciousness, it would remain doubtful whether the divine intellect could even succeed in mastering it.

The Idea-in-itself—which manifests itself within the universe—has thus been eternally present therein; it is nothing other than that very Order which we encounter within it. This possibility, which bears its own reality within itself; this Concept, which is eternally realized; this Being, which necessarily encompasses its own existence—such is the character of the Divine Being itself. And in conceiving of God in this manner, we have articulated precisely what has traditionally been termed the "ontological argument for the existence of God." From this premise, the eternal act of Creation necessarily flows. For if God—as Anselm of Canterbury presupposed in this argument—is the embodiment of eternal Perfection, then it is inherent in this nature that the aspect of Reality cannot be lacking to Him; consequently, God has never been merely an Idea-in-itself, but has always been an Idea fully realized—for a perfection devoid of reality would, by its very nature, be something profoundly imperfect. God and the World are, therefore, correlatives that mutually presuppose one another, such that the one is inconceivable without the other. God is the eternal Concept of the World, and the World is the eternal Reality of God.

The human race alone constitutes an exception; it can neither lay claim to the ever-constant, beginningless duration characteristic of the general forms of nature, nor did it emerge—at the moment of its inception—in perfect conformity with its own concept, having sprung directly from eternal Reason. Nevertheless, it is the supreme blossom of creation, and in it, God has become fully realized and has attained personality. Here, however, the most significant objection to our mode of understanding arises. For since the human race has developed only within the realm of time, it would seem to follow that the divine personality, too, is something that has merely *become*—something that has come into existence. To counter this objection, however, we need only recall what we have stated in our doctrine regarding the Incarnation of God, regarding the eternal personality of the Spirit, and—more generally—regarding the very concept of Spirit itself. We observed, namely, that the nature of Spirit in general—and consequently that of the Infinite Spirit as well—consists in recalling the distinctions developed from within itself back from their state of mutual externality to a state of unity and ideality. Toward this end, the Spirit must advance through its own autonomous activity; for only in this manner is it truly Spirit. The path toward this perfected ideality, however, requires that the Spirit first sublimate the *spatial juxtaposition* of these distinctions in such a way that they appear within it—at the very least—in a *temporal succession*—an ideality which, nonetheless, remains still tainted by the element of mutual externality. This, then, is the specific function of Time—the consciousness of which emerges simultaneously with the human spirit. Thus, within human consciousness, the Spirit has apprehended itself as the *abstraction* of ideal unity—standing in contrast to the *real* externality of Nature, which constitutes an abstraction in its own right. And to this antithesis of Nature and the Self—of Space and Time—it must come, so that Spirit, within history, may resolve it with full consciousness, and thus lay out, for its absolute realization, the entire fullness of its determinations. 

The in-itself existing essence of the Universe—which is merely unconsciously poured out into the whole content of Nature, and which, in the human Self, attains to consciousness *for itself* (but separated from its content)—is now, through the development of the human race, to attain to an existence that is *in and for itself*. God—who is not a mere moment of this process, but rather the totality of its movement—is neither solely the *in-itself* of Nature, nor merely the *in-itself* existing foundation of the human spirit (which, in the paradisiacal state, was posited as the temporally first and original perfection thereof). God is the identity of Nature and History: the ultimate goal of the development of the human spirit—and thus the complete subjection of Nature to the power of Spirit—as well as the *Self-knowing* of the eternal essence of Spirit within every individual; an essence which stirred primordially within the formations of Nature and the configurations of World History, and which, in these latter, emerged in ever-clearer outlines.

God is, therefore, the self-preserving and constant Being—existing amidst these contrasts of the eternal repose of Nature and the eternal movement of Spirit—which, having rendered itself objective within finite consciousness (for otherwise it would not itself be the infinite Being), therein rediscovers itself. Finitude is a necessary moment and point of passage for God; time, therefore, belongs to the mode of His manifestation just as much as space does. Yet, just as we must not, for this reason, conceive of God as a mere form of extension—even though He has poured Himself out into infinite space within Nature—but rather as the abiding, unified Idea underlying this spatial dispersion; so, too, does the succession of times not fragment Him; rather, even under this temporal form, He remains the indivisible order of events, identical with itself. His eternity is thus in no way impaired by the fact that His personality reveals itself as having become spiritualized within human consciousness. For in order to be truly real, it must pass from a state of mere *being-in-itself* to a state of *being-for-itself*; and for this transition, precisely this temporal development is required. Once, however, this consciousness has awakened within the human spirit, this eternal personality reveals itself as the primordial First—that which, for the discerning observer, had already gazed forth in recognizable features from every form of Nature and History; that which had to win for itself a temporal form of consciousness *within* time, yet which—once attained—simultaneously annuls time *as* time. The realization of this eternal personality within the human spirit is, in this way, reduced to a mere form—a form which, amidst all its own mutability, harbors the immutability of the Divine Being.

In a similar manner, we can now address the second objection—namely, that if no extra-worldly personality continuously governs and guides the world with wisdom, then chance must remain the master of the world, and the most perfect beauty, order, and harmony could not be sustained. However, if God is merely the eternal Reason—that which is constantly realizing itself, and has always realized itself, within the natural and moral universe—then He has, from the very beginning, imparted His entire essence to both. Thus, the assertion that God’s Will governs Nature—even though Nature appears to act of its own accord and through its own agency—can bear no other meaning than that the laws of Nature themselves perpetually generate and preserve Nature’s inherent harmony. In accordance with the ontological argument for the existence of God outlined above—which posits God’s Will as absolutely identical with the object of that Will—God’s Will is constituted precisely by the laws of Nature themselves; laws which, in their various phenomena, attain a perfect expression of their own essence. In the *idea* of Nature, these laws form a single, coherent, self-contained unity—a unity, however, that can only be designated as the *in-itself-existing* Personality of God, inasmuch as Nature, in its empirical totality, does not itself attain to self-consciousness. Is the harmony of the universe not established upon far more unshakable foundations when the rule and guiding principle of Nature is the Divine element *immanent* within it—rather than being guided by a conscious, external Will (one to which the world would stand opposed as an external object), given that an object can always elude the grasp of consciousness? Goethe, therefore, rightly observes:

>What a God that would be—one who merely gazed down from above,

>And let the universe spin round His finger like a toy! 

>It befits Him to stir the world from within,

>To cherish Nature within Himself, and Himself within Nature;

>Such that whatever lives, moves, and has its being in Him

>Never lacks His power, never lacks His spirit.

If the creative forces of the universe are themselves the realm of spirit—the realm of laws that governs the world—then divine wisdom is immanent within the universe, whereas a realm of spirits with a Lord beyond the stars—in heaven, above the starry firmament—would be an empty abstraction devoid of all reality.

The same applies to the preservation of the spiritual universe—to the guidance and direction of the human race, which is commonly referred to as Providence. How, one asks, are such Providence and Foreknowledge possible, unless a Being endowed with personal consciousness has preordained the entire chain of world events—events which now, unfolding in time through the sustained will of God, develop in accordance with that plan (much as a plant grows from a seed), or rather, unwind like a thread from a ball upon a spindle? Yet such a conception of Providence would, once again, be utterly unworthy of God. For everything that had entered into the pure consciousness of God would, by that very act, necessarily exist as an immediate reality. The plan and blueprint of world history, as it resides in the intellect of God, would constitute world history itself—unless, that is, God lacks the power to grant immediate existence to His thoughts precisely as He conceives them, and instead requires a vast span of time to realize all His thoughts gradually, one by one.

Thus, world history can no more be left to chance than can nature—however much chance *as such* in nature, and human arbitrariness in history, may seem to stand in opposition to universal reason and its natural and moral laws. Yet, were we to posit a personal consciousness guiding nature and history from the outside, then it is precisely *arbitrariness* that would reign over both—interrupting the course of nature through external intervention, and determining history *a priori*, yet capriciously. The moral world order, therefore—as Fichte asserts—is itself the Divine element that we must presuppose in history; and this God—the law of the moral world—is identical to the realm of natural laws, differing only in form. This God is no longer a mere abstraction, a fanciful or imaginary personality, but rather the eternal Personality that comes to consciousness within the human race—and, in doing so, consciously and as a truly active Providence, guides humanity through the course of history, preparing the final judgment and the ultimate end of all things.

We may term this conception of the preservation of the world the higher significance of the cosmological proof for the existence of God, whereas our doctrine of creation rested upon the ontological proof. The superiority of this significance lies, however, precisely in the fact that—just as in the ontological proof we did not, as is customary, proceed from the presupposed concept of a most perfect being—here, too, we do not take contingent things as our basis in order to infer from them the existence of a necessary being. Rather, the self-realizing universal Reason of the matter has been the starting point of our reflections; and since this Reason must be recognized as the sole reality inherent in the matter, it follows that it constitutes the *in-itself* of Nature and Spirit—the source from which their specific forms emerge with necessity, inasmuch as they are already originally prefigured within it. The Reason of the matter—which, amidst the seemingly contingent events of Nature and History, preserves itself spontaneously and through its own inherent power—is Divine Providence itself; for at every moment it reveals itself within this semblance of contingency and arbitrariness, bringing itself forth as an objective reality—and thus, as theologians rightly affirm, constitutes nothing other than a continuous act of creation.

The third point of view we mentioned earlier is, in effect, resolved by this of its own accord—partly through what we have developed today, and partly through what we set forth in our last discussion. For if we have perceived the Goodness of the Divine Idea in the fact that, within the universe, it constituted itself as an unconscious realm of laws; and if its Wisdom lies in the fact that the eternal nature of the matter itself is the Wisdom—which, as Heraclitus says, will guide us through all things, or, as Christ teaches, is the Spirit of God that will lead us into all truth, even unto the end of the world—then, by the same token, the Justice of God is thereby demonstrated. For since the nature of the matter is the only thing that eternally sustains and asserts itself, the goal of God’s governance is to allow Universal Reason to emerge as the victor from the struggle of world history; such that everything—whether among nations or individuals—that does not correspond to this eternal Idea must face judgment. The Justice of the eternal Idea thus consists in this: that the accidental, the particular, the arbitrary, and the sinful—which are in themselves inherently negative—are also posited as such in their manifestation, and must perish. Thus are the moral demands for an eternal justice—whereby each individual is recompensed according to their deeds—satisfied. Yet this, too, is not an arbitrary divine arrangement, but rather, just as much, merely a consequence flowing from the very nature of the matter. Thus, insofar as eternal justice constantly shatters the temporal against the rock of the eternal, the Last Judgment endures eternally—for it is realized precisely in the self-assertion of Universal Reason. All things—all that constitutes their essence—are ever and again restored.

>Behold, this world passes away. What? It does not pass away at all!

>It is only the darkness within it that God shatters.

If, then, in the creation and preservation of the world, we must still admit to a certain unconsciousness of the divine Idea—inasmuch as these constitute merely incomplete moments in the development of the divine life—this very unconsciousness is nonetheless transcended by the fact that, at the culmination of world history, universal Reason enters into human consciousness as the constitutive essence thereof, and history is thenceforth continued and brought to completion through the medium of thought. Yet this goal—this ultimate judgment—of history has, in turn, frequently been misconstrued as a mere terminus. For the actualization—the objectification, the attainment of consciousness, or by whatever other name one might choose to call it—of universal Reason is present not solely at the end, but at every stage, in every single moment of history. Each stage constitutes a necessary link in the developmental process of the Absolute Idea; in each stage, therefore, a specific moment of the Idea attains to consciousness—and with it, the Idea *in general* attains to its reality at that particular standpoint and juncture. It is only in its final form, however, that we find the truly perfected union of this consciousness—whereas every earlier form is, by the one that follows it, brought to judgment. If God is the ultimate end of all creation, and the glorification of God the goal of history, then this end is, in truth, attained everywhere; the "end of all things" is therefore present just as much at the beginning as it is in the middle—for all things are but a manifestation of God.

Thus, the beginning and the end merge into one; and Creation and the Last Judgment are identical. For in the preservation of the world—which endures eternally—it is created anew at every instant; and thereby, at every instant, whatever is null within it is shattered—not in such a way that its Divinity is thereby diminished, but rather so that the consciousness of this Divinity—and thus the Divinity of man himself—emerges ever more sharply. The union of past and future into the moment of absolute presence—a union proper to Eternity—has thus found its substantive realization here. The preservation of the universe through absolute Providence is, in essence, the eternal presence of that Providence within it; and it manifests itself—as Creation and Judgment—only within human consciousness as a "before" and an "after."

In this way, by means of the aforementioned viewpoints, we have arrived at the teleological proof of the existence of God. For if God is the End of all things—an End, moreover, that was already present as the Beginning—then the Purpose (which, as a *subjective* purpose, constitutes the very activity) is to translate itself into objectivity, so as to appear as a *fulfilled* purpose. Yet this subjective purpose is not to be understood in the sense of an *external* teleology—according to which the consciousness of the unfulfilled purpose would have resided in the mind of God *before* He endowed His determinations with existence within disordered matter, conceived as a chaotic object. We have already dismissed such a notion as inadmissible in our discussion of the two preceding viewpoints. Rather, God—as the subjective, as yet unrealized Purpose—is precisely the *in-itself-existing* Essence of the world; an Essence that was present from the very outset, and which, through its objectification within the spirit of the human race, merely elevated the form of *being-in-itself* to that of an existence *in and for itself*. God is, as Scripture declares, the Alpha and the Omega; as an *inner* teleology, He is thus the Soul of the universe—a Soul that, within the universe’s organic body, was already in motion from the very beginning, and whose activity merely sustains what is already present, while simultaneously cultivating it toward a state of perfect consciousness. Thus, God is the absolute Personality of the universe—a Personality that, at the culmination of world history, attains consciousness within the human spirit, only to then, in the final outcome, give form to itself as absolute Deed. However, since this consciousness of God within man—this knowing of God—constitutes the eternal life of man himself, this leads us to the vindication of the second doctrine we have posited—a task we intend to undertake in our concluding lecture.

## Apology for the Doctrine of Immortality

If we summarize the arguments advanced against our doctrine of the eternal life of man, they can be reduced to the following main points:

1. First, if no reward follows death for the deeds we have performed here on earth, then—it is argued—man’s happiness and the ultimate purpose of his existence are lacking; earthly life is merely a path toward this goal, and man would then be more wretched than the animal, for he realizes that he cannot attain his goal, whereas the animal attains the goal of its existence at every single moment.

2. Furthermore, virtue is impossible without faith in a continuation of life after death. For who, for instance—for the sake of virtue alone—would face death, and thus act with courage, if utter annihilation awaited him? As Saint Thomas Aquinas asserted: "If we did not await the resurrection of the flesh, we ought rather to commit any crime than to die." 

3. Finally, the justice of God is also incompatible with the philosophical conception of immortality, because in this life the good often suffer and the wicked are often happy, and this imbalance can only be rectified in another life.

The task of our present inquiry is now to demonstrate—precisely with regard to these three points—how ordinary consciousness, on the contrary, falls into the very errors it imputes to the speculative view; and to show that the latter alone is capable of resolving those difficulties and contradictions which are, indeed, encountered in the doctrine of immortality.

As for the first point—the assertion that man has no ultimate purpose unless his individual existence endures into infinity—we have frequently drawn attention to the fact that it is precisely this infinite progression which precludes the attainment of the goal. "Within this span of time," it is argued, "we do not attain our destiny"; we must, therefore, transcend this temporal realm and envision for ourselves another life—another objective for our activity—on a different planet, beneath a different sun. Yet, were the goal to be attained there, we would then have no need of any further life; thus, to ensure the continued existence of the soul, the goal must remain unattained even there; and so, after a second death, a third life must commence—and so on, into infinity. But are we, then, any closer to the goal? I maintain: not by a single step. For it matters not how far we have advanced along a path of infinite length; we remain forever infinitely distant from the goal. Let us posit, for instance, that the ultimate purpose of our existence is the knowledge of all those spheres and worlds that shine in the heavens, and that the means to this end is the traversing of them all: in that case, an infinity of space lies before us—an infinity which, consequently, can be traversed only in infinite time; that is to say, never. It is, however, absurd to devise a means for the attainment of a goal that renders this attainment forever impossible.

Only in our interpretation is the ultimate purpose of life attainable; for we locate it within life itself—and if attaining this goal is termed "happiness," then our view alone bestows true happiness. To be sure, we do not regard happiness as a state of inert repose, nor as a merely passive enjoyment following upon toil and labor; rather, happiness is—in accordance with Aristotle’s accurate definition—an activity that finds its purpose within itself: an *entelechy*. For were the purpose to lie outside the activity—which would then serve merely as a means—it would not be immanent to the reality of life, but would instead constitute a disembodied concept and a merely subjective ideal; yet this would preclude eternity from being the true destiny of man, given that in eternity, concept and reality are meant to correspond to one another.

If even the animals and all the formations of nature bear the purpose of their existence immediately within themselves, then man—the favorite of God—must not lag behind. "But," it is objected, "if the occupations of human beings in this life are unequal, how can they all achieve the same purpose?" Pomponazzi offers an excellent response to this objection: 

"Just as God has bestowed upon each individual limb within a single organism that which is best for both the limb itself and the universe, so too must we assume the same regarding the entire human race. It is a single body, whose members have distinct duties, yet in certain respects, they all share a common accord; otherwise, they would not constitute a single whole. Without such inequality, the human race would perish; and from this *commensurate diversity* springs that which is perfect, beautiful, and pleasing. Human beings possess either a speculative, a practical, or a mechanical intellect. While all human beings partake—to a certain degree—in the speculative intellect and in mechanical skills, bringing these faculties to perfection is the vocation of only a few. Conversely, to partake fully in the moral intellect is the universal vocation of the human race; and it is solely through this faculty that man is absolutely good, whereas through the others, he is good only in a relative sense. Every human being ought to be a good person, though not everyone need be a good philosopher. Nevertheless, the speculative intellect remains the purest and most divine of faculties—and thus, according to Aristotle, it encompasses the most attainable form of blessedness. And while it may not constitute the common purpose of the entire human race, it is, nonetheless, the highest purpose. Just as only a single point on Earth can serve as its true center, so too do not all human beings embody this ultimate purpose within themselves—a purpose that belongs only to a specific segment of the human race. Yet, to attain blessedness, it suffices that every individual—beyond pursuing the common goal of moral goodness, fulfills its specific purpose."

Just as we would not lament that a stone is merely a stone and not a plant—or that a plant is not an animal, and so forth—neither may we assume that individual human beings fail to fulfill their destiny simply because they perform such diverse labors in the Lord's vineyard. Indeed, in the case of human beings, such a lament would be all the more inappropriate than it is for irrational creatures; for here—notwithstanding the natural inequality that individuals possess from birth—freedom has shown everyone the way to at least partially overcome this disadvantage of nature, and to ascend to a higher plane through their own diligence and activity. Each person freely chooses their station, thereby occupying—by virtue of their own will—the sphere that best accords with their individuality. Hence, as the saying goes: a man's will is his kingdom of heaven. The aspiration of the lower classes—who distinguish themselves primarily through mechanical skills—should not, therefore, be to emulate those of higher station who have qualified themselves for their vocation through the scholarly cultivation of their intellect; rather, their goal should be to ever more fully perfect the ideal within themselves—and thus not to strive toward an infinity that lies outside of them, but to attain a perfection that they discover within themselves.

Thus, at every stage of natural and spiritual existence, the reflection of the divine Idea reveals itself; and to each stage, the Idea imparts itself in its entirety—even if each stage occupies a distinct degree of dignity. And were God now to grant speculative knowledge to a mechanical craftsman in the afterlife—as a reward for his moral virtue—that craftsman, assuming (in accordance with our premise) that he retains his individuality, would neither accept nor even comprehend this reward; for otherwise, he could surely have striven for such knowledge during this life as well. For surely, no one would wish to assert that in the afterlife, speculative knowledge is attained without effort—that it will, so to speak, fly into the believer's mouth like a roasted pigeon, should he happen to crave it. With a truer insight, therefore, the ancients assumed that the shades continue their earthly pursuits:

>Faithful love found the faithful spouse,

>And the charioteer, his accustomed course.

>Linus’s lyre sounds its familiar songs;

>Orestes recognizes his friend once more,

>And Philoctetes, his arrows.

If the inequality of occupations in no way hinders the attainment of happiness, the standpoint of moral action is—in any case—that which accords equal dignity to all individuals because it sets the very same goal for them. For to act morally does not mean to accomplish great deeds; nor does the worth of a moral action depend on whether it has contributed more or less to the welfare of humanity and to the attainment of its ultimate end. The moral element consists in the intensity of the activity that is commensurate with the specific ultimate end at hand; thus, whoever worthily fulfills the place he occupies is moral—and all the more moral, the better he fulfills it. Virtue is, therefore, the same regardless of the sphere in which it is exercised; and in this sense—as the Stoics maintain—there exists but one virtue.

"Righteousness," says Fichte in this regard, "views the individual person as existing solely for the sake of a certain vocation. Man’s temporal life is intended to leave behind an imperishable and eternal result in the spiritual realm; only insofar as he fulfills that vocation—which he recognizes as his own—may he tolerate and affirm his own existence. He regards his individual person itself as a thought of the Divine, and precisely as the Divine conceived him, so constitutes the purpose of his existence." Thus, in moral action, the divinity of the individual does indeed manifest itself—as Schelling asserts—for the moral element, being the activity of the individual that is adequate to his specific purpose or concept, constitutes that which is eternal within him. Here, therefore—for every human being, no matter how high or how low his station—lies the perfect attainment of his vocation (of whatever nature that vocation may be), and consequently, eternal blessedness; and this is available to him without his needing to await some future blessedness that is to be granted to him—without any agency of his own—in some miraculous manner.

From this alone it is already evident that, secondly, our view—far from being an impediment to virtue—will, on the contrary, promote it most effectively. For if we desire eternal life (and surely everyone will do so), then we must also ensure that our earthly existence leaves behind a lasting result in the spiritual realm, and thus, in itself, partakes of eternity. That this is best achieved through virtue, however, is self-evident. For since our moral action consists precisely in giving concrete expression to the concept and purpose of humanity within our own individuality, this very action bestows upon us that eternity which is inherent in this very realization of the Idea. We view ourselves as a specific mode of God's revelation, operating within the confines of this particular individuality; in doing so, we have received God into ourselves and have thus escaped from sin and the death it entails. According to our view, therefore, virtue is the sole source of eternal life—and, by extension, of blessedness; sin, conversely, is the source of misery, death, and annihilation.

However, we must examine more closely the grounds upon which the alleged incompatibility of our doctrine with a virtuous way of life is based—not in order to refute our opponents' views by arguing solely from our own premises (which requires no particular skill), but rather to expose the inherent contradictions within their own arguments and to demonstrate how the arrow of their accusation rebounds upon themselves.

"What great sacrifices," people now say above all else, "does not virtue demand! Who would willingly make them, were he not confident that he would be rewarded for them in another life?" Here we ask: by what means could virtue possibly be reduced to such a self-serving transaction? Does it then remain virtue at all—and are those so-called sacrifices truly sacrifices? What kind of sacrifices, in fact, are these that it boasts of? And so the flood of questions with which we could assail our opponents never ceases.

If we examine these questions more closely, the utter distortion of the conventional view will become apparent. If, in exchange for a few deprivations in this life, I can barter for an entire eternity of pleasures, then, in practicing virtue, I have had nothing but my own advantage in view—and have struck a very good bargain. I have made no sacrifice whatsoever; or rather, the sacrifice I did make has been repaid to me tenfold—and I made it only because I knew it would be repaid to me with interest. But in that case, does any merit whatsoever remain on the part of this so-called virtuous person? "It is conceivable," says Schiller, "that I might increase my own happiness through a sacrifice I make for the happiness of another. But is this still true even when that sacrifice is my very life? How is it possible for us to regard death as a means of increasing the sum of our pleasures? How can the cessation of my existence be reconciled with the enrichment of my being?" The presupposition of immortality resolves this contradiction—yet it also forever disfigures the sublime grace of this phenomenon. Any regard for a rewarding future excludes love. There must exist a virtue that suffices even without a belief in immortality—one that performs the very same sacrifice even at the risk of utter annihilation. Admittedly, it is indeed an ennoblement of the human soul to sacrifice present advantage for the sake of eternal gain; it represents the noblest stage of egoism. Yet egoism and love divide humanity into two vastly dissimilar races, whose boundaries can never merge. Egoism establishes its center point within itself; love plants it outside itself, within the axis of the eternal Whole. Love gives; Egoism lends: it is all one before the throne of judging Truth—whether for the gratification of the very next moment, or for the prospect of a martyr’s crown;—it is all one, whether the interest falls due in this life or in the next.

Since the common human view of immortality appears invariably to stem from that narrow-minded clinging to one’s own particular personality, Schleiermacher rightly casts a moral reproach upon this view: 

"They do not wish to step beyond their accustomed limitations; they desire to be nothing other than the mere manifestation of those limits. Anxiously concerned for their individuality—so far from wishing to seize the unique opportunity that death offers them to transcend that very individuality—they are, on the contrary, apprehensive as to how they might carry it with them into the life hereafter, striving at most for keener eyes and better limbs. Yet God speaks to them, as it is written: 'Whoever loses his life for My sake shall preserve it, and whoever seeks to preserve it shall lose it.' The life they seek to preserve is a life that cannot be preserved. For if they are truly concerned for the eternal duration of their individual person, why do they not trouble themselves just as anxiously about what that person *was* as they do about what it *will be*? And of what avail is the forward gaze to them, if they cannot also look backward? The more they yearn for an immortality that is no true immortality—and over which they are not even masters in their very *thinking* of it (for who can succeed in the attempt to conceive of a temporal mode of existence as infinite?)—the more they forfeit that true immortality which is theirs to possess at any moment; and, in the bargain, they lose their mortal life as well, consumed by thoughts that serve only to torment and distress them in vain. Would that they might but attempt—out of love for God—to surrender their lives!"

Thus, instead of the conventional notion of immortality facilitating virtue, it actually renders it more difficult. For if virtue demands the sacrifice of one’s particular individuality, then offering such a sacrifice becomes impossible if the conviction that my specific personality will be preserved for all eternity is firmly rooted within me; and the more unshakable this belief, the more self-serving the agent appears. Such a person will indeed surrender his life with great readiness—yet not in the service of the Idea; for he does not set the well-being of the Whole as his objective, but only his own personal happiness. Consequently, he will desire death solely that it may liberate him from this earthly vale of tears and summon him to eternal delights in the realms of peace. If, however, he lives here below in earthly comfort, nothing will induce him to relinquish his life. For he finds the very purpose of his existence—which is solely enjoyment—already attained right here; and he feels no urgency to abandon familiar joys in order to pass on to unknown ones. Yet should he feel unhappy, he will not shrink even from suicide—a course of action that most surely endangers virtue, precisely because voluntary death is permissible only when undertaken in the service of the Idea. Indeed, have we not witnessed individuals, driven by religious fanaticism, take their own lives so as not to be deprived any longer of the bliss of the afterlife? In truth, who would be so cowardly as not to cast off the burden of life, if doing so cost him nothing but the brief struggle of dying, only to enjoy eternal bliss thereafter? And if he endures his earthly suffering because the Eternal has issued His commandment against suicide—then it is, once again, merely a self-interested motive that restrains him from the deed: namely, the fear that he would thereby forfeit eternal bliss and be cast into Hell. Thus, self-interested determining grounds always lurk in the background; and even the purest virtue must submit to seeing such accusations leveled against it, if it harbors that faith within the depths of its heart.

Thus, this view contradicts itself: for at the very moment when you imagine you are renouncing all your individual aims on this side of the grave, you are in fact doing everything to secure them on the other side. But then what is the true nature of the sacrifices you claim to make right now? You seek to sacrifice nothing other than your sensual inclinations, selfish impulses, and egoistic interests. You boast, therefore, of giving up something so trivial and fleeting—as if such things did not already contain their own demise within themselves, or were in any way capable of enduring for eternity! The longer we indulge such sensual pleasures and interests, the more weary of them we become; and carnal pleasure ultimately perishes through its own excess. If, then, it is deemed a virtue in this life to sacrifice such pleasures, surely they would constitute something highly vicious were they to serve as a reward in the next. And if they are not vicious, why sacrifice here what we are destined to regain there as a reward for all eternity? Yet would not an interminable duration of such pleasures render them all the more a matter of indifference—nay, a burden and an unbearable torment—to us? And if it is spiritual joys that await us in another life, then we need not wait so long to attain them; for we can secure these very joys for ourselves right here and now—precisely through the sacrifice of those sensual pleasures—inasmuch as the active exercise of human love and of our own reason spontaneously bestows upon us these very higher joys.

If you now assert that virtue necessarily entails sacrifice—and that this sacrifice consists in renouncing personal pleasures, feelings, and aspirations—very well, then you have effectively subscribed to our view. For since, to us, virtue signifies precisely this—subordinating our particular individuality to the common good, living within the idea of ​​humanity, and performing those deeds that advance this welfare, even should it cost us the sacrifice of our entire personality—it follows that virtue is exercised most perfectly when this sacrifice of our individuality is offered not merely for *this* life (as you would have it), but also for *that* life (as we maintain). For surely you cannot allow a double standard—a different measure and weight—to apply to these two lives. Now, the more the individual relinquishes his particularity, the more purely does he manifest the divine Idea within himself. Therefore, if you assent to our metaphysical definition of eternity—namely, that within it, reality corresponds to the concept—you must concede that every virtuous act fulfills this requirement and thus already encompasses eternal life within itself; for in the person performing such an act, God has become incarnate. Your favorite expedient—that of attempting to reconcile the philosophical view regarding the *presence* of eternal life with the *future expectation* of its infinite duration—would be of absolutely no avail to you here; for, quite apart from the metaphysical contradictions inherent in the notion of infinite duration (which we have touched upon on several previous occasions), such an approach would—as we demonstrated earlier—further serve to tarnish and cast suspicion upon the moral purity of your motives.

Far from our view of eternal life rendering virtue impossible, virtue and eternal life are, for us, one and the same concept—since, in our conception of eternity, we are concerned not with the infinite duration of time, but solely with the substantial content of even the most fleeting temporal phenomenon. Inasmuch as virtue constitutes this very content, our virtue is not a legal title upon which we might base a claim to some future reward; rather, it inherently encompasses this very enjoyment, this blessedness, this higher peace of the soul—in short, the recompense of another, higher life. In contrast, your own self-centered, calculating virtue—which lends itself out at usurious interest—must constantly fear losing its entire capital; thus, in sacrificing present enjoyment for the sake of future gain, it remains perpetually suspended in anxiety, fearing the loss of both. Consequently—according to our view, not yours—virtue entails a genuine sacrifice, even while its reward, consisting of spiritual joys, remains entirely free of self-interest; for these joys are merely the necessary and spontaneous concomitants of a virtue pursued for its own sake. 

A virtue that still hopes for external rewards, on the other hand, would not be practiced for its own sake, but rather for the sake of the consequences it yields; it would not be its intrinsic beauty that moved us to embrace it. Such virtue, however, remains something highly precarious and unstable, for it rests upon an external motive—one that the shifting nature of our convictions may at any moment undermine. If, therefore, one levels against our doctrine the charge that it is dangerous to the State—on the grounds that, without the prospect of punishment and reward in an afterlife, the great mass of people would not be virtuous—then this very reproach rebounds upon the accusers themselves.

Admittedly, most founders of religions were also civil legislators; and one might therefore well entertain the view that they had faith in the gods. Religion was introduced—so Pomponazzi asserts—because, while the virtuous seek virtue for its own sake, others are moved by honor, others by physical rewards, and still others by nothing of the sort; thus, for this latter group, such an invention was deemed necessary. Yet this view would be as degrading to religion as it is insulting to the legislators. For it would imply, firstly, that these lawmakers—without concerning themselves with the truth—permitted themselves to tell lies, much like physicians do, in order to cure the sick. Secondly, such a course of action would be just as impolitic and shortsighted as it is morally reprehensible. For who can guarantee that the people will not soon see through the deception—the notion that religion was invented merely to enforce obedience? Furthermore, how is it possible to deceive a people in the long run? Must not enlightenment—advancing alongside education—inevitably penetrate the populace and thereby expose every external motive as insufficient? Although, admittedly, we do not wish to deny that during a nation's infancy, such motives may indeed suffice. But in times of mature reason, could a hypocritical piety ever truly succeed in forcibly squeezing us back into the worn-out shoes of our childhood? And even if—hypothetically—that belief were indeed valid, it would nonetheless remain immoral to degrade it into a mere means to an end.

How entirely different, by contrast, is the case with our own mode of perception! If, through higher education, the character of the people is habituated to love virtue for its own sake—then, as Simonides says, is virtue founded upon a "cubic base." Once the conviction has become truly vivid—that selfishness, sin, and the like are inherently hollow, and that for the momentary gratification they afford, they leave behind the ceaseless sting of satiety and the torment of a guilty conscience; whereas moral action pours the peace of God and all inalienable goods into our hearts—then is virtue sought for the sake of this very inner nature of hers; and since this nature necessarily remains ever the same, there exists no ground whatsoever why we should ever abandon her path. Thus, he who regards virtue alone as the source of blessedness, and vice as the source of misery—without believing in individual survival—is no less virtuous than he who merely expects a reward; indeed, he alone is truly free, whereas fear and hope reduce everyone else to servitude—a state utterly repugnant to the nature of virtue. Moreover, it is solely upon this view that a stable civic life can be founded in the long run, inasmuch as self-interested motives invariably serve only to foster further self-interest; and the Saint-Simonians are quite mistaken when they deem a civic life impossible in the absence of such faith. Admittedly, however, it is also requisite that the state has matured to a higher level of morality, and expresses this through its institutions; for only from general morality can that of individuals flow with certainty.

Finally, if we consider the assertion of Saint Thomas—that were there no afterlife, we would sooner commit every conceivable crime than suffer death—it is easily refuted. What, after all, have we gained if, in order to avert death, we burden ourselves with such guilt? For we cannot, in truth, escape the eternal annihilation that ensues with death. For crime does not remove death; it merely postpones it. But for how long? Perhaps Fate has already struck its blow against us; and yet, to prolong our lives—perhaps by a single day, or a few days, months, or years—we commit such a crime. But what, then, does this life look like—this life we ​​have thus managed to prolong? It is a life consumed by inner anguish and the torment of conscience—a life we ​​would willingly surrender a hundred times over, could we but undo what has been done, and drown the serpents of the Eumenides—which gnaw at our trembling hearts—in our own blood. We gain nothing but a life of shame—a life that weighs all the more heavily upon us the longer it endures; just as many, in the full awareness of their guilt, have chosen to put an end to these torments of conscience through the act of suicide. Thus is the practice of virtue—from the earliest years of youth until the very last breath of our mortal existence—a sure guarantee of inner contentment, a state infinitely preferable to the guilt-ridden life of many decades.

Thirdly, from our perspective, God’s justice, too, emerges as purer and clearer than it does from the assumptions hitherto held. For what kind of justice is it that allows us to suffer innocently in one world only to compensate us for it in another? Does it even know whether we desire this equivalent? Can compensation ever entirely nullify the harm suffered? True justice must not lag behind with slow steps—like the Homeric goddess *Ate*—but must rather flow from the very nature of things and follow upon deeds immediately. Pomponazzi, too, observes this point most aptly: “No evil goes essentially unpunished; no good goes essentially unrewarded. The essential reward of virtue, however, is virtue itself, which renders a human being truly blessed. Thus, no evil can befall the good person—neither while living nor after death. The punishment for vice is vice itself, inasmuch as the vicious person never knows peace.” Theologians term this punishment and this reward “natural,” and they enumerate them among the various modes through which God’s justice manifests itself. Yet this natural consequence of human actions is not merely one mode of divine justice among others; it constitutes its exhaustive manifestation. For since God is the *immanent*—rather than the *transient*—cause of all things, He never acts upon them from without, but always solely by virtue of the laws inherent within them; for these laws *are* God Himself. Given this reality, the choice—which Saint Thomas deems impossible—presents no difficulty for us. Our moral philosophy is, therefore, purer than the conventional rationalist ethics that some seek to sanctify by cloaking it in the guise of Christianity. Thirdly, from our perspective, God’s justice, too, emerges in a purer and clearer light than it does from previous assumptions. For what kind of justice is it that allows us to suffer innocently in this world only to compensate us for it in another? Does it know whether we even desire this equivalent? Can the compensation completely nullify the harm that has been suffered? True justice must not limp along with tardy steps, like the Horatian Nemesis; rather, it must flow from the very nature of the matter and follow deeds immediately. Pomponazzi, in particular, makes a very apt observation regarding this: "No evil remains essentially unpunished; no good remains essentially unrewarded. The essential reward of virtue, however, is virtue itself—that which renders a human being truly happy. Thus, no evil can befall the good person, whether living or dead. The punishment for vice is vice itself, for the vicious person knows no peace." Theologians term this punishment and this reward "natural," and they frequently cite them as among the various modes through which God’s justice manifests itself. Yet this natural consequence of human actions is not merely one mode of divine justice alongside others; it constitutes its exhaustive manifestation. For since God is the immanent—rather than the transeunt—cause of all things, He does not act upon them from without, but always solely by virtue of the laws inherent within them; for these laws are God Himself. If, then, we recognize that virtue rewards itself every time, and that vice punishes itself every time, we have thereby sufficiently comprehended divine justice.

Indeed, this is precisely how matters stand. If we consider the moral act in its highest significance—as a world-historical deed that fundamentally advances the course of the human race—then the power of the divine Idea inherent within it is, in and of itself, so commanding that success is inevitable. Such an individual senses the God who moves him dwelling within; driven by divine inspiration, he acts, and—behold!—sees a new world emerging, radiating outward from the circle of his thoughts. The mere act itself—even without witnessing its outcome—constitutes, for such a hero, the highest bliss. Typically, other individuals are soon stirred by the impulse he has set in motion; they rally around his banner and, with united strength, bring his plans to fruition. Yet even if the human race attains the maturity requisite for the hero’s aims only after his death, he has nonetheless already tasted that bliss in anticipation, and he dies in the unwavering conviction that he has laid the foundation for the well-being of generations to come. External splendor, fame, and honor may surround him—or, should they fail to do so owing to the belated maturity of his contemporaries; indeed, even if that same world inflicts upon him external coercion, injustice, and misfortune—what, to him, are all these tribulations compared to the consciousness of the divine Idea within him—an Idea which, by virtue of a new ontological proof, as it were, already encompasses reality within itself?

Yet even within the most restricted spheres of moral action, this inner, essential reward remains undiminished. As Herder aptly observes, everyone is the architect of their own destiny. As I act and perceive the world, so does the world receive me. The circumstances, situations, and relationships in which I find myself—aside from external preconditions, which may, of course, be more or less favorable—are my own doing; through its actions, my individuality establishes itself within the surrounding world, in a sense, as an enduring legacy. Whoever conducts themselves toward their fellow human beings in a moral, noble, and humane manner sows a seed in their hearts—a seed that will take root and, in due time, bear for them the richest of fruits. Their conduct awakens the affection of others; and, as the proverb tells us, love makes all things easy. The ancients, therefore, were justified in their conviction that the virtuous are happy. For, in the first place, virtue possesses—in and of itself—that happiness which is the only secure, inalienable, and therefore truly genuine happiness: inner contentment and peace of mind. And thus, when—in our modern view—we seek to separate the inner from the outer, often portraying the virtuous man as unhappy in order to thereby glimpse and leave open a back door to another world wherein this discord might be resolved, the intellect inherent in this perspective simultaneously furnishes a counter-argument to its own assertions: namely, that happiness is even harder to bear than unhappiness, and that the latter is often a necessary trial from which our virtue emerges all the more radiant—such that, through this very means, the highest happiness is ultimately prepared for us.

Whatever rewards and punishments might be allotted by God to the virtuous and the wicked—whether in another sensible world, on this or that planet, in heaven or in hell—would be inessential and accidental; they would therefore pale into insignificance when compared with the judgment we have just described—a judgment that is constantly being administered by God here on earth. Man, as Schiller says, carries his heaven and his hell within himself. And how could God possibly mete out punishments and rewards other than those that are in harmony with the spiritual nature of the souls being judged? Dante grasped this insight with great beauty in his poetic portrayal, depicting the torments of hell most aptly as the internal consequences of one’s deeds—as when he compels false prophets to look backward, tyrants to wade through blood, and the lustful to spin endlessly in a whirlwind. Thus, if the knowledge of God is to be granted to the good in an afterlife, then this very energy of reason—through which the Divine reveals itself to our spirit—is already, here and now, the rightful inheritance of virtue; conversely, for vice, the absence of such knowledge constitutes no deprivation, for vice is inherently incapable of partaking in such spiritual delights. Sensual torments, however, would clash too sharply with the refined sensibilities of any cultivated mind to warrant our entering into a detailed refutation of them here. Yet, if the "worm" mentioned in Scripture—the one that never dies and gnaws at the heart of the damned—is interpreted in a spiritual sense, what do we find but that very "natural wages of sin" we described earlier—a consequence whose sting, even across the infinite expanse of time, would surely grow duller rather than sharper?

Accidental punishments and rewards, Pomponatius further observes, are separable from the deeds themselves. God would thereby appear as an arbitrary figure undertaking a connection entirely unfounded in the nature of the matter. Yet this would not only run counter to the metaphysical concept of God, but would also nullify His moral attribute of justice itself—according to which punishment must flow from the very nature of the deed. Finally, Pomponatius very rightly notes that accidental rewards detract from essential ones—as would be the case if one were to act morally solely in the hope of recompense; for in doing so, the very purity of the motive—and consequently, inner peace of mind—is lost.

With this, my highly esteemed gentlemen, I conclude these lectures—with the request that you view them as attempts: attempts, in matters that lay claim to humanity’s highest interests and that tear it asunder into bitter factions, to both anchor myself firmly in my own scientific convictions and to gain, through your agreement and applause, the objective validation of these very convictions.
