# Decision

> This webpage is a direct translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Anthropology and Psychology, Vol. III* (1840) 

The freedom that the mind acquires through its drives and its dependence on them is still contingent upon the existence of an actual goal that it is driven towards. Yet, it is now within the power of the subject to determine which object given to it shall fulfill its ends, to awaken the drive that has developed within it to the point of passion. This drive constitutes its interest, the first determination of the reflective will. The interest itself, however, is not imposed upon the mind from the outside, but precisely because it can prioritize the system of drives as it sees fit, it secondly has the the power to choose what will satisfy it or not. As a product of nature, the will is faced with a contradiction, that it is free from every individual drive and yet not free from drive as such. The mind strives to resolve this contradiction by, thirdly, elevating the individuality of drives into the universal drive for happiness, thereby contradicting the contradiction of the independence and dependence of the will, since the underlying autonomy of the individual drives makes their integration into a true universality impossible.

## Interest

In animals, the immediate content of their drives is also the purpose of their activity. It is for this reason that animals have no free will and necessarily obey the drive that presents itself to them unless an external obstacle suppresses it. Human beings, however, need not act for the sake of drives because their will is capable of infinite self-determination. Therefore, even if some drive is demanded by my sensory nature, this does not necessarily give it any inherent right to be satisfied since it entirely depends on my decision to satisfy it or not. This permission that I grant it comes from the interest I have its content. The animal seeks in the drive only to satisfy its hunger and thirst. The human being, on the other hand, seeks to satisfy its own interest, not because their nature wills it, but because the mind has placed its interest in it. The primary content of interest that the human being is thus the execution of their purposes. The presence of mind is not lacking, but is the condition of all human action. Whatever the content may be, the formal aspect of interest is contained in the fact that the human being carries out their purposes, overcomes this limitation of being merely subjective, in order to make them objective. The natural drive thus becomes only a means to human interest, and so Fichte conceived of it as the conditio sine qua non of freedom. The animal always follows only the drive of nature, and is therefore always guided by its instinct. It is only the human being that transforms the natural drives into interests that are entirely their own.

Nothing comes about without interest, it is the motive of all human action, as Helvetius so rightly stated. However, we must carefully distinguish the interest as such from self-interest, even if the majority of people are generally driven to action by mere self-interest. For this, we must also conduct a defense of interest in the same way we have done for the previous viewpoints. Interest, like passion, can have a positive relationship to the good as the form in which every content of the drive or passion is made into one's own. Just as nothing great can be accomplished without passion, so too is nothing great accomplished without interest, for the interest is what accompanies all action. The only question is, where do we place our interest? The person of great character is the one who directs their interest to absolute ends, towards good actions, and the like. It is therefore a skewed and one-sided assertion of reason in morality when it demands of a person that they must act selflessly and deny themselves any personal satisfaction and interest in what they do. To erase interest would be to erase the driving force for all action, for interest itself is nothing more than the force that drives all morality. And as wrong as it is to want to banish interest entirely from morality, whatever its content may be, it is equally misguided to consider self-interest in as the sole source of all action. It is a narrow-minded view to want to sufficiently explain the greatest deeds as the result of pettiest interests. 

Since the content of satisfaction does not depend on any specific drive, at first the content of interest will only be the indeterminate concept of the drive itself, the activity of transitioning from the subjective to the objective. Activity in general is therefore the immediate content of interest and we are only interested in that in which we can actually perform as an activity. The goal of our existence is thus contained in the activity of existing in-and-for-itself and is not contingent on any other end. Aristotle also calls life an activity that is an end in itself. However, the activity of life cannot be understood in the abstractly from its content. The value of activity lies solely in its content, thus it is essential to move beyond the abstract, contentless freedom of interest by giving it content. Since this content has hitherto only been found in the drives, interest must return to the drives in order to find its specific content of interest. However, since the will has autonomy over the satisfaction of its drives, it likewise has the power of choice to pursue what drives that will satisfy its ends.

## Choice

The will does not remain at the level of formal interest nor at a single drive constituting the entire content of the interest, but because the will knows itself to have risen above every specific content, it establishes itself as the indeterminate universal, which acts indifferently towards every content it encounters. However, the will does not bring this indeterminate freedom to consciousness unless it shows itself as the power to determine itself out of its indeterminate universality. Both moments, indeterminacy and determinacy, necessarily condition each other. When the will makes a choice, however, determinacy negates indeterminacy and vice versa, whereas in happiness both moments are united and bring the totality of the individual drives into a spiritual universality. The transition that the will makes from the abstract universality of interest to the concrete individuality of the drive is now the choice that the decisive will makes between its various drives. Since the animal cannot choose between its drives, it is still captivated by strongest drive that drives it to act even if more than one drive acts upon it simultaneously. The human will, however, has the power of choice to pursue any possibility of its choosing and can change course at any moment if it decides the goal is no longer suitable its interests.

With choice we have arrived the first condition of practical freedom, and it is therefore also defined as the freedom to choose between different inclinations. However, this first condition of freedom is not yet true freedom, but is precisely arbitrariness, or choice as an end-in-itself. The formal understanding praises arbitrariness as actual freedom, yet this of idea freedom is destined become unfree by its own logic. Therefore, if one accuses philosophy of fatalism because it refuses to recognize that actual freedom is arbitrariness, then the accusers are themselves the fatalists since they know no other freedom than that which is no freedom at all. Philosophy teaches a higher freedom, one that is more enduring than the concept just described. 

That freedom of choice is not true self-determination is demonstrated by the doctrine of determinism, which has appeared in its greatest development since Spinoza and Wolff. The decision that seemed to arise from the will is, in fact, not pure self-determination at all, but is dictated by interest and passion. Passion is itself determined in turn by an external object and an inner stimulus derived from desire. The decision thus depends on the drives and inclinations that are exerting their influence on the person. The individual has the power of choice between this or that drive, but the choices they are given are always a content given to them externally, not generated from within themselves. According to this, man is completely unfree with regard to the content of their actions, and if acting to satisfy a drive is the only kind of action, then there can be no meaningful freedom for man at all. The drives we have are our own impulses, they constitute the nature of our spirit, but only in relation to an objectivity that is independent of us. The only thing that remains for the self-activity of man is that they choose what content to connect themselves to. This connection naturally occurs in animals through instinct, but in man it is the result of free choice. Man thus appears to possess a formal freedom in the power of choice, and arbitrary choice would be precisely this formal freedom, similar to the power of imagination. The purpose is provided by the passion that moves the will to act, but the means to this purpose seem to be in my hands: the choice of whether or not to realize this or that purpose is always my own.

However, even the freedom to choose between various drives and ways of satisfying them is easily refuted by determinism. When several drives stimulate our will, we are allowed to choose among these different objects of our activity. But how can we make a choice for ourselves when the preference we give to one thing over another is already determined from the outside? Determinism views the will like a pair of scales. As long as I do not act, as long as I am not yet decided on my decision, the motive for choice remain in equilibrium. To decide, on the other hand, means to follow the preponderance of a motive that tips the scales to one side or the other. The motive is what determines me, thus I am not free even in the choices that I make. Even in games where a person is presented with two choices, such as the choice between two coins placed at different distances on a table, the choice that the person makes is not free. If the will chooses the coin that is closest to them, they are, without knowing it, determined by an external motive. However, even if the will is aware of the purpose of the game and reaches for the less conveniently placed gold piece, the intention to not be determined would be what determines them. Just as Buridan's donkey, placed exactly between two equally large piles of thistles, would be pulled with equal force in two opposite directions and be unable to move from the spot, so too would man, if each side of the scale of deliberation held equal weight for him, remain absolutely inactive. A consequence that Buridan draws from this is that under absolutely identical circumstances, two people would necessarily have to do the same thing. 

What determines the will cannot be found in nature, for the fact that the will is determined is because it is determined by its arbitrariness. The will is what determines its determinism, or as Leibniz put it, that the motive inclines but does not necessitate the action. What applies to the donkey, for example, does not apply to man, and in the case of man, the absolute presupposition and demand of free rationality must be made. Every animal of the same species, on the other hand, has the same drives and stimuli, and necessarily satisfies them in the same way under the same circumstances. Man, however, makes this or that interest, this or that passion, the maxim of his actions, and thus each person is determined differently by their drives. While determinism is right that unfreedom is contained in the satisfaction of every drive, this does not preclude the existence of choice, which is to be found in arbitrariness or liberum arbitrium. Arbitrariness is the false freedom of the slave, acting only as far as their chains permit. Arbitrariness therefore depends on necessity in that freedom and unfreedom are simultaneous in it. They are only bound together in contradiction without also being their necessary interconnection, which is the very deficiency of arbitrariness. The form of freedom is still separated here from the content of freedom, the latter of which belongs to external necessity. The indeterminacy of the will, which constitutes the essence of arbitrariness, cannot possibly determine and fulfill itself from within, but requires an objective means for this purpose. These heterogeneous elements are now reconciled in true freedom. Like arbitrariness, freedom is not without necessity, rather, the ethical purpose or the good is the fulfillment of the will. When the will chooses this content, it simultaneously relinquishes its arbitrariness and unites the form of freedom with the content of its purpose.

Circular necessity is freedom itself, just as God cannot act otherwise because he can only will what is actual, yet these determinations flow only from his own nature since they are simply the emanations of his absolute being. The greater insight a being has, the less arbitrary its choices are, for the more clearly and purely the necessary moral purpose emanates from beneath the veil, the more it comes to light in God. In this way, arbitrary choice is neither true freedom nor true necessity because it maintains both in their separation. Arbitrary choice is not free, for its choice always has an externally given content that it did not choose. The necessary, on the other hand, is that which cannot be otherwise. The good is necessary because the best outcome is willed unchangeably, but for arbitrary choice, the more arbitrary the choices are, the more the person wavers in their decisions from one thing to another. The indeterminate will is the will that wills nothing, anything that it chooses to fill the void is equally as indifferent and arbitrary as any other. Arbitrariness is therefore the existence of contingency in the will.

The particular content which I consider mine in arbitrariness, because of its contingency, is not adequate to my universality. I do not feel free if my choices are arbitrary, so I naturally reject them because none of the choices matter to me. Arbitrariness is thus caught in this contradiction, shifting from indeterminacy to determinacy and back, without allowing the two to truly permeate each other in and through this change. The will needs determinacy to function because the indeterminate, arbitrary choices are empty ones. However, every single determinacy is inadequate to the concept of the will, there is no one satisfaction that can satisfy it. The will, as Fichte understands it, is driven to go beyond this specific content because it limits the absolute activity of the ego, but the overcoming a limit is only the creation of a new one. From the satisfaction of one particular drive, the ego proceeds to the satisfaction of another, and so on ad infinitum, without ever being able to overcome the intrinsic limit that it faces. The obstacle is the particular and self-particularizing satisfaction, in order to remove it, the will strives for the universal satisfaction of the drives it has, so that in it the reality of the will corresponds to its concept. This universality that the will seeks is happiness, the form of universality applied to the content of the drives.

## Happiness

As the infinite progression of drives moves from one to the next, no one drive is considered absolutely final. The universality that the will achieves in happiness presents a dialectic of drives that seeks to transcend itself. The will, in order to attain happiness, sacrifices the satisfaction of one drive and proceeds to another, but the same deception of satisfaction in the first drive is once again repeated with each subsequent one. The will is caught in this contradiction, recognizing each individual drive as insufficient for happiness, and yet having no other means to attain it. The question is then, how the drive should be satisfied so that it is adequate to the universality of the will. In itself, the drive does not possess the measure of its own satisfaction. Happiness, on one side, elevates a specific drive to this form of universality, the moment of passion and interest that it incorporates within itself. Its other side, however, is that because this generalized drive is still bound to external conditions, it can only be supplemented by arbitrariness, which presents the entire manifold of drives for the will to choose from. Happiness is therefore the interest that makes one drive the ultimate end and seeks to justify its formal universality by not excluding the satisfaction of all others, reducing them to a means for the satisfaction of this main drive. Happiness consists primarily in the purposing of drives as means to its own ends. The drives serving the interest are satisfied only on the condition that they promote it,or at least, that they do not hinder it. Thus, a hierarchy of priority is established among the drives themselves such that there is always one that is preferred over the another, which is the only way the will is emancipated from its dependence on drive. Arbitrariness proceeds with calculating reason when it weighs which drives provide more satisfaction and should therefore be preferred over the others. But this subordination and sacrifice has just as little inherent measure as desire itself, arbitrariness remains accidental here as well. Everyone places a different drive at the top and subordinates the satisfaction of the others to it.

If every drive has the same value as the others, subordinating all of them to one drive does not lead to true universality, nor to a complete system of happiness. For by elevating the first drive to the status of universality, the remaining drives disturb and destroy each other, seeing each other as a means that must be sacrificed to the purpose of the moment. In order to maintain peace between the drives and to not exclude them from universality, we must cannot leave them in their immediate, original form, which is their dependence on external circumstances. Just as sensible intuitions must shed their contingent contents if they are to be elevated to general concepts, then the elevation of drives to happiness works the same way. Already in the pursuit of happiness, the will acquires the insight that the drives, in their purely natural mode of satisfaction, function as obstacles to happiness and in this sense are evil. The will now understands that there are some drives that have to be purified in order for it to achieve happiness. If one cannot hinder the activity of another, then no individual drive is be allowed the original immoderation of its desire. Here, not even one drive is given precedence since that would be a one-sidedness to which the passion of the natural will is still attached, and to which the reflective understanding of happiness has no other choice but to abandon. To this end, and because all drives have equalized, they must limit each other, each yields to the other so that none can exercise complete dominion or elevate itself to unlimited universality. Since the naturalness of each is thereby suppressed, they can all be pronounced good, for only the excess and exclusivity of drive makes it evil. However, how far this purification and taming of the drives must go in order to eradicate the bad in them falls entirely within the arbitrary judgment of the subject. But if the drives nevertheless retain their purely sensual content in spite of their moderation, and this content itself is not reprehensible, how can it be good to strip away even a part of its gratification, let alone completely reject this or that drive?

Each drive is an essential determination of the will that follows from its very nature, none may be excluded, and happiness is only achieved in satisfying the totality of these drives. Only when the will is aware of itself in the totality of its drives does it realize the universality that it inherently possesses. This standpoint in the development of the reflective will is higher than that of the natural will since the spirit is not immersed in a single satisfaction, but rather, with the universality inherent in the purpose of happiness, rationality already begins to appear in it. Even though the universality of happiness is only empirical universality, it does not yet concern the content, which remains an endless series of particulars. Although thinking does not yet constitute the content of the purpose, but only its mere form, it nevertheless elevates the sensual will toward the demands of rationality.

In happiness, a certain universality is indeed attained and realized, but not the kind that is the object and goal of our striving. Since this universality appears only as a sum or totality of parts, it exists only in the total comprehension all individual satisfactions, not as the universal present in each one. Each individual satisfaction is only an imperfect moment of the whole, and since their totality can never be empirically given, happiness becomes an empty ideal once it is pushed into an infinite progression. However, arriving at the irresolvable contradiction of the infinite progression within a given standpoint is nevertheless a sign that we have exhausted this standpoint and must resolve its contradictions. Thus, since happiness is the totality of individual satisfactions, no one satisfaction can maintain itself as an end against this universality, but is always reduced to a mere means or instrument of it. The individual satisfaction as such is superseded, only the universal contained within it has truth. The will now desires only the universal that is present to itself in every particular, and since this universal is intrinsic to the nature of the will, it is driven to satisfy only itself in every satisfaction. This universality, as the infinite form of the will's self-determination, is no longer empty, but has itself as its content. Therefore, the fact that the will desires only its own self-determination means that it is identical with itself in the otherness, i.e. the will is free.
