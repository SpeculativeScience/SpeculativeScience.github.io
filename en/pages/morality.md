# Morality

Note: This webpage is a direct translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Natural Law, Vol. I* (1866) 

In morality, freedom attains an existence that is appropriate to its inner concept. For property, the existence of freedom depended upon the ownership of external things, and for contract, the existence of freedom depended upon another will, which only ever posited freedom as an external existent. But for injustice, since the criminal arbitrariness of one generated revenge in the other, freedom now exists as the knowledge of freedom for spirit. Not only does the injured party, through the deed of the offender, attain an inner sense of justice, but, as the injured party carries out revenge, this inner existence of freedom also arises in the offender. The arbitrariness of both parties is transformed into the universal existence of freedom, which finds its object in itself, no longer in or as an external thing. The subjective freedom of the moral standpoint must now demonstrate its objective existence just as the objective freedom of the legal standpoint demonstrated its subjectivity. To this end, both standpoints must be compared more closely. For both standpoints, the free will manifests itself as the will that wills itself. Thus, the formal freedom of guilt and imputation cannot be absent from either standpoint. Without formal freedom, actions are neither legal nor moral. The difference between the two standpoints, however, is determined by the content of what formal freedom has as its object.

In legality, the content is drawn from nature. Freedom manifests itself in a thing that is not itself free, but serves the will, so the standpoint of legality is moreso concerned with the manifestation of freedom in the thing. Legal attribution is therefore different from moral attribution in that the former only depends on whether the harm contained by the intention exists or not. But since the entire content of the existence of freedom is now determined from within, the spirit seeks its freedom in its relation to its own conditions. Spirit is only free when it posits itself as free and liberates itself, purposing the unfree conditions in it in order to realize the objective existence of its freedom, i.e. the manifold of drives within the will. Since in legality only the formal intention is what matters, in morality the primarily concern is about the material intention of the action, the motives and purposes behind it, in short, the inner nature or character of the subject. The contrast between the two standpoints can thus be described in one phrase: if in the moral standpoint the impersonal existence of freedom is irrelevant, then in the legal standpoint the personal existence of freedom is irrelevant. Legality does not concern itself with the inner motivations and purposes within the will since it only needs to know whether a certain right has been violated. Hence, only now does it become clear that legality has nothing to do with affirmative commandments and duties. Apart from the external existence of freedom in which I am permitted and whose violation in the other is forbidden to me, regardless of the motives of my action, I am free to determine myself as I wish. However, if the will is to make itself the objective existence of universal freedom, it is incumbent upon it to elevate its drives to the universal content of freedom. This universal content, flowing from the creative source of its own inner being, is the Idea of ​​the Good, which strives to shed its subjectivity by making itself objective. The Idea of the Good generates the strengths of the moral standpoint, but also its weaknesses. The shortcoming of the moral standpoint is simply that, because the objectivity and generality of purpose is strictly subjective, it forgoes any external guideline, permitting any purpose to be arbitrarily chosen and discarded. The moral standpoint consists precisely in overcoming this contradiction to that it necessarily transforms and reconciles itself with objective freedom.

The path to this is marked by the threefold division of the moral standpoint. On the condition that the drives and their satisfaction in well-being are no longer indifferent to each other, as they were in legality, but are to be transformed into the universal existence of freedom to realize the good, then this can have no other meaning than the coordination of the drives by the rational will. The inner disposition of an agent that drives them to do good is virtue. The correspondence of the drives to reason still leaves the drives with an affirmative meaning. However, the drives preserve their independence from reason by demanding action. This command to do good solely for the sake of the good is duty. But since duty would be empty without the content of that the drives provide, they must be utilized as a means to an end of any dutiful action. As a means, however, the drive remains indeterminate and can be shaped in whatever way is the most amenable to achieving the goal. This indecisiveness must be eliminated, and so the final decision must be made by conscience.

## Virtue

As the elevation of the drives to reason, every virtue presupposes an action based on knowledge. We do not follow our drives as such, but with clear insight and firm values. But not every action that is based on knowledge is virtuous, only actions that have the universal existence of freedom as its goal. Any agent can easily set the arbitrary gratification of any particular drive to be their goal, which also requires knowledge to achieve. But when the drive is made to be an end in itself, the knowledge that serves this activity is obtained in bad faith and is all the more vicious the more stubbornly and consistently an agent carries out their goal. In virtue, however, the drive is determined by reason, nature is transformed into a higher, second nature that is called habit. Virtue is therefore a habit of doing good, a firmly established skill that emanates from the character of the person, a deliberate skill of determining oneself according to reason, and with the perfection of this skill, the inner disposition of the good is perfected as well.

But in order to specify how the drives are determined by reason as virtues, we must first see how they behave when reason does not determine it. As a pure drive, it has no determination in itself, but is determined only by something else, i.e. it is indeterminate, has an indeterminate determination, or in other words, it is determined only quantitatively. In this way, the drive is capable of increasing and decreasing infinitely and can be satisfied in infinitely many degrees, either too much or too little. The determination of the drive by the reason that governs it can therefore only consist in limiting off its indeterminate progression into quantitative infinity. Reason avoids the outermost limits of magnitude, the infinitely large and the infinitely small, so that every virtue is limited by two vices, the excess and deficiency of the drive. Virtue is thus the moderation of the drive, its reduction to a definite limit.

If we want to arrive at a more precise definition of virtuous skill, we must describe more accurately the mean that the virtuous person must strive for. In the conventional meaning, the mean is that which is equidistant from both extremes, and so is always one and the same. But the virtuous mean is only a mean in the relative sense. Between two and ten, six is ​​the unconditional, objective mean. But if two pounds of meat is too little to eat, and ten pounds is too much, it does not follow that six pounds is the right mean for everyone. If it is too much for one person, it may be too little for, say, an athlete. Furthermore, that extreme to which we are most naturally drawn is for us the furthest removed from the mean. Therefore, in order to find the right mean, which is not easy, we must move furthest away from this extreme. Thus, virtue is the deliberate skill that maintains the mean for us. But since the mean relates back to the drives, this implies there are as many virtues as there are drives.

### Personal Virtues

#### Temperance and Courage

The drive for pleasure appears in a twofold form, depending on whether it presents itself from the positive or the negative side. One is the pursuit of pleasure, the other the avoidance of pain, and these two sides correspond to the virtues of temperance and courage.

Temperance relates to the sensual pleasure derived from the interactive senses of touch and taste. But even with taste, the vice of intemperance, which overindulges in pleasure, it is purely concerned with the pleasantness of the sensation, irrespective of its origin. Likewise, it is not the common feeling that the immoderate seek, but the intensity of feeling that gives them a rush. The other vice, the opposite of virtue, could be called dispassion or deprivation, which ignores or abstains from pleasure altogether. In Christianity, temperance is the highest virtue, because here only the excess of the drive is considered evil. Because we strive more for sensual pleasure than we avoid it, the excess of pleasure is a much worse condition than the lack of it, hence why the other extreme of dispassion is at least free from the endlessness of craving.

The virtue that relates to fleeing pain is courage. Because we fear pain, we flee from it. The correct mean exists between the passion of fear, and its opposite, fearlessness. The most terrible of all evils, however, is death, and the noblest of the dangers into which our lives are plunged is war, so the virtue of courage relates primarily to these and similar forms of death. There are also different forms that courage can take, such as heroism, resilience, etc. The excess of fear (or lack of fearlessness) is the vice of cowardice, while recklessness falls into the opposite faults of these two states. By itself, recklessness is somewhat closer to courage because fleeing pain is more natural to us than subjecting ourselves to it. However, it is easily and very often combined with the other extreme. Reckless people are hasty and impulsive before danger, but they flee when it actually appears. Brave people, on the other hand, are the reverse, they are persistent in danger and are calm beforehand. Courageousness encompasses resilience, but not vice versa. Courage further presupposes the good as its purpose and seeks to avoid shame, since the brave person would rather die than do something shameful. While temperance still included sensual pleasure as its purpose, the pleasantness of courage is of a much more spiritual nature.

#### Generosity and Magnificence

The virtues that relate to the drive for possession no longer relate to immediate feelings and their degrees of intensity, but relate to the activity of receiving or giving possessions, an activity that becomes universal in the drive for money.

Generosity maintains the mean in the everyday expenditures of ordinary life, although it relates more to giving than to receiving, as the New Testament declares the former to be more blessed than the latter. However, giving only has this significance because of receiving, and being receptive to the gifts of others is generous. The vice of greed is an excess of receiving and a deficiency of giving. If the former predominates, it is pure greed or rapacity, but if the latter, then it is avarice or stinginess. If the spendthrift spends too much while taking too little, they eventually fall back into taking too much and spending too little, otherwise they would lack the means for excessive spending. Because spending is more dignified than taking, extravagance is somewhat closer to the mean and can be brought back to the mean more easily through experience and age, whereas avarice and stinginess tend to increase with age.

Magnificence differs from generosity in that its object does not concern the management of money in general, but rather relates to costly expenditures, especially on festive occasions. The love of magnificence thus involves expenditure on things worthy of it, but the grandeur is always proportionate to the circumstance. Thus, the mean is not merely an action, but as a relationship between the action and its object. Therefore, when evaluating this virtue, the actor, the place, the time, and the objects are all relevant. The vice of deficiency is also stinginess, the vice of excess is ostentation. The objects of this virtue are public monuments, celebrations, ceremonies, theatrical performances, etc., which were carried out by individuals in ancient times, for example, in the Athenian liturgies. The miser, even after expending large sums, spoils the beauty of the entire work abandoning it early and leaving it incomplete. They always try to spend as little as possible on it, and each time lament the expense and consider it excessive. The ostentatious person, on the other hand, will fund something excessive simply to show that they can, such as preparing an extravagant feast at a wedding just to show off their wealth.

#### Ambition and Humility

The drive for honor provides us with two virtues that have precisely the relationship between generosity and magnificence, in that one relates to personal honor, whereas the other relates to social honor.

The love of personal honor has to do with ambition or achieving things for yourself. We denote the vice of excess as overambition or lust, while the vice of deficiency we denote as underambition or sloth. But since this is only the negation of excess, it can also serve to denote the proper mean of ambition, just as the word ambitious can either imply an excess or proper mean of ambition.

The love of social honor, on the other hand, has to do with being recognized and respected by others as virtuous, whether on the basis of magnanimous or generous actions. Whoever loves social honor believes themselves worthy of great things, even if they are not actually worthy of them. Of the two vices, each possesses only one part of the aspects just mentioned. The pompous or arrogant person considers themselves worthy of greatness without actually being considered worthy, whereas the diffident person is considered worthy of greatness without considering themselves worthy. The humble person possess both elements of the relationship to the highest degree, but in terms of how they conduct themselves, they stand in the middle. The recognition and self-recognition of worthiness of the humble person are in balance. The extremes of excess and deficiency are, in the first case, the worth does not reach the appreciation, while in the second, it surpasses it. These vices, like those opposed to magnanimity, are also founded on a disproportion, but between feelings and actions on the one hand, and the objective worth of the person on the other. If the humble person considers themselves worthy of great things, they will desire them. The most valuable of good is honor, and so the magnanimous person will consider this honor to be the most valuable, followed by prestige, wealth, etc. The vice of diffidence is further from the mean than arrogance in that while the arrogant person is just ridiculous, the diffident person actively rejects recognition by making themselves appear uninteresting.

### Social Virtues

#### Kindness

The mean in relation to the passion of anger is kindness, and it is considered the mean since kindness negates anger by *neutralizing* it. Here, excess and lack pertain to the inappropriate expression and responsiveness of anger. For excess and lack, we can distinguish between a quick temper, which quickly flares up and erupts, but then easily dissipates again, from resentment, which slowly and privately builds up anger over a long time, but never lets go of it.

#### Wit, Friendliness and Honesty

Following the virtues relating to benevolence and its practice, that is, to friendship, these are called the sociable virtues, which manifest themselves in actions and speech.

The first sociable virtue, which has as its object the pleasant things that people want to do with each other, such as recreational activities and fun conversations during leisure, is wittiness. The jester takes this to an extreme by making fun out of everything and taking nothing seriously. By contrast, the boorish person takes this to the other extreme by taking fun out of everything and making nothing pleasant.

The second sociable virtue is friendliness, relating to the comforts of life that people obtain for one another in their interactions, seeking mutual advantage, the recognition of others and the enhancement of their own self-esteem. Excessive friendliness consists in approving everything about the other person, never contradicting them, and always believing that one must grant everyone praise and advantage, even if it is accompanied by the greatest inconveniences on the part of the person acting, i.e. obsequiousness. Additionally, if a self-serving intention is added to this, seeking to gain an advantage for oneself through this behavior, then this is called flattery. The opposite vice consists of being unfriendly to people, always contradicting them, starting conflicts, excessive criticism, and generally causing people frustration and pain (whether they enjoy doing so or not). This is the character of a contrarian. The right mean we can call sociability or friendliness, it is the right way of interacting with people and comes closest to friendship, the only difference being that friendship requires loyalty and commitment in a way that friendliness does not.

The third sociable virtue relates to the devotion of one person to another through word and deed. Honesty holds the mean in the drive to communicate and is the correct relationship between speech and action. Boasting, as the side of excess, ascribes to oneself advantages that one does not possess. If a profit-seeking intention is added to this, then we have charlatanry and quackery. Irony, which is the side of deficiency, conversely denies the advantages that one actually possesses. This character already presupposes a finer sense of decorum than boastfulness. When used for good purposes, irony brings others to the right mean of honesty by exposing their dishonesty. Socrates himself had a good purpose for this since he wanted to awaken in others the insight that they did not know what they thought they knew.

#### Justice

The drive for justice, in which personal and sociable ends are in perfect harmony, then forms the basis of the highest sociable virtue that is itself justice. This balance of the personal and the sociable presupposes the equality of the persons involved, and this equality is the first characteristic of justice. Justice is the virtuous disposition to treat others as equals in the satisfaction of their own drives. In this relation to the other, however, justice is not merely virtue in general, as in the skill and disposition of being good, but is the exercise of justice for the other. Since this virtue must benefit the other, it appears to be not only the good of the agent, but also the good of the one to whom the action relates. Finally, the fraternity that justice demands has its basis in the fact that it transcends the limitation of private law by relating individuals to the community and thereby to public law.

Since all legal rights and moral duties are to be exercised in civil society, justice is, first and foremost, the universal virtue that contains all other virtues, bringing the drives into harmony with reason. Justice in this sense consists in observing the laws of civil society that protect and promote the virtues of its citizens. Just as justice as such can be called legality, so too can its opposite, injustice, be called illegality, which encompasses all crimes and vices just as justice encompasses all rights and virtues. However, justice is also much more than legality, in that it not only strives to act in accordance with the law, but that this very striving arises from their own moral conviction.

The particular moments of justice, which are limited to legality, is the equality of two or more individual persons with respect to the objects of private law, applying narrowly only to property, contract, and crime.

Justice with respect to property consists in the fact that each person receives as much as they give. This form of justice is distributive justice. It is a proper ratio between the quantity and quality of goods. Its exercise consists in bringing about this proper ratio, not only for oneself, but also for another. Distributive justice requires at least four elements, two persons and two things, and determines the geometric ratio of them. If person A relates to person B, then thing C relate to thing D, so the mean is equality AD = BC. Distributive injustice consists of the distributing party either giving themselves or a favored party more than what they deserve (and implies the third party receives less than what they deserve) is indeed a twofold extreme of too much and too little. But these two extremes are not two different vices, but are only the two inseparable aspects of unjust possession.

If all goods are distributed according to the contribution, and the parties of a contract want to make an exchange, then justice lies in the equality of what is exchanged and injustice lies in the inequality of what is exchanged, that one gains from the loss of another. This form of justice is corrective justice. The task of corrective justice is to find the mean ratio between excess and deficiency in the distribution of things. Since distributive justice has already established the correct ratio, and it is now only a matter of restoring the disrupted ratio. Hence, the ratio here is not geometric, but arithmetic.

Finally, if a crime has been committed, then the task of punitive justice is to determine the correct punishment for the crime. It only remains for us to define this correct measure more precisely from a moral perspective. For it cannot now merely depend on the compensation for the external damage, as in reparative justice, but consideration must also be given to the intent from which the external deed followed, which is relevant in distributive justice. Retribution establishes the connection between distributive and reparative justice. Apart from the relationship between intent and action, Aristotle demanded an inequality of punishment depending on whether the wrongdoer has a higher or lower dignity, but this completely contradicts and defeats the purpose of the equality of all people before the law.

Having descended from general justice to particular justice, this particularity can also be conceived as something general, which must always be further defined and modified by the specific circumstances since all actions relate to the individual case. This justice of the individual case we call equity, or as the Romans say, ex æquo et bono. The law cannot take all individual circumstances into account and can only decide in a general way. Thus, the correct perspective that upholds the general in each particular case, even if it is legally contradicted, is equity, which substitutes a higher right for the lower one. Since equity corrects the drive for justice through pure reason, the drive as such has ceased to be the material basis of the virtues, and so we must consider the rational virtues of knowledge or reason itself.

### Rational Virtues

The rational virtues will differ from one another to the extent that knowledge is, on the one hand, the source of the mean established by reason in the practical drives, and on the other hand, the pure activity of cognition itself. Thus, the rational virtues are partly virtues of the understanding, and partly virtues of reason.

#### Prudence

Just as justice proved itself to be a universal virtue conceived in its exercise towards others, the excellence of having this knowledge itself, by virtue of which it finds the right mean in all practical virtues, is prudence. This is also a universal virtue, though it is conceived from the perspective of understanding as opposed to action. Prudence is a complete inference of the understanding, it establishes the principle for doing good as the major premise, it recognizes the means that are useful for carrying out this end as the minor premise, and performs the action that realizes the principle as the conclusion. Prudence is therefore a certitude of character, as described previously in the drive for knowledge, that is necessary condition for realizing any practical virtue. For what good is a virtue if the motivation and ability that puts it into practice are lacking? If we then ask what vices opposed to prudence is, Aristotle summarizes it very well as an excess and lack of certitude. The unwavering certainty of character, even for evil purposes, is clearly the vice of excess, whereas as the lack of certainty of character, even for good purposes, is the vice of deficiency. The first is arrogance, the second is naiveté, and prudence is right mean between them.

#### Judiciousness, Counsel and Insight

If we distinguish more closely the different aspects that define prudence, this gives us the particular virtues of the understanding.

The correct understanding of a good purpose is judiciousness. However, as only one aspect of prudence, it also differs essentially from prudence as whole. Judiciousness pertains to the evaluation and recognition of good purposes as opposed to their execution.

The skill of the understanding to find the right means for a good purpose is counsel. It recognizes that a means can only serve a good purpose if the means is itself good, disproving the proposition that the end justifies the means. Good counsel formids a bad means to be used for a good end just as it forbids a good means to be used for a bad end. Such person can give correct advice, but still be poorly advised.

Lastly, since in action, everything depends on the individual case and the correct judgment of the circumstances, the virtue of reason that knows the distinction of good from evil in each individual case is insight. Since all virtues converge in justice, and equity is the correct knowledge of what is just in each individual case, insight is nothing other than the correct judgment of what is equitable. Hence we can also use a related word, leniency, to indicate when a punishment should be waived for equitable reasons.

#### Wisdom

Finally, the virtue of thinking that no longer relates to the changing circumstances of human actions, but to the eternal ground of truth, is called wisdom. When Aristotle calls this virtue the most perfect, self-sufficient, and and blessed, this is justified by the fact that in it the harmony of personal and social virtues are most completely and universally established, and the standpoint of the unconditioned, where subject and object coincide, is grasped as speculative knowledge. All the drives and inclinations of practical life, everything that relates to the finite being of humankind, recedes into the background with this virtue. Its drive is the unconditioned drive for knowledge, and so this virtue relates only to the previous conditioned drives as the higher negation of them. The good is no longer a measure of desire, but struggles against desire as an obstacle to its realization. This is the standpoint of the doctrine of duty, as it is particularly developed in Stoic philosophy and Christian morality.

## Duty

Since the drive is no longer, from the standpoint of virtue in general, limited to a certain measure, but is completely negated so that the good may be accomplished, duty is essentially without content, and only becomes a system of duties when the content of the drives are introduced as a means to fulfill the duty for its own sake. But since many different means can lead to the same end, the choice of means under different circumstances and duties in casuistry comes into play here. To escape the conflict of duties, the insight acquired through training the drives toward the good must decide which duty is preferable in each case, i.e. asceticism.

### System of Duties

Initially it may not be obvious how a multiplicity of duties is possible. The multiplicity of virtues arose from the diversity of the underlying drives, and without them, the good appears abstract and simplistic. Duty for its own sake is only one, hence why the Church Fathers considered the many virtues of the ancients to be vices. The virtue of being angry at the right time has now become a vice, so if anyone wants to take your shirt, you hand over your coat as well (Matthew 5:40). Magnanimity bows before altruism, temperance gives way to abstinence, and so on. Thus, the satisfaction of drives or happiness are no longer in beautiful harmony with the highest good. The harmony lost in this life is to be restored in the next, making happiness in the next life the consequence of virtue in this life. But this idea ignores that morality itself already contains inner satisfaction as true happiness. When one turns to a specific duty that gives a general duty its content, then one is faced with nothing but a maxim for happiness, i.e. the duty gives a purpose to the drive. Duty rejects the basis for its own existence, which is the true reason for the immorality of this moral standpoint. Since duty for its own sake remains in the indeterminate generality of inactive contemplation, whereas morality necessitates action that relates to a drive, pure duty cannot do without drive. Since universal duty aims to carry out the good, the purpose of reason, all rational beings, myself and others, are all objects of duty. This is reflected in the division of duties into personal and social. 

#### Personal Duties

The personal duties, or the duties to oneself, states that, since the individual is a means to the end of reason, they should preserve themselves as such and continue to develop physically and mentally in this regard. The duty of self-preservation is therefore not different from the drive of self-preservation in content, but in its motivation. The same applies to the relationship of the other duties to their corresponding drives, so that we can be briefer here overall, because no new content is introduced. Just as the classification of personal drives is threefold, so too are three duties distinguished here: to preserve one's life, one's property, and one's honor.

Firstly, the duty to preserve one's own life follows the prohibition of suicide. Although life is a means to the end of reason, it may nevertheless be sacrificed tosave the life of another. Otherwise, to kill oneself means to destroy the purpose of reason to which the individual belongs as a participating member. One might call suicide braver than bearing the evils of this world because they are too cowardly to accept the pain of death, but this completely ignores that the person endures suffering for the sake of duty, showing that they have courage far greater than courage to face death. The negative duty not to destroy one's life corresponds to the positive duty to prolong it as much as possible through a with a good diet, exercise, temperance, etc. Secondly, the obligation to maintain and preserve one's property commands industriousness, diligence, thrift, and good management. Finally, what remains is the duty to preserve one's honor. Yet, honor rests on the worthiness of a person to be a suitable means to the moral law, and so the drive for honor becomes here the duty of self-respect. A person must respect themselves enough not to act immorally. But since the person is also aware that they need to have the drive to act morally, and that the drive is inappropriate to the moral law, the converse duty of humility arises from this fact.

Furthermore, if person is not as they should be to be a means to the end of reason, they must first make themselves suitable for this purpose, i.e. improve themselves. The nature of self-improvement varies from physical improvement, through training of the body, to intellectual improvement, through training of the mind, and finally to moral improvement, through the development of good habits in general. The activity of self-improvement as a whole becomes the epitome of happiness, which must now itself be used as a means to fulfill duty. This is the duty of preserving one's happiness, connecting the first two moments of personal duties to each other. One duty that accomplishes this is to endure happiness, so as not to make the pleasure it provides an end in itself. This is no doubt one of the most difficult duties since pleasure as such can be misleading. Moreover, the other duty that accomplishes this is patience, composure, and steadfastness in misfortune, so as not to be deterred from fulfilling one's duties. Thus, even in misfortune, a person will retains their happiness, as the contentment that flows from the awareness of fulfilled duty, and prepares one for the fulfillment of new duties.

#### Social Duties

The social duties, or duties to one's neighbor, are partly negative and partly positive, the latter of which relates either to the physical well-being of others or to their spiritual well-being.

The negative duties toward one's neighbor are duties of justice or coercion: to neither infringe upon their life, health, and freedom, nor to damage or injure their property, nor finally to harm their character through slander, lies, defamation, mockery, etc.

The positive duties towards one's neighbor are duties of love, which as positive ones, are meant to promote the well-being of the other, are called duties of conscience or imperfect duties: these include benevolence, service, truthfulness, gratitude, appreciation, sympathy, harmony and peaceability, forbearance, tolerance and reconciliation, even love of enemies, and finally courtesy, approachability, etc.

Ultimately, the social duties as a totality must only be seen as means to demonstrate the moral law to each individual. Everyone thus has the duty to promote the moral improvement of others, partly through admonition to the good, and partly by setting a good example.

#### The Moral Universe

Since everyone has the idea of moral perfection of all as their goal, the standpoint of God as the moral world order is established. As the third link in the system of duties, this is duty towards God. However, God is the object of all duties in that the creation of the moral world order is the end goal of each and every duty. There can be no special duties toward this unconditional world order, unless we were to count our own adequacy / inadequacy to the moral world order to be a duty to love / fear God. The confidence that the individual can conform to this ultimate purpose by the power of their own will is to trust the divine world order, as the belief that reason will prevail more and more over time. But since this is merely a belief, the will can be deceived in certain cases. Likewise, the fulfillment of a certain duty as a means to an end must yield to another duty when the latter proves itself to be a better means and thus renders the former immoral. The conclusion of the general system of duties is casuistry, the notion that no one duty can endure on its own, and in their mutual struggle, one eventually supercedes the other.

### Casuistry

Because the specific content of duty is added externally, it can only be determined by external reasons whose content is included for each case. Individual cases are thus presented, and since the external reasons are as manifold as the external content itself that is to be elevated to the concept of duty, the intellect has a broad scope here to elevate the specific content to the concept of duty. This is casuistry, a method first invented by the Stoics, taken to its extreme by the Jesuits and cast the latter's morality in such a detrimental light because, like the Sophists, they were enabled by their sophmoric education to present any content whatsoever as a duty. Here it becomes apparent that the negation of the drive is not taken seriously, but on the contrary, inclination and drive are precisely made the determining factor for duty. Since duties against the divine world order cannot be subject to casuistry, as it purely concerns the general and ultimate purpose of duty as such, there can only be struggles between duties to oneself, between duties to one's neighbor, and finally, the struggle between both.

#### Conflict of Personal Duties

Regarding the conflict of personal duties, self-preservation takes precedence over perfection because it is its necessary condition. Self-fulfillment, since it is the consequence of the other two duties, is secondary to and derived from them. However, since it is also a means to fulfilling duties in general, it also takes precedence. If we consider the specifics, life takes precedence over all other matters because it is their ultimate condition, with the only exception that life may be sacrificed to save another life. But to sacrifice one's own life for the sake of honor is absurd, since death does not prove honor or dishonor. Honor depends on circumstances that are accidental and typically forgoes the contemporary recognition in bringing about a better future, so the person must prefer life to honor, since it is the means to fulfill their destiny, which is to bring about this future. But when backward-looking ministers, place honor in being unpopular with their people for the sake of restoring the past, this defiance of recognition becomes ridiculous. Kant rightly wants to allow the duty of thrift to apply only when all other duties to oneself, including happiness, have been fulfilled. In the pursuit of self-improvement and happiness, the intellect can more easily decide what is right for it. 

#### Conflict of Social Duties

In general, regarding the conflict of social duties, we can say that the question of to whom I have a duty towards can be answered in very different ways. If we compare the social duties to one another, the duties of justice take precedence over the duties of love, since no right intention does not excuse wrong action, such as when Saint Crispinus stole leather to make shoes for the poor. Likewise, one must pay what they owe before giving gifts to others. The duty to one's neighbor, though it is itself subordinate to the duty of justice, nevertheless takes precedence over the duty of love. For instance, an act of charity should not be performed if one knows that the recipient has bad intentions. Charity causes the worst kind of casuistry because is often impossible to know if your donation will benefit someone or not, as well as how much you should donate.

As for the struggles within the individual duties to one's neighbor, it is unproblematic that I can trespass the property of my neighbor to save them if their life was in danger. But what about when honor and life come into conflict? Are we permitted, in the so-called "lie of necessity," to save another's life with a lie? Saint Augustine does not permit it, not even for the good of all humankind. Fichte claims that we should mislead someone who is pursuing a hidden person with drawn weapons, in order to lead them astray from the trail of murder. Fichte replies that one can indeed say, "I do not know his whereabouts," even if the pursuer's fury turns against the speaker. Socrates permits a lie tha would lead an army to victory, just as he permits stealing weapons so that a friend does not commit suicide. But with certain social duties, such as the duties of love or friendship, one wonders whether it is even necessary to lie at all. Should one tell the truth to a poet who asks for our judgment, even if knows that this will anger them to the utmost? Who, says Kant, always has the wit of an evasive answer at hand? Kant, knowing the fluctuating nature of casuistry, remains silent on the matter. But if one considers that one telling the truth would dissuade the poet from writing terrible poetry, then being honest about what you think would actually to the poet's benefit and so it would not even be necessary to lie.

#### Conflict of Personal and Social Duties

When personal duties and social duties collide, we must first decide, assuming equality of the object, what duty takes precedence for the person. If two people cling to a plank after a shipwreck, and the plank can only support one, it cannot be decided whether the duty to oneself or to one's neighbor takes precedence because both shipwrecked people will perish in either case. To answer this question is to measure the life of each person as worth more or less than another's, which is itself an arbitrary and selfish judgment on part of the measurer. There is always the option to refuse to take action and leave the outcome to chance, even if it contradicts the proposition that the moral law must command something at every moment. Of course, it can be argued that the moral law in this case commands you to do nothing (or that it forbids you from action). One's own and another's perfection can never be in conflict, and both explicitly coincide in the duty to set a good example. With regard to various duties to oneself and to one's neighbor, the duty of self-fulfillment will undoubtedly take a back seat to the duties of justice. For instance, it is not permissible to enjoy a pleasure that inflicts injustice on others. Thus, only three kinds of collisions remain: first, between self-preservation and justice, second, between self-preservation and love, and third, between self-fulfillment and love.

We have already described two cases in which the conflict between self-preservation and justice was decided in favor of the former, partly in self-defense to preserve my own life, and partly in theft during an emergency. Furthermore, there is also the case in the beneficium competentiæ, where the creditor must leave their property to the debtor in order to secure latter's livelihood. The Jesuits even go so far as to declare the life of another person inviolable if they are protecting their property. Conflict between persons brings not only one's honor and life into conflict, but also the honor and life of another. Here it must be noted that the honor of the insulted party is not completely restored by taking revenge on the insulter. The action restores outward honor, or honor in the eyes of others, but not inward honor. With this, casuistry is once again granted considerable scope. If someone attacks my honor, the Jesuits permit going as far as lying and slander. Fichte only allows the slander of the other to be exposed, even at the expense of their honor.

In the struggle between self-preservation and love, it is clear that depriving oneself of necessities is not a necessary condition of doing good for others. But if one can only give from the surplus of what one already has, then what qualifies as a surplus? The indeterminacy of this concept has been exploited by casuists to almost completely banish the duty of giving. Kant presents a more playful collision between self-esteem and courtesy in that traditional gestures and phrases of politeness can seem like dishonest groveling. However, he notes, that this can become serious, as "he who makes himself a worm cannot complain afterwards if people step on him."

When self-fulfillment and love finally come into conflict, the former seems to take a back seat, since we even sacrifice our own property for the well-being of the people we love. But here the antinomy is that no one can partake in pleasure as long as there is another human being in pain, whichever is the case. If the rich only satisfied their necessary needs, the poor could live in abundance from their surplus. It is also argued that the advantage self-fulfillment has over love is that the peace of our conscience cannot be sacrificed for the well-being of others. But since the peace of conscience belongs self-fulfillment, it would be very wrong to eliminate this element from self-fulfillment. This case study shows that reason is not always able to give a definite answer about what must be done and what must be omitted. Since the specifics of obligatory action are also subject to reasoning, they must be traced back to their source and grounded in virtue as the habit of goodness. Only someone who has acquired virtue through habit will also be certain of it in their actions. This habituation of virtue is asceticism.

### Asceticism

The cultivation of good habits are only a means to achieve the fulfillment of duty as such. By making a habit of doing something good, it becomes part of the person's character, allowing the good to arise as a habit of its own accord. The purpose of asceticism is to make morality a habit so that the right decision can be made even without reflection, just like any other mechanical habit. To attain this habit, the individual must first know themselves, identify their weaknesses and inclinations, so that they know where they are lacking. From this, the individual must secondly eliminate and negate bad impulses and inclinations through practice in order to prepare themselves for dutiful action. Thirdly, from the preparatory rejection of sensuality arises the knowledge of the good and its expression to others.

#### Self-Knowledge

Even the ancients elevated self-knowledge to a primary rule of life in the maxim *γνῶθι σαυτόν zu* - "know thyself." Just as careful observation of one's own body largely serves to diagnose it, so is self-knowledge, to an even greater degree, a diagnostics for the spirit. To determine what desires I must guard against most vigilantly, I must closely examine the ones are most strongly ingrained in my nature. For since, in the realm of duty, a drive is not an end in itself but is only a means to an end, I must refuse to allow that specific drive, the very one bestowed upon me by nature, to assert itself for its own sake. Through our deeds, the judgments others pass upon us, and comparing our actions to theirs, our self-knowledge is objectively enriched, whereas Rosenkranz rightly designates the former mode of self-knowledge as *subjective*. Thus, through these two modes of self-examination, vigilance and reflection, a clearer picture of our character will emerge. We uncover the methods and maxims by which we are accustomed to acting in order to discover what we are like. Rosenkranz terms this "unconditional self-knowledge." This comprehensive self-knowledge constitutes the surest path to self-improvement, for the first step toward betterment, as the proverb tells us, is to recognize one's own faults.

#### Practice of the Good

However, we must subsequently act in accordance with this insight, specifically by suppressing the very drives we are accustomed to making the principles of our conduct. In general, we must impose upon ourselves certain deprivations in order to habituate ourselves not to elevate our drives to the status of ends, but to master them as means to our ends. The Stoics expressed this renunciation of sensuality in the formula *ἀνέχου καὶ ἀπέχου* (sustain and abstain), and in the Indian religions, just as in the Christian Middle Ages, this practical asceticism was carried to its utmost extreme. One renounced every pleasure and every sense of self in order to give expression, within oneself, solely to the Divine, the Eternal, and the Moral. By completely withdrawing from all earthly affairs, the bonzes, monks, and hermits acquired the reputation of sanctity, transforming themselves into dwelling places for the spirit. From this standpoint, reason and sensuality stand in an inverse relationship, although the extreme practicesof ascetics, such as spending one’s entire life atop a pillar, sleeping while standing, and so forth, may dull reason no less than they dull the senses.

#### Knowledge of the Good

In truth, however, the negativity of asceticism inevitably turns positive. For whoever becomes a master of their sensuality attracts the admiration of the world, and whoever lives in constant penance will find the commandment of duty ever clearer and more distinct before their soul. Even theoretically, they will have gain this knowledge for themselves in a purer form and so will have attained true self-knowledge, the unconditional Idea of ​​the Good, that now constitutes their innermost being. This is the standpoint that Schleiermacher adopts in his "Monologues," and on which he claims to have attained the highest morality, directly articulating moral duties without being hindered by the collisions of casuistry. To share this knowledge with others is the highest gift that a person can offer. Those who interact in this way form a society of morally striving individuals, fulfilling the Stoic demand for a paraenetic ethics by morally instructing and strengthening one another through sharing their moral experiences and discoveries. The fruit of this association is that everyone finally recognizes the moral law so completely as the inner essence of themselves that they relate to it as to the eternal personality of the spirit, and perpetally finds in it the unwavering basis for their own dutiful deeds. The Idea of ​​the Good is now so intertwined with the personality that it becomes the drive to do good in general, i.e. conscience.

## Conscience

Conscience is duty that has become a virtue, skill, and habit, where inclination and custom are no longer excluded from the Idea of ​​the Good, and so can be expressed as the satisfaction of the drive to do good. In conscience, morality has reached its highest stage of development. The infinite subjectivity of conscience completely derives the objective content of the moral purpose from within itself as its own good conscience. However, since the infinite subjectivity of conscience is groundless, it has the freedom to decide against the good, asserting their own good to be greater than the good itself, which is the true origin of evil. Finally, the irrationality of evil destroys itself, taking its opposition to the good with it, and the natural drives become willing instruments of the primal drive to do good, which is the standpoint of ethicality.

### Good Conscience

Because conscience appears as the drive to be conscientious, it also possesses the quantitative nature of virtue. The mean of this drive is conscientiousness, whereas the extreme ends of insufficient conscience either appears as scrupulousness or unscrupulousness. The mean of conscience must, however, lead to dutiful action, while the extremes either act contrary to duty out of recklessness or fail to act out of embarrassment. Good conscience creates the entire content of duty from within itself, resolving the division between different duties. Conscience does not reflect like the casuist, nor does it remain in contemplation like the ascetic, but in the midst of its actions, the unconditional independence and certainty of its content shines through.

Conscience knows itself to be the master over every duty-based content and accepts the law because it knows that its content cannot bind it. Jacobi took this standpoint of Socratic insight, elevated above the law, to its highest point when he said that he would pluck ears of grain on the Sabbath, even if only because he was hungry. Jacobi calls this most sacred certainty that man possesses within himself the right of pardon for all crimes against the letter of pure law, the true right of majesty of man and the seal of his divine nature. Christianity is quite right to call conscience, as the inner court of reason, the voice of God in man.

However, the unconditionality conscience turns into its opposite. For on the one hand, the voice of reason presupposes an unconditional objectivity, and on the other hand, conscience has to draw all its content from its own subjectivity, so the two sides come into conflict. Since the subject's content consists solely of drives and inclinations, good conscience fears that this content does not correspond to the voice of reason, and so it becomes a wavering conscience. But because this uncertainty is the very opposite of conscience, it must move to the unwaveringness of action, thereby exposing itself to the danger of doing something wrong. Finally, by acting with the awareness of the possibility of error, it cannot escape its own self-division, i.e. the conflict that transforms its unconditional self-certainty to self-doubt, leading conscience to the uncertainty that it is good or evil. Conscience questions whether its arbitrariness places its own good above the universal principle of the good. But since the decision based on one's own good is precisely the principle of conscience, this formal subjectivity of conscience is always on the verge of being evil.

### Evil

Since the fulfillment of moral freedom requires the unconditional power of self-determination, which it the power to actualize any possibility it chooses, the origin of evil begins here. For freedom to become self-certainty, it must have explored all possibilities, even if only, as Scripture praises Christ, through inner temptation. Therefore, evil does not have an independent starting point from the good, as the Zoroastrians and Manicheans claim, but is rooted in the self-determination of good, whose inverted aspects are evil itself. These aspects are: firstly, the unconditional self-determination of subjective reason, and secondly, the inherent objectivity of universal reason. If we separate the objective content of reason from the subject's self-determination, the former becomes the natural drive and the latter becomes formal arbitrariness. For the agreement of both sides to arise as the victory of the good against evil, their struggle and conflict must precede it. The stages of this development are as follows:

#### Origin of Evil

Before the will makes a decision from within itself, contrary to the objective content of reason, it lives in the immediacy of the natural drive. For virtue, the natural drives are good, but for duty, the natural drives are evil. But the natural drives are neither good nor evil by themselves, so the human being originally lives in a state of innocence that is unconsciously in simple accordance with nature, which must be abandoned precisely because its being is completely dependent on nature, just as the literal etymology of the word paradise is a walled enclosure. The only state that is appropriate to human beings is self-conscious reason, so the standpoint of pure naturalness must be abandoned. Because the self-determination of reason is first and foremost good for human beings, the natural determination of the drives is evil for them. There is always the possibility of being evil if the natural drive as such is seized with formal arbitrariness. In biblical terms, this means that human beings, although innocent by nature, are nevertheless born with original sin.

Because man is supposed to be a realized instance of the self-determination of reason, he goes beyond the naturalness of being determined by instincts, thus seizing arbitrarily this or that instinct instead of being guided by the constraints of nature. In this way, he leaves the state of innocence, where the instinct is given, and follows his arbitrary impulses, which derive their content from his particular subjectivity. The external aspect of the natural drive in itself is internalized and becomes formal self-determination. Thus, not only does the natural drive become evil because it is no longer pure natural, but arbitrariness becomes evil as well because it has been severed from the reality of nature. This is the true meaning of the Fall. It is a giant leap for mankind, as Schiller says, because man has progressed from a natural being determined by nature to a spiritual being that determines itself. But since the content of what man is destined for is dependent on his will, he now perceives the incompatibility between what he wants to will and his current state. He recognizes good and evil, and has indeed attained the likeness of God in doing so, but also the awareness of his own sinfulness.

With the knowledge of good and evil, however, the possibility of return is also established. Man is evil both in himself and through his reflection when he willingly opposes the primal drive to do good. But man is neither intrinsically nor reflectively evil. On the contrary, it is because man reflects on the difference between good and evil that the very act of reflection is the beginning of justification. Whoever recognizes that they have done wrong is already on the path to improvement, and the repentant conscience is the first step towards redemption. The same elements of self-determination and natural drive in evil are also in the good. However, self-determination and the natural drive are in such agreement in the good that reason appears as the identity of both, in that reason is naturally driven to determines itself as the being of being. Conversely, in evil, formal self-determination is estranged from reality and the natural drive is servile to nature, bringing both into conflict with reason. However, the return of the repentant conscience from this conflict creates a new conflict.

#### Justification of Evil

The return to objective, intrinsic reason is from a moral standpoint is also a return to the intrinsic nature of the natural drive. This means that every action, even if it is good on the one hand, is also an evil action on the other — good and evil are inextricably linked. Because every action, no matter how good, also satisfies a drive or an interest, the action is also contrary to duty. If duty commands one to serve one's friends, then the inclination and interest that I feel in doing so appears precisely as a selfish act. I act while fulfilling a duty with the awareness that I am not virtuous, but evil. In short, I act with a guilty conscience. Conversely, since the concept of an evil action includes the requirement that it be done with a guilty conscience, the Jesuits sought to deduce the non-evil nature of the action from the absence of a guilty conscience in their morality, i.e. an action can only be considered evil if it was undertaken with full awareness of its unlawfulness.

Since the selfish interest that appears even in a dutiful action is only apparent to the actor, who alone knows their own intention, the action appears as one that seems good to others, but is undertaken with a guilty conscience for the actor. In other words, the moral action carried out is hypocrisy, the appearance of good for others while the actor themselves has lost the sanctity of conscience. This hypocrisy is not merely an accidental form of evil, but the necessary result of the contradictions of the moral standpoint, since the actor always carries the bad conscience of their own self-interest with them.

Since the others for whom the action is intended have had the same experience, what is presented to them as a good is also for them a conscious evil. Every action is evil both for themselves and others. The two sides that were distributed between two persons in hypocrisy are now united. Everyone now acts with a bad conscience to the extent that they are also deceiving themselves with the appearance of good. This is probabilism, one of the cornerstones of Jesuit morality, as Pascal calls it. Various reasons can be given for any action, such as selfish, benevolence, and the like. The point of probabilism is to derive every action, for the actor and for others, from a reason that has a probability of being good. The Jesuits were very flexible in this regard and considered the slightest probable reason, e.g. the reputation of a Jesuit writer, sufficient to appease conscience. The first teacher of probabilism, however, was the serpent in Paradise, which cited the fruit of knowledge and godliness as the reason for human disobedience — a reason that even God acknowledged to be valid when He says: “Behold, Adam was born, as one of us, knowing good and evil.” Even the less probable reason sufficed for the Jesuits' acquittal, and thus the new mode of salvation, which Pascal alludes to, has taken a wider turn. Of the evil act, nothing but its good side remains and has been elevated to the whole.

#### Self-Destruction of Evil

With this, the reversal of evil into good is initiated. For conscience, which alone decides from this standpoint, can find any reason to make an action good. This reversal, however, is at the same time the highest point of evil, and is the subjectivity that dissolves all content.

The second principle of the Jesuits, cited by Pascal, presents itself here first: the direction of the intention with which they complete their redemption. Of the many aspects that appears in an action, one aspect is singled out as the aspect that makes the action good. One has good intent because their action proves, if one only focuses on this good aspect and pushes the others into the background. Thus, every bad action can be justified: the struggle with the duty to uphold one's honor, abandoning one's duty to protect another, etc. The concern for our lives is the positive aspect, especially when one has a wife and children to feed at home. Such positive content is considered an ultimate end to which the means are subordinate. For the sake of a good end, even a bad means become good if it is connected with the direction of the intention.

Secondly, however, one can ask why so much of a fuss is made over finding the right justification for an action. Even Fichte could not overcome this trend by saying: “Do what your conviction tells you is your duty.” The next consequence of this is that if my conviction is false, then I have made a mistake, not a wrong. But we have already quoted Aristotle that a false understanding does not excuse the action, but is the reason why the action is wrong. To act from conviction, there is now the added awareness of the inescapable necessity of falsehood. According to Kantian philosophy, if the implicit essence of things and thus also of the good is unknowable, then we act from conviction with the awareness that our conviction could mean nothing. By contrast, Fichte demands the knowledge of the objective end purpose in order to escape the purely subjective standpoint of conviction.

In this way, evil culminates in the subjectivity that knows itself to be the highest, which acts even though it knows that the principle of its action is false, is the standpoint of irony as it appears in Fichte's philosophy. While the irony of Socrates degraded the person, it was Schlegel, in his Lucinde, who placed the ironic self above all content, unable to realize the good, yet acts in order to know itself in the foaming waves of its deeds, and is this constantly changing Proteus, who in all action enjoys only themselves. Convinced of the futility of their actions, the self then comes to the conclusion that it can only assure itself of its goodness in the sacred silence of genuine passivity and inaction, just as one does in politics by remaining idle and leaving the future of history to the world spirit. A finer form of this inaction is the beautiful soul, which Goethe so excellently describes in his Wilhelm Meister. It is the self-satisfaction with one's moral worth that everyone, as in Schleiermacher's monologues, expresses against themselves and others, seeing in this assurance of their conscientiousness and good intentions the fulfillment of the moral end itself. That the completeness of the good in the beautiful soul is not lost, even though the ego devotes itself to a single drive, is the standpoint of ethicality, the true overcoming and reversal of the evil into goodness.

### Ethicality

The reconciliation of legality with the blossoming of moral life is truly achieved by ethicality. While the beautiful soul still considers self-determination impure, ethicality accepts the objective power of the ethical relationships it has with others without relinquishing its own subjectivity. It is now irrelevant that morality and ethicality mean the same thing. We take ethicality to be morality filled with objective content, reflection that is intrinsic to substance. In ethicality, the Idea of ​​the Good, as the highest pinnacle of subjective freedom, has been united with the objective freedom of right. So in ethicality, we will now have to distinguish its three aspects of objective, subjective, and absolute freedom. The first aspect is ethical relationship, the second aspect is ethical self-consciousness, and the third aspect is custom.

#### Ethical Relationships

To the extent that man, through the satisfaction of his drives, knows his freedom to exist in and for itself, good is now identical to right. The freedom in which I know myself to be free is not an external one, as in the case of property, but the real connection I have with other persons, i.e. my partnership with them. The system of drives appears as a circle of ethical powers, and the duty that binds the individual appears as something universal that transcends the individual's personality, which is at the same time the highest right and existence of the individual's freedom. This brings us to the standpoint of the Greek epigram in which man is addressed thus: “What you desire, you turn into a god.” It is, however, the desire for an ethic in which right and duty are inseparable. This moralization consists in the fact that the actor is no longer the absolute master over the content of freedom, but is a member of a whole whose freedom is their own. When we describe these relationships, we can also call them duties, like the system of duties described in morality. While the latter are general human duties, those to be described now are the specific duties of family members, parents and children, teachers and students, leader and follower, etc. Schleiermacher rightly calls these relationships (of marriage, country, art, church, and science) goods since only they, as objective goals of the spirit, are truly good. But since they are also subjective drives, the pathos of individuals, we must consider them from this perspective as well.

#### Ethical Self-Consciousness

Ethical self-consciousness is the true ethical conscience, possessing the form of certainty that is identical with its objective content, whereas previously we described a conscience that was ignorant of evil. Following ethical duties in no way infringes upon our freedom, for they are already inherent to our way of life. What is curtailed is the impulsive gratification of natural drives, as well as the external reflection that assigns duties to a person for arbitrary reasons. Nor can ethical self-consciousness be purely designated a virtue, for even in the latter, the appropriate measure of the natural drive is determined subjectively. The perfectly free disposition that derives the content of the good and right from the concrete circumstances at hand is *righteousness*, the virtue of the person who does good in their actual relationships with others, and is a living example of this virtue.

#### Custom

The harmony between the subjective and the objective aspects of ethicality is *custom*, where good and right actions have become habitual in a society. The particular actions of all its members appear as the total activity of the national spirit, which expresses itself vividly within them and through their own freedom. To educate a human being ethically, as an ancient philosopher rightly observed, one must make them a citizen of a good commonwealth. The individual then acts merely as the representative of the general will. In times of moral corruption, during the dissolution of political life and of the emergence of a new spirit, the existing order is no longer good and the custom has vanished. In such times, those of Socrates and the Roman Emperors, or indeed our own, the *moral* standpoint necessarily takes the place of the *ethical* one. Man must seek the good within his own insight, for it has retreated from the existing social conditions. Hence the criticism Aristotle leveled against Socrates: that he abolished custom and natural inclination (*tò páthos kaì tò êthos*). Yet in such times, one can no longer simply follow the prevailing inclinations, habits, or customs. With the rupture in reality, the good takes refuge in the realm of conscience. However, because this conscience contains within itself no objective criterion for its decision, we witness the appearance of partisan strife characterized by the most monstrous bitterness, a bitterness whose very essence lies in the fact that the concepts of right, duty, and the good are mixed together, with opposing factions interpreting them in mutually contradictory ways, while each accuses the other of dishonesty. 

However, this historical development cannot be anticipated here. At this moment, we are purely concerned with the good as it immediately presents itself to the individual in the form of custom. However, the legal person has become the moral subject, and this unity of the moral subject with the legal person is precisely the ethical human being. This ethical person, while remaining an individual, constitutes the highest manifestation and consummation of individuality, is the family, the third and highest standpoint of private law.
