# Mathematics

As the science of pure magnitude, mathematics has no sensory content for its objects because they are not yet self-existent or individual. As a formal science, mathematics deals only with the most abstract of entities which it treats as independent objects, concerning itself only with the general ideas of shapes, numbers, equations, etc. It stands midway between the pure thought of Logic and the sensory being of Nature, so it is the bridge between both areas. However, only the general principles of mathematics are also the starting points of nature. For mathematics is applicable to nature, but only to certain parts of it, namely the areas in which quantity predominates. 

The idea of externality in its simple, immediate, and unrestricted form is, first of all, space, the subject matter of which is geometry. The second is the beginning of the idealization of
space, the first negation and dissolution of the spatial separation is time, in its original form, i.e. as number, is the object of algebra. Third, the unity of space and time will reveal itself to be motion, the calculus of rates and changes.

## Space (Geometry)

Space is the substantial carrier that underlies all beings, dissolving again into nothing but non-existent accidents, since every here is indistinct from every other here. Space is the identity of the Absolute, which differentiates and interprets itself in nothing but mere relativities. It is also the most abstract universal of Nature, which includes no real particulars. But all of this taken together, space is in the form of constant coming outside of itself, as the complete indifference of its moments toward one another. But because space is nothing but this complete disintegration in its unclouded uniformity, it is the concept of nature in its primary simplicity itself, and nothing other than this concept, namely, the Idea in the most original form of otherness. Therefore, it is the beginning of nature, as its most abstract category. The Idea cannot endure a greater disintegration and multiplication than space, nor can it endure a greater contradiction.

In the concept of space, the moments of its externality still exist as an undivided unity, and with no real distinction of continuity and discreteness, each of these moments now brings about
the determinations of space. First, continuity overflows all boundaries of discreteness by continuously expanding in all directions, into infinity, to ever-changing instances, the dimensions of space, which, given the complete indifference of directions, still allow their differences to dissolve into identity. Second, the discreteness implicit in continuity introduces the essential differences of space, as its opposites, into these indifferent differences of continuity, which are the formative elements of space. The third is likewise the totality of spatial determinations as the return of the formative elements to the dimensions, and only from this unification of the first two moments do real formations arise as geometric shapes.

The scientific study of these manifold shapes, their nature, their properties, and quantities, is now the task of geometry, which is therefore called the science of continuously extended magnitudes,
the science which has the measure of extension as its object. However, the specific theories of geometry do not properly belong to philosophy because they do not develop their objects dialectically from one another, but rather by way of introducing specific axioms that allow for the construction of certain kinds of spaces. The identity striven for by geometry is one in which the differences remain external and indifferent to one another as identifiable quantities. Therefore, the conclusion of quantity, which presupposes the abstract equality of all terms, is the one that geometry uses exclusively. Even if in this science the individual forms, also attain qualitative differences, they nevertheless remain quantitative qualities at the same time. The dimensions, elements of space, shapes, etc. are qualitative differences within quantity itself, which on the one hand arise from one another on the ladder of quantitative identity and on the other hand compared and traced back to one another. Thus in measuring, their qualitative differences always return to a quantitative expression. The ever-greater identification of forms and their return to purely quantitative differences is precisely the goal of geometry; and thus, in this science, the idea of space has reached its completion.

This return of space of all its moments back to the point from which it began is time as a point in time. Because the negation of spatial boundary is now actual, time is not the calm, positive coexistence of the mutually external ideal points, but rather the constant, restless negation of them as concretely existing; it is the incessant positing and negating of their successive points. Succession has replaced juxtaposition, and thus the first step towards the abolition of the externality of nature, of spatial separateness, has been taken. In contrast to space, in which continuity triumphed over discreteness, time is now the rule of discreteness, but as a constantly continuous one.


## Time

While for space, what is there vanishes into another there outside of it, time is nothing more than the vanishing of thereness. Every moment negatively posits what is before and after itself, but because it is only moment. Thus the here becomes the now, a moment so brief that ceases to be and is replaced by another moment in the very moment that it comes to be. But just as each now replaces the other, so it also produces the same namely, through its own annihilation. There can be no first now that is not begotten by its predecessor just as there can be no last now that is not destroyed by its successor. For if a moment in time were the first, there would have been no time before it; which would be absurd, since the before is precisely time. As such, the dimensions of time are nothing other than the unfolding of the moments contained in its concept.

If time, as the absolute negation of spatial coexistence, in which no now can exist alongside the other, then the now must always strive to generate the coexistent because otherwise time would have nothing to negate. Time thus always goes out of itself in order to return to itself: it destroys what was created and creates what will be destroyed. Thus it is arbitrary whether one takes the being or the non-being of the now as the starting point, either process implies the totality of time in its own way. 

The existing now that passes away becomes the past and the non-existent now that arises from passing away becomes the future. Future and past are therefore both becomings but also opposite moments of it. The future is a being that has not yet come to be and the past a being that has ceased to be. The non-being into which the past sinks is the same non-being from which the future arises and the being that passes away in the past is the same being that arises in the future. This being which stands between the no-longer-being of the past and the not-yet-being of the future, is the present. Though it does not merely stand in the middle, rather it constitutes the very boundary by which the past and future are distinguished. The same indivisible moment in which the past becomes future is the present itself, as the moment in which the being of the past, which turns into non-being, is simultaneously the non-being of the future, which turns into being. Every determinate now that is negated is, in the following moment, another determinate now, which is entirely the same as the one preceding it.

## Motion

That the dialectic of space has established the exclusivity of time in it, and the dialectic of time has established the indifference of space in it, and that both are thus linked to one another, is proven by the identity of geometry and algebra. This unity of the spatial with the temporal only acquires its reality nature in motion. Since time has become spatial and space has become temporal, the unification of both sides is itself a movement from one to the other. For either space determines time in-itself as position, or time determines space in-itself as speed. and finally, in addition to this, both are immanently negated as a third category, which can only be designated as their true unity. This gives three natural categories that appear in motion: position, speed, and mass.

### Position (Indeterminate Motion)

If time has become spatial, so that the exclusive discreteness that we recognized in time is posited in the resting continuity of space, then we have position. Such a space is no longer general, absolute, or abstract space, which for this very reason possessed uninterrupted continuity. Rather, space is now to be thought of as discrete, absolutely separate from other spaces. It is the enclosing boundary of a body. This does not refer to space in general, but to position, to this space, to the space of a body. Even if the position identical with the surface of the body also excludes every other position from itself, it nevertheless does not have the consuming exclusivity of time, as a negating continuity, in itself. Rather, it is the positive continuity of continuing its boundary immediately outward into the boundary of another, equally existing, stereometric body. And in the same way, it is internally entirely continuous, in that it does not, like time, discretely bring a single now to power as a present, to the exclusion of all others. Rather, the particular here of position is in itself an infinity of continuous heres. Thus, position has predominantly retained the nature of space; and it seems to grant time little scope. The most that time can do to it seems to be that, because of its discretion, it is measured by number, as frozen time. 

Position is still only a one-sided unity of space and time because to be in a specific point in space is to also be in specific point in time. What this conclusion shows, however, is that the definition of position is its own limitation. A position can only relate to another position by being its own limit. Since limitation has negation within itself, the negation of this position is only another position. The change of position the change of place, or movement. Thus,
time, in its most authentic form, has broken out again at the place. The existing place becomes non-existent, and the non-existent becomes existent. Origin and decay are transferred from
time to space; and as time flows, so now place has begun to flow. If in place, time had become spatial, now in speed, space has become temporal. This is the pure concept of speed, in which we again abstract from the moving body, just as we previously thought of space and time as detached from their fulfillment. 

## Speed (Determinate Motion)

As units of time have elapsed, units of space have been traversed. The concept of this movement contains its infinity, just as that of space and time flowed from their concepts. There is no reason why now and here should ever cease to merge into one another. Motion must therefore be elevated to the principle of nature. In fact, however, we must assert that, because the dialectic of space and time is absolutely eternal, nothing in the universe rests and stands still, but everything remains in eternal motion, as Heraclitus very correctly argued. It is therefore also a nonsensical claim on Zeno’s part when he denied movement because it contained a contradiction. He believed that if the body were here now and then there, it would first be at rest here and then there, so motion in this description would be no more than a sum of rest frames, i.e. the content of motion is motionless. But the proof that Zeno provides presupposes that there exists an indivisible unit of space and time. If a body were to remain at rest at any point in space, no matter how small, it would still not be in motion. Indeed, if this were true, motion would be impossible and Zeno would be right. For if the moving entity were to rest first at the first point in space and then at the second, a third instant would be required between these two, during which it would move in the space between them from one point to the other. This middle third point would therefore also be distant from the first: they would both have a fourth point between them, which would first have to be passed; and so on to infinity and so the entity that is in motion would never arrive to its destination. The answer to this is that, just as in the incessant flow of time every instant passes while it arises, so too does motion not occur instantaneously without transition. In the restless restlessness of continuous transition, the moving entity is therefore, rather, at the same moment in this point in space, and no longer in it, but has already passed into another. It does not rest in a single point, since rest is being, but being, as becoming, transforms into non-being at every moment. But if the moving entity is in both points in space at the same moment, then two points must be one; which is quite possible if there are no atoms, but only continuous points. Then it could also not be in these two simultaneously, as in one atom, and so on ad infinitum, it therefore only ever becomes in all according to the measure of time, which like itself, is constantly progressing. It is wrong to deny that motion exists because its definition ends in contradiction, rather, the fact that motion contains this contradiction is proof that it is conditioned by a higher concept. This contradiction can only find its resolution in mass, precisely because mass is the very transition from motion to rest.

### Matter (Self-Determined Motion)

The contradiction of motion was contained in that in which the contradiction of becoming also consists. Just as in becoming, being transforms into nothing, but these mutually contradicting things continually generate themselves from the unity into which they merge, so it is also with motion. Every here of space becomes a now of time; and notwithstanding that space and time have thereby become one, they also always, as mutually contradicting things, transform into one another.But if being and nothing, space and time, something and other, eternally transform into one another, then they are also eternally transformed into one another. This transition from the restlessness of becoming to the rest of existence constitutes the concept of matter, and with it, the solution to the contradiction we encountered in motion is accomplished. For since every here, as now, extends into another here, but has also already passed into it, it no longer passes over as into another. Rather, since one here is entirely the same as the other, each is with itself in the other: not alongside the other, for otherwise there would be an intermediate space. Since each here peacefully occupies its own place in every now, while simultaneously intervening in that of the other, without any distinction of their content that is not yet present here, they form a calm unity of continuity, no longer the continuous restlessness of movement. Just as the rings of an unbroken chain are closely connected to one another, without their oneness lacking the separateness; so the one here disperses into infinite multiplicity, and the many heres that have dispersed are all one. The reality that the spatial point still lacked was gained by the material points by establishing the discreteness of time in their continuity. If the moments of matter—space, time, and their transition, motion—are eternal, then matter, as their totality, must all the more be unoriginated. And it has been called, primarily, the primum existens because only in it, as totality, do these ideal moments attain reality. If we think of matter as permanent, then it is to be understood as spatial: when it changes, it appears to be temporal. But since it is both spatial and temporal, in its rest it is also movement, but in such a way that now even these opposites no longer contradict each other. But if space and time, as the moments of matter, first find their support and reassurance in matter, as its fulfillment, then, in reality, this only means that matter, as its truth, is the first thing in which these abstractions are founded. The transition from ideality to reality is therefore only the coalescence of the moments into a totality.

Each here of matter is discretely distinct from all other heres, just as each now of time excludes all other nows from itself. And yet, each here relates to the other so strongly that, far from negating it, it continues positively into it as part its own being. This is the composition of matter, the spatial identity of space and time, the continuity of continuity and discreteness. The points of space lying continuously next to one another are not yet composed because they still indifferently fall apart from one another, but despite their separation, they are nevertheless one. They are not atoms separated from one another by a void, but rather the motion of substance that is curved and compressed into a compactified form. Likewise, each point of matter maintains itself discretely in its being and does not allow the being of the other to arise within itself. This is the impermeability of matter, in which the restless discretion of time asserts itself in the stability of the continuous here. Position is not impermeable, it admits every other position into itself, whereas the material point does not. The coherent unity of all material points therefore bears within itself the constant striving for each point to be a one existing for itself, thus excluding all other points from itself and so each is absolutely identical with the whole. 

In matter, however, we have so far seen this striving only in itself, thus only in the form of rest, being, space, continuity. We have therefore not yet considered matter correctly, not yet comprehensively, but only one-sidedly. The unity of space and time, however, must not be conceived merely as a dead rest, but must also be understood in the form of time and discretion as movement. The moments of matter are therefore not merely inanimate qualities, such as composition and impermeability, they must also take on the form of activity, and thus repel one another. For the contradiction resolved into the rest of existence breaks out again in it, because the moments of opposition remain preserved therein, only to be resolved once more.
