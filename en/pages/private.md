# Private Law

Note: This webpage is a direct translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Natural Law, Vol. I* (1866)

## Legality



### Property

Property is derived from possession as its highest manifestation. If from a psychological standpoint, possession is the end goal because it concerns the satisfaction of drives, then from a legal standpoint, possession as the physical holding of someobject is only the means for the free will, whether it can satisfy my drives or not. This is the negative character of property law. It is only a permission to use one's property, as well as a prohibition against violating that of others, not a command or a duty to use one's property and to increase that of others. For it is only concerned with the individual's freedom at any given moment, and only in public law, in the mutual guarantee of all for all, do affirmative commands and duties arise.

When an individual places their will in a previously ownerless object, it thereby acquires a completely different character. Instead of being a dead, unfree object of nature that any person could subject to their will, it becomes an extension of their personality and is thus commensurate to the respect for persons. The person now extends as far as the objects that are in their possession. Private law thus begins with this mechanical, separate position of individual persons in relation to one another, who are separated from each other as indivisible centers and draw a larger or smaller circle around themselves, whose outermost boundaries meet without intersecting each other. The person residing in the center transfers their inviolability to the objects that they own, constituting the entire sphere of their existence. The inviolability of the object thus applies only to others and not to the owner themselves.

Regarding the specifics of property law, we can still find the philosophical determinations everywhere within the material of experience and simplify them. This is also the art of the legislator within positive law, as they have to reduce the infinite mass of individual laws and customs to general principles. The different aspects that we have to distinguish in property are only to be found in the different relationships that the person has to the object. The first relationship is the affirmative, tranquil relationship, the appearance of the person in the object such that the object is subject to the will without being truly inseparable from it. This is the origination of property in acquisition or taking possession. Secondly, the will realizes its purpose in the object, abolishes the determinacy of the object as such, and uses it to fulfill the purpose, after which the object is consumed and negated. Use is the negative relationship of the person to the object. Thirdly, the will annuls the negative relationship between itself and the object once the object has served its purpose. This is the relinquishment of property, the restoration of the object from its negation in use. Relinquishment is the opposite of the initial acquisition of property, the cessation of property, whereas use is the middle ground, the duration of property. However, every acquisition is also an act of ownership. The kinds of property acquisition that we have to consider here, however, are the original ones, which take place without the help of another person. 

#### Acquisition 

In the kinds of acquisition, the person first makes themselves physically present in the object, using their body, in the act of occupation. Then, the person makes itself spiritually present in the object, using their will, in the act of designation. Finally, in the act of formation, the physical and spiritual acts of acquisition are interconnected in the most complete way.

##### Occupation

Occupation is, generally speaking, the unification of person and thing. In order for the will to be present within the thing, it must express itself as corporeal, for the thing is also corporeal. Since the corporeality of the person is their body, the person thus acquires ownership by virtue of their body touching the thing. In this context, two distinct scenarios are possible: either the person moves toward the thing, or the thing comes to the person (or a thing belonging to the person). The former constitutes actual *occupation*, or physical seizure, while the latter constitutes *accession*. In the doctrine of finding, such as the discovery of treasure, both modes of acquisition are combined.

###### Seizure

The person acquires ownership through their personal presence within the object. Whatever I tread upon with my feet, or touch with my hands, becomes my property, provided that I am the first to do so, or that a previous occupant has since departed. This constitutes physical seizure, the primary mode of acquisition, effected directly by my own person. In this instance, it is the absolute object, i.e. my body, that performs the act of occupation, whereas in the case of accession, it is an external object of mine that does so. Physical seizure is still the most limited mode of acquisition, for it is bound to specific places and times. In this instance, if the possessor allows the object to pass out of their sight, it ceases to be their property. Nevertheless, even in this context, the process is not *exclusively* physical. For since right is the real existence of freedom, the *will* must accompany the physical act of seizure in order to bring ownership into being. 

###### Accession

If another object unites with my own in such a way that the two form a single object together, then the accessory object follows the principal object. The owner of the principal object acquires title by means of their own property, which serves as the agent or representative of their person. The object to be acquired is either attached to the property externally or arises organically from it. This constitutes a form of *indirect* occupation. Accession thus facilitates occupation and renders it possible for the owner alone. Yet, even though accession is a physical process and may indeed appear to be *solely* physical in nature, even in this context, the will must grant its assent. The only situation that presents a difficulty in determining property rights is the case where the supervening object already belongs to another owner. When this is not the case, there is naturally a single owner.

The most immediate form of accession, one that coincides entirely with the concept of occupation, is the acquisition of everything that already stood in connection with the principal object at the very moment of occupation. An example of this would be mineral deposits situated beneath the soil, deposits which cannot reasonably be regarded as state property. In such instances, strictly speaking, there are not two distinct objects, but only one. We therefore find it utterly impossible to justify the separation of mineral wealth from surface property rights, even less so as a measure grounded in economic principles. 

Conversely, if an entirely external, movable object comes to rest upon my land (e.g. in the case of the stranded property belonging to shipwreck victims), then this is the so-called *strand right*, which automatically rendered the owner of the coast the owner of the stranded property. This is a relic of barbaric piracy, and any state that elevates this right to the status of a state prerogative is not to be envied. Prussian General Land Law stipulated that this right is to be exercised only against states that themselves practice the strand right. However, if no owner can be found for the stranded property, then the owner of the shore becomes, by virtue of accession, the owner of the stranded object, which in most cases will be the state itself, given that the state is deemed the owner of the shores "under common law". Even so, it could just as well be any association, city, province, or even an individual who owns the property on the coast.

###### Finding

The most complete form of occupation is, finally, that in which physical seizure and accession occur in combination. On the one hand, the object presents itself to the person in their path, but on the other hand, the person must also physically take hold of the object. Taking these two elements together, we call this "finding", regardless of whether one was intentionally searching for it or not.

Upon the discovery of a movable object that belongs to another, one that the owner has lost, the act of finding does not by itself result in the acquisition of ownership if the owner comes forward within a specified period. However, the finder's reward, constituting a small portion of the object's value, may well be regarded as an effect of *occupation* (by taking possession), and thus as a partial acquisition of ownership. But in this context, *occupation* and *accession* operate inseparably, unless the object was found accidentally. In these cases the principle of *accession* appears to predominate, whereas in cases involving active searching, the principle of *occupation* is more apparent.

This distinction manifests itself even more clearly in the doctrine concerning *treasure trove*. The peculiar circumstance that a treasure is, in most instances, a *res nullius* (an ownerless thing) introduces additional specific provisions into this context. If an individual discovers a treasure upon or beneath their own land, they become its sole owner by right of *accession*, even though *occupation* must also be present as a concurrent cause. Conversely, if an individual discovers a treasure upon land belonging to another, that treasure is awarded in equal shares to the finder and to the landowner: to the former by right of *occupation*, and to the latter by right of *accession*, such that both modes of acquisition stand in perfect equilibrium.

Finally, regarding hunting and fishing, legal history reveals to us a struggle between these two modes of acquisition. Both activities belong to the doctrine of acquisition through active discovery, that is, cases where occupation is supplemented by accession. Under Roman law, occupation rightly prevailed, for given the independent nature of animals, they cannot reasonably be regarded as extensions of the forest or the river. Admittedly, one was permitted to hunt and fish only upon one’s own property, but in so doing retained the right to retrieve game that was shot within one’s own territory from a neighbor’s land should it have strayed onto or flown over the neighbor’s property before dying. The act of occupation remained valid even after the felled game had effectively become an appurtenance of another’s land. These provisions are eminently reasonable, for they fully uphold the liberty of the person and property, principles that were frequently violated in the Middle Ages. For in that era, game was regarded as an appurtenance of the forest, and hunting was a privilege granted to a knightly estate, i.e. a proprietary right *in rem*. Indeed, the *haute chasse* (hunting of large game) was deemed a prerogative of the Crown to which even the privileges of the nobility had to yield. The peasants never possessed the right to take possession of these "appurtenances" of the knightly estate, even when game had strayed onto their own fields. On the one hand, the peasants were compelled to endure the damage, effectively feeding the game without possessing the right to shoot it down upon their own land. On the other hand, the hunters were permitted to extend their hunt onto the peasants’ fields, trampling underfoot the very crops that the game had spared. This was truly an unfree state for property, and even if the farmer could demand compensation for damage to their crops, compensation often proved to be inadequate since the sustenance consumed by the game could scarcely be factored into the calculation. After these glaring injustices in Prussia had been eliminated by the legislation of 1848, the reactionary movement in this regard met with only limited success. They had only managed to reinstate a fragment of the old privileges through police ordinances, all the while feigning moral outrage over alleged violations of property rights, whereas in other matters, such as the privilege of tax exemption, they had achieved complete victory. 

##### Designation

Occupation is a mode of acquisition that is not yet commensurate with the person as such, since in occupation the will appears only in a sensible form as the presence of the human body. The person, however, is not only a natural being, but is also a spiritual one. Designation forms the other extreme to occupation on the condition that the will gives the object a spiritual form. The sensible aspect cannot, of course, be entirely absent. The form that that I give to the object is not the sensuous content of my will, but only represents my will as a purely spiritual manifestation. By giving the object a form that represents my will, others do not so much as see the existence of the will in the object as they imagine it in the form I gave it, and so the sensible aspect of my existence reduced to a minimum. Because designation minimally conveys the existence of the owner, it is not bound to a particular place and time like occupation is, and so it is therefore the most comprehensive mode of acquisition. Where a person has placed a sign, their will is most securely expressed, and even the most insignificant words can become names for significant objects. But because the sign, in terms of its content, remains external to the person, it often remains undetermined what the sign was originally intended to express beyond the fact that it was made by a person, which also makes designation the most indeterminate mode of acquisition. A sign on a piece of land either does not express its length and width, and even if boundary stones are used, they can still be a designation for someobject completely different, if they have a meaning at all.

##### Formation

The deficiency of designation was the externality that exists between the person, as the acquiring will, and the sign, as the object acquired, while in occupation the acquiring agent and the means of acquisition coincided in the physical person. While the deficiency of occupation was the predominance of the physical existence of the person in the object, in designation, on the other hand, the spiritual aspect prevailed. Thus, the third mode of acquisition, which avoids the deficiencies of the first two and combines their strengths, is formation. The object occupied by formation is the work of the person, a content born from spirit, which is not alien to the will since, as a product of its organic body, expresses the existence of the will in the object. The person no longer needs, as in occupation, to always be in direct possession of the object. For the form is a creation of spirit that is congruent with spirit because of its content. In this way, acquisition through formation is the most enduring in time since spirit has realized itself in the object. It is the most perfect acquisition because person and object are completely connected with each other, wherein the spiritual and sensual existence of the person and the object are in complete equilibrium. The form is the corresponding expression of the spirit in nature on the condition that the form has itself become a object. This self-contained objectification of the person in the object is its purpose. For instance, if a person were to gather wood from trees in the forest and the stones from the mountains and combined them to form a house, their purpose, like the activity through which the person has realized it, is in-and-for-itself the inherence of the will in the object, such that the new form can only be recognized as a work of spirit. Thus, the three modes of acquisition correspond to the three types of objects as different means of acquisition. Firstly, I acquire with my body, secondly with an external object, thirdly with an external object produced by my body, i.e. by means of my labor. In this sense, labor is the true acquisition of property.

Though the concept of formation has been demonstrated, the different types of formation mostly contain inessential determinations and are applications of the concept to a given variety of objects. However, due to the different nature of the objects being shaped, a more or less intimate kind of formation comes into being, and so we can still discover a philosophical gradation of the kinds of formation according to the objects of its activity.

The formation of living natural objects is, firstly, the immediate stage of volitional activity since the form is not directly created by the person, but maintains the conditions that allow the natural object to shape itself independently. Here, formation occurs in the manner of accession. Just as in accession my property occupies the place of my person, so in formation my property shapes itself instead of me. The form produced is thus a secondary aspect of the living natural object. However, the person still participates in formative activity of the object. This includes cultivating the land, tending and caring for animals, efforts that can be called indirect formative activities.

While in the first case the natural form shapes itself with my assistance, secondly, the formation of inanimate objects is the mediated, intellectual form of volitional activity in that the natural form of the inanimate is not preserved, but an artificial form is put in its place, whether in the interest of utility or beauty. Here, occupation is predominant because the person independently shapes the object into an artificial form. However, the form is also the intellectual sign of the will that is placed in the object. For example, a miller who builds a mill acquires the surrounding airflow, forming the air into a tool that powers the mill.

The most concrete interconnection of form and object is, thirdly, the formation of the person themselves. The form here is not only the innermost development of the object itself, as in development of the skills and abilities of the person, as in the first case, but also, as in the second, a purpose drawn from the spirit of the person that is imparted to the body. Just as both types of formation are interconnected, so too are occupation and accession here most closely intertwined. Though formation is the highest affirmative relationship of the person to the object, it is also the transition to the negation of this relationship. The object to be shaped already has a form, for there is no matter that is without form. The formation process therefore replaces the natural, original form with a spiritual form, giving the original object another form altogether. The object is not left as it originally was by nature, it is changed and negated for the purpose of fulfilling the will's objectives. In this way, the selfless nature of the object is revealed, as it fulfills its purpose in serving the person. This negative relationship of the person to the object is called its use or utility.

#### Use

Since the free will realizes itself only through the actual use of the object, we may designate use as the actual aspect of property. Property as such a only a possibility, a capacity to manifest oneself within the object. The owner is under no obligation to exercise this right. For legality only pertains to the permission and authorization of actions, not the duty to exercise them. Legal theorists term this possibility "bare ownership" (nuda proprietas), distinguishing it from "use" (usus) on the valid grounds that only the combination of the two constitutes the concept of full ownership. Specifically, while the concept of "bare" personality is indeed satisfied through the act of taking possession, the person remains unactual, that is, ineffectual. The actual person is a particular individual endowed with specific drives and impulses. These also demand satisfaction, within the sphere of right, and this satisfaction is achieved precisely through the use of property. In the act of using an object, the concrete existence of the personality is employed to satisfy its drives. Thus, use constitutes the finite, phenomenal aspect of property, whereas property in-itself, as the manifestation of freedom constitutes its infinite aspect. Possession, through the shaping of the object and aiming at the satisfaction of a drive, has thus now become an essential aspect of property. Possession is a necessary prerequisite for use, to the extent that use represents the factual dimension of property. Yet the ideal dimension of property can exist independently of possession. On the one hand, if property serves as the means by which one may legitimately satisfy one’s drives, a process we might term the "transfiguration" of possession, property nevertheless remains the concrete existence of the free will as such. Admittedly, since use constitutes the real aspect of property, the ideal existence of personality within property, when severed from use, by virtue of being useless and unreal, is an empty dominion. It is only through use that property is recognized, and only with the dissolution of the object in the service of the person does the existence of freedom appear.

Depending on the degree of this dissolution, legal scholars distinguish between different stages of use: usus, abusus, consumtio. Use is, firstly, utilization (usus), particularly in the case of immovable objects and living things. The latter, by virtue of the fact that life shapes and regenerates itself, retains the greatest degree of self-maintenance. A tree bears fruit, and a field yields grain. This does not wear the object out, but it does make demands upon it such that it conserves itself, or indeed must be conserved. The field must lie fallow and be re-fertilized, the tree does not bear fruit every year, and so on. Thus, even utilization already shades into wearing out (abusus) the object, the second stage of use, which applies particularly to inanimate, movable objects that gradually become unusable or perish through use. Initially, in the process of its negation, the object remained entirely intact, but now, the process of negation is a gradually wears down the object. Finally, in the stage of consumption (consumptio), this negation becomes sudden. There are objects that can only be utilized through consumption. To this category belongs, in particular, the fruits of living things, which by virtue of their separation from their source, effectively become inanimate and perishable objects (such as grain, fruit, etc.), in short, fungible goods.

If we now consider use in relation to the specific quality of the object, whereby it corresponds to a particular drive or desire of the individual person, we arrive at the utility or use-value of the object. The universal presence of personality within the usable object, abstracted from any specific means of satisfaction, constitutes its exchangibility or value, its universal utility. Finally, because use simultaneously posits within itself the aspect of universality, it elevates itself, through the process of prescription, to the status of ownership itself.

##### Utility (Use-Value)

The use of property is not an isolated aspect of ownership out of others, but constitutes the entirety of ownership itself, viewed solely from the perspective of its manifestation. However, since the totality of manifestations inherently encompasses the essence itself, and since this essence is not some entity lurking *behind* the manifestations, it follows that any claim to ownership seeking to assert itself as something distinct *behind* the full plenitude of actual uses is, for that very reason, accurately termed "bare ownership." Thus, just as the principle of exclusivity was inherent in the very concept of ownership, so too is it inherent in the concept of use. The owner possesses the right to full utilization, and if this were not the case, they would not be the owner. They must be able to exclude anyone else from such utilization. Conversely, full utilization is ownership itself, and whoever enjoys the full use of a thing is the owner of that thing.

Nevertheless, for reasons of utility, one can readily conceive of a scenario in which the owner has transferred a limited portion of their right to use to another party. Legal science aptly designates this right of use, detached from the core of ownership, as a servitude. For through it, ownership is rendered subservient and unfree. Servitude can be deemed tolerable if only if it originated voluntarily and is of a limited nature. The servitude represents the element of contingency, whereas ownership represents the element of necessity. The Romans termed the former a *jus ad rem*, a right *to* a thing, in contradistinction to a *jus in re*, a right *in* a thing, the latter expression serving to denote ownership in its true and proper sense. But, in truth, this distinction is actually ill-conceived, for every right of use constitutes a right of ownership, and secondly, a right *to* a thing is, for all intents and purposes, one and the same as a right *in* a thing.

The distinction between servitudes lies in the nature of the restriction, relating either to the duration or the scope of the rights of use, or to the person or property itself. Specifically, it may be a very specific right of use pertaining to one property that is granted to neighboring property for an indefinite period, such as a right-of-way, water drainage, the *servitus luminum*, and so forth. These are rights through which the *praedium dominans* (dominant tenement) is rendered usable in the first place. Alternatively, transfer of the full right of use may take place for a limited time, at most for the lifetime of a single person, as is the case with usufruct. Because these latter servitudes are tied to a specific person, they are termed *personal* servitudes, whereas the former are designated *real* servitudes. Finally, the encumberance constitutes a restriction on ownership consisting of a personal performance that is required of the current owner. 

In the Middle Ages, and to some extent even among the Romans, the restricted nature of ownership went as far as to grant a perpetual usufruct, one that even extended to one’s heirs. This constituted the *emphyteusis* and *superficies* of the Romans, forms of hereditary leasehold, as well as the law of feudal tenure. Originally, none of these legal relationships were intended to constitute full ownership, and yet, the holders of these rights, acting as full beneficiaries, were in reality the true owners, whereas the "superior owner" remained only the *nudus dominus* (the bare lord). A similar situation prevailed regarding the distinction between *bonitary* and *quiritary* ownership among the Romans. Specifically, if certain assets were not alienated through the strict forms of transfer prescribed by the civil law, the acquirer would obtain *in bonis*, constituting full ownership, while the alienator retained the *nudum jus quiritium* (the bare right of the quirites), which was of absolutely no practical benefit to them. In the aforementioned legal relationships, however, the owner retained, in addition to the honorary rights associated with feudal tenure (the right to homage, etc.), certain rights of material benefit, such as a *canon* (fixed rent), a *laudemium* (relinquishment fee), and the like. The owner did indeed derive some benefit from this arrangement, albeit a minor one—whereas in the case of *servitudes*, he had retained the greater share of the benefit. Consequently, within these specific institutions, the nature of the legal relationship became completely inverted: the user became the true owner, while the owner became merely the holder of an isolated right of use. The historical development of these relationships has therefore demonstrated their steady approximation to full ownership, until their holders came to be designated by the term *dominus utilis*, while the superior owner was termed *dominus directus*. 

##### Exchangability (Value)

Since use constitutes the actual aspect of property, it represents the existence of the will not only in terms of its particular drives, but also in terms of its universal personality. This universal existence of freedom, when viewed from the perspective of utility, is no longer *this* utility, but is utility as such, and this is what we call *value*. In value, the *qualitative* utility of a thing, its capacity to satisfy a specific need, has been transformed into a *quantitative* utility, the capacity to satisfy *any* need. Embedded within every specific thing is its quantitative utility, the degree to which it satisfies the drives that it is capable of fulfilling. Every qualitative thing is a value for its owner prior to any act of exchange. And yet, by virtue of the quantitative aspect inherent to value, every thing becomes commensurate in terms of value with every *other* thing, something that its particular qualities, considered by themselves, are incapable of being. The universal representative of this value is money, which simultaneously measures of the value of all things and is independent of any particular value. Since money presents value externally as a distinct entity, serving in a sense as the extracted or abstracted value of things as they exist *for others* in the context of exchange, Adam Smith thereby designates money as the "instrument of commerce." Proudhon distinguished these two forms of value as a value *in itself* or *use-value* (*prix*) and value *for others* as *exchange-value* (*valeur*).

The first proposition we encounter, following this clarification of terms, is that the owner of the *use-value* of property must also be the owner of its *value*. Full ownership does not tolerate their separation. I must be able to elevate myself from the specific nature of the object back into my general personality, i.e. I must be able to realize the object's value by converting it into money. If the entire substance of the object belongs to me, then naturally its value belongs to me as well. The separation of an object's value from its use-value constitutes that same unfreedom of ownership encountered in the institution of feudal law and now reappears from a different perspective. For while the vassal possessed only the ownership of *use*, but not that of the *value*, the feudal overlord, conversely, possessed only the ownership of the *value*, but this value remained hollow since they made no *use* it. The holder of an entailed estate finds themselves in a situation similar to the vassal, though somewhat more advantageous: they can make full use of the estate, but cannot realize its value. This value, being inalienable, resides in the hands of a rigid, abstract, and unreal personality: namely, the "family spirit" intended to serve as a pillar of royal authority within a nobility by virtue of the continuity of its property holding. 

Just as we first spoke of use as an inseparable part of ownership, and then considered it as separate from ownership, the same can be said for value. This separation, whether of the whole or a part of the value, legal scholars term the right of pledge. Here, the matter at hand is not the utility of the right of pledge, but the legal concept of the relationship itself. The pledgee is not entitled to the use of the object, but is permitted, if they are unable to repay their debt to another, to have the pledged object sold in order to realize the value of their property. Thus, in the case of a pledge, the ownership of the value is simultaneously of such a nature that it can be readily converted into money, particularly if the sum of the lended money remains within the enduring exchange value of the pledged object. However, if the exchange value of the object is less than the debt, the pledgee thereby becomes the full owner of the object. A movable pledge is termed a possessory pledge because the security arises solely through its actual possession, which is not the case with an immovable pledge, i.e. a mortgage.

##### Prescription

Since use constitutes realized ownership, encompassing within itself the very essence of ownership, it necessarily transitions into full ownership. For the elevation of utility to universality is already the very existence of the free will itself. Just as use is indispensable to the preservation of ownership, I acquire ownership when I make use of the object. Use subsumes occupation, so it may also involve marking or shaping the object, though this is not necessary. Thus, this constitutes a fourth original mode of acquisition: prescription or usucapion (*usucapio*). The owner who does not use their property thereby forfeits ownership of it, while the possessor who uses it acquires ownership. With the extinguishment of the old ownership, the new ownership comes into being. In this manner, extinctive prescription and acquisitive prescription coincide. Here lies the point at which possession constitutes and creates right. Bare ownership inevitably loses in its struggle against use precisely because it lacks all reality, it is a negligible shadow that the user eventually acquires as an adjunct. However, this acquisition occurs only after the lapse of a certain period, such that if the original owner initiates legal proceedings before the expiration of that period, the property is rightfully theirs.

But is the duration of this specific time limit philosophical in nature or not? Why is time even necessary to prescription at all? Indeed, it has even been doubted whether prescription itself is necessary concept of legal science or is positive in nature. The *Pandects* assert the latter, stating that: "Prescription was introduced solely for the sake of utility, lest the ownership of things remain uncertain for too long" (l. 1. *De usurpationibus et usucapionibus*). However, the ground of utility must yield to the ground of reason. In this case, an owner who fails to make use of their property is deemed to have withdrawn their will from the object. However, this presumption can only be made if they have allowed their property to pass out of their custody, and as a result of this, the exercise of their right of use has become impossible for a certain period. The duration of time is necessary because it is the sole basis from which the possessor’s *animus sibi habendi*, their intent to hold the object for themselves, can be inferred. By contrast, in other modes of acquisition that involve ownerless objects, the instantaneous act of acquisition suffices to establish ownership. Such acts are complete within an indivisible instant, for they give expression to the pure concept of free will in the very moment of its realization. Prescription nevertheless requires the passage of time to the extent that the phenomenon or existent manifests itself only after a certain amount of time has passed. Seizing or marking an object is momentary, if not instantaneous, whereas the satisfaction of my drives through use requires a series of operations. A field of grain, for example, must be plowed, sown, and harvested, and so forth. In this context, the consummation of use essentially presupposes a duration of time. So it is only the requirement of a temporal duration that is philosophical in nature, but the specific length of that duration is of a more positive nature. Roman law, however, fixes the period of acquisitive prescription at one year for movable property, and at two years for immovable property. Finally, an interruption of possession (*usurpatio*) nullifies the prescription, for use is possible only through an uninterrupted continuity of possession.

Prescription applies not only to individuals, but the relationship between individual and public property. For instance, ownership of the monuments of a non-existent people is extinguished through prescription due to non-use. When the Turks conquered Greece, the monuments of the Greeks no longer served as memorials to the victories of their forefathers. The monuments became ownerless, and any finder could take possession of them as *res nullius*, and indeed, the Turks burned lime from the beautiful marble statues. However, following its liberation, the Greek people reasserted possession and prohibited excavations by foreign scholars. Just as extinctive prescription arising from non-use took effect in this instance, so conversely does an author’s ownership of their works lapse through prescription resulting from their general use by the public. A book is a creative achievement like any other, the proceeds of which pass to the author’s family. However, most legal systems stipulate a period of thirty years, calculated from the author’s death, after which anyone is free to print the works. But why this distinction between intellectual property and tangible property? A house remains the property of the family precisely because it is not utilized by the public at large. An author’s works, however, pass into the realm of general public use. The thoughts of Schiller and Goethe belong no more to their families than they do to everyone else. What has taken place here is acquisitive prescription, the acquisition of ownership through long-term possession. However, a translation of an author, since it presupposes a specific scholarly endeavor, may in turn become the property of the translator or editor, subject to the same restrictions previously applicable to the author themselves. 

#### Relinquishment

Through prescription, the positive relationship of the person to the thing arises once again from the negative one. Now we find, within the highest form of property relation, the interconnection of these two aspects. The interconnection appears in one moment as positive relationship mastering the negative, then conversely, the negative relationship triumphs over the positive, until finally, both are in equilibrium with each other.

##### Self-Expression

Self-expression constitutes the fifth and most perfect of all original modes of acquisition, for it encompasses all the preceding ones: occupation, accession, designation and use. As a result, property that is acquired in this manner is the most secure. By expressing my self through the exercise of my physical and mental skills, a performance or work is created, an act which, as a modification of both my own person and external things, constitutes a twofold negation. The ethical aspect of work lies in the fact that, in working, I simultaneously restrain and negate my own drives in order to give the purpose of my spirit to the external world. It is not only the negation of my personality, effected through the exertion of my physical and mental powers, that transforms into an positive relationship through the creation of an external object that only becomes *mine* when I have separated it from myself. Instead, the modification of the external object brought about by my activity also causes the negation of that object to appear as the positive existence of my own person embodied in it. With this twofold negation, the positive aspect of the acquisitive relationship decisively remains the predominant one.

One of the activities that falls under this category, literary work, merits particular mention, for it presents a peculiar case of it. The expression of the self constitutes not only an *externalization* in the positive sense, but is simultaneously an *alienation*, a negative relation to the object itself, while, nonetheless, ownership is preserved in one specific respect. Here, we need to clearly distinguish what is alienated and what is not, when a book is published. As soon as the manuscript, the drawing, or a similar work is completed, the author, through the externalization of their own self, has divested themselves of the object and thereby acquires it in doing so. In the case of a singular work of art or a handcrafted article, the creator can lay no further claim to the object once they have alienated it, so the object is free to fall into the hands of another. However, this is not the case with manuscripts. The author sells the publisher the permission to reproduce the manuscript a specific number of times, determined by the size of the print run. As a result, any copy printed in excess of this agreed-upon quantity constitutes an act of theft committed by the publisher against the author. Alternatively, the author may sell the entire manuscript, including all future editions and printings. In this instance, they have indeed divested themselves of the full right to reproduce their thoughts, but they have done so voluntarily in favor of the publisher.

The aforementioned relationship is straightforward, but what is the relationship between the publisher and the purchaser of an individual copy? There is no question that, by acquiring a copy, the purchaser acquires the right to use the book, though not the *full* right of use. In other words, two entirely distinct elements are at play within this legal relationship, elements that must be strictly differentiated. The purchaser of an individual copy acquires the full use of that specific piece of property, the right to appropriate the thoughts contained within the book, to enjoy them, to use them, and to build further thoughts upon them. In short, the purchaser has the right to educate and instruct themselves, thereby acquiring the very ability to write a book themselves. However, in purchasing the individual copy, they have not acquired ownership of the universal thoughts that reside within the individual copy merely as a specimen of its kind, nor the right to sell copies of the book they have purchased. It is this right that the publisher and the author have reserved for themselves. From this, we can derive the unlawful nature of unauthorized reprinting. The situation is similar regarding oral lectures, copperplate engravings, and so forth. The unauthorized reprinter acts as if, along with obtaining right to use the individual copy, he had also obtained the right to reproduce it. This is not true, and they have committed an act of theft by taking another person's property. It is in this light, and in no other, that unauthorized reprinting must be regarded.

Louis Blanc argues that intellectual property does not exist: that thoughts, the moment they are published, immediately become part of the public domain. Quite right! However, the *right* to reproduce copies is *not* part of the public domain, for it constitutes not intellectual property, but tangible property. Of course, one might argue that if thoughts truly belong to the public domain, the common heritage of the people, then it should not be left to the arbitrary discretion of an author or publisher whether or not those thoughts are disseminated. But no publisher would ever refuse to produce fresh editions, continually and without hesitation, whenever the public demand requires it. But if the objection is raised that this remains a privilege and that competition should apply here as well, Louis Blanc responds: “Do not grant the writer a privilege, as you would an inventor; rather, grant him—once and for all—a public reward as the purchase price for placing his work at the unrestricted disposal of everyone; and then you may permit unauthorized reproduction.” But does this not amount to making material property, just like intellectual property, into common property? The thirty-year prescriptive period for the acquisition of literary works by the public simply cuts the Gordian knot of the difficulty without resolving the question on a fundamental level.

The legal assessment of plagiarism proves to be more complex than that of unauthorized reprinting. The plagiarist does not infringe upon the right to reproduce copies. They merely use the thoughts of another to construct a different book, which is then printed as their own work. Where is the dishonesty? After all, I am entitled to become an author precisely by drawing upon the works of others. Here, everything hinges entirely upon the *form*. If the same thoughts are presented in a similar, slavishly imitated form, albeit with a few insignificant "trifling modifications," as Hegel puts it (*Philosophy of Right*, § 69), then it constitutes plagiarism: a fraud, a subtle, covert theft that circumvents the law, whereas unauthorized reprinting violates it openly. However, one cannot reasonably demand that every author possess thoughts that are entirely and exclusively novel. Indeed, it is often more important, instructive, and meritorious to cast another’s thoughts into a new form that appeals to everyone than to originally set them down in a form that is unpalatable. Plagiarism does not exist provided that the work has arisen, as a unified whole, from the author’s own creative spirit, even if the underlying and guiding thoughts were, to a greater or lesser extent, borrowed. Who is, or who *can* ever be, original at all times? Out of a hundred books, scarcely even one fits that description. To this number, one must add the many books whose very existence is owed purely to utility: textbooks, compendiums, arithmetic manuals, devotional works, and the like. In such cases, is not the subject matter mostly dictated by the very nature of the material itself? For this reason, a translation, when compared to its original source, never constitutes plagiarism, for the *form* in this instance, the linguistic form, belongs invariably to the translator.

##### Dereliction

That the will externalizes itself into an external object through its labor is not the only mode of relinquishment. Instead of externalizing itself, the will may instead externalize the object relative to its person, terminating an old relationship rather than forging a new one. This predominantly negative relationship is what legal theorists term *dereliction*. The unity of person and object that was present in ownership is thereby annulled. However, since the object is negated only to the extent that it ceases to be the *existence* of the free will, it is thereby simultaneously affirmed. In this way, the object ceases to be negated by the free will through its use of it. Its negation is negated, and this constitutes a profoundly positive relationship, not only with respect to the object, but also with respect to the person. For the act of the will withdrawing itself from an external object, thereby affirming its own pure personality, represents a higher mode of existence for its freedom. I am freer in the remainder of my property precisely because I have cast off what is displeasing to me. The right of alienation thus constitutes the very pinnacle of property rights, bringing the concept of ownership to its full completion.

But question arises: what can I derelict? Since dereliction is intended to be a legal act, an expression of my free will, I can only derelict a *part* of the expression of my free will in an external thing. Only in this way do I retain the existence of my freedom while renouncing a specific part. Were I to attempt to detach the entire scope of the existence of my freedom from myself, no legal person would remain to perform this legal act. It follows from this that suicide is wrong. Here, we are not yet concerned with the exceptional cases where the individual voluntarily chooses to sacrifice their person and life for the sake of another person, social ideal, or higher purpose. Instead, from a strictly legal standpoint, there must be a *party performing the alienation*; yet in the case of suicide, the *entire scope* of the personality is the very thing being alienated. Suicide is therefore a *fact*, but it is not a *right*, it is not an expression of freedom. Attempts are made to justify suicide by citing the maxim: *Nemo sibi injuriam facere potest* (No one can do an injustice to himself). This maxim is technically correct in that I have the power to arbitrarily alienate myself from any specific aspect of my rights, given that legality imposes only a *competence* (a power) and not a *commanding duty*. In the case of suicide, I cannot do an injustice to myself because "I" no longer exist at all once the act is completed. Suicide has, therefore, rightly been regarded as a momentary insanity, as a suspension of the free personality. Nevertheless, suicide is still *wrong* precisely because it constitutes the negation of the free person, treating the person as if they were a *thing* (and this irrational judgment is the very concept of a crime). Freedom is the purpose of right, to annul its entire scope for oneself is not a right, but a wrong, even if, from a strictly legal standpoint, one cannot specify against whom suicide is committed. From a social standpoint, however, suicide is committed against the personal relationship, against the other persons that constitute their own substantial essence. Of course, it is impossible to punish suicide because punishment is a temporary infringement upon their freedom, which they have already perminently inflicted upon themselves.

If suicide is wrong because I relinquish the entire existence of my freedom, then one might argue that severing a part of the body, such as a finger or a hand, is permissible because only a part of the personality is relinquished, so it is argued. Origen supposedly castrated himself to escape carnal sin, others mutilate their right hand to avoid military service, etc. The obligation to procreate, the obligation to military service, and so forth, do not yet fall within the purview of private right. But even here we have to maintain that I can relinquish things that belong to my free existence. A limb of my body, however, belongs to my person itself, and the person cannot be divided. In severing a vital part of my body, I infringe upon the person *qua* person, upon freedom as such. Therefore, every act of self-mutilation is wrong. Indeed, given that everything within the organism is interconnected, might it not even result in death? Might not life itself be severed along with the severed finger?

##### Sale 

The perfected form of relinquishment is that in which the positive and negative relationships of the person to the thing hold one another in equilibrium. This constitutes a *dereliction* on the part of the one owner, a relinquishing of the thing, which coincides simultaneously with the acquisition of the thing by another owner. In selling, the one owner withdraws their will from the thing on the condition that the other owner instills their will into it. The person who alienates the thing, strictly speaking, abandons it by casting it away from themselves. The person who sells the thing, however, ensures that it passes into the ownership of another person, so that it does not lose the dignity of representing a person.

If one asks again, like the case of dereliction, what things can be sold and what things cannot, a similar answer must be given. I may not sell to another person my life, nor my freedom, for instance, by selling myself out to them as a slave. In such a case, the free person, the very agent by means of whom I would execute this legal transaction, is once again absent. Even if civil servants do indeed bind themselves to their duties for the duration of their professional career, the scope of what they have sold encompasses only their official duties, not all their other human expressions of freedom, as the current regime of coercion would now have it. Even one’s vote is supposed, in a certain sense, to have become the property of the government *tanquam cadaver* (like a corpse), as the Jesuits say. In that case, the elected official would just be a tool, machine, or slave to the elector and nothing more. They are not a slave so long as, beyond being an official, they are also a citizen and a human being. Serfdom is also an unfree relationship according to this, while the day laborer, subject to the condition of termination upon hire, retains their freedom unharmed.

Accordingly, all unlimited or absolute rights are inalienable. Just as I do not have the right to alienate my life, the entire scope of my freedom, so do I not have the right to alienate my entire spirit, honor, or freedom of conscience. Should I have done so, such a transaction would be devoid of legal force. Such a right is imprescriptible, and even the promise to do so is invalid and non-binding. According to Tacitus’s account, the German who had in the heat of gambling staked all of his finite possessions, would then, on the final throw, stake his freedom and that of his entire family, binding himself to the winner as a slave. They are under no obligation to honor such a promise. Likewise, this imprescriptible right of freedom not only applies to individuals, but to entire nations as well. An oppressed people, even one that was forced to submit through a peace treaty, is not bound by the promise it gave to the victor. At any moment, it may reclaim its freedom and independence, and it always possesses the right to do so. Greece languished for three centuries under the bondage of the Turks, until finally, amidst the jubilant acclamations of all Europe and beneath the thunder of the cannons of England, France, and Russia at Navarino, on the anniversary of the Battle of Salamis, it once again took its place as a free member of the great European family. Every downtrodden nation possesses the same right to self-determination.

In the act of alienation, the transfer of ownership presupposes the existence of another free will. In the realm of property, we were concerned with only an individual free will. Now, the existence of a second person in the thing is mediated by the non-existence of the first person in that same thing. A person acquires ownership only by virtue of another person losing it. This is the transfer of ownership (*traditio*), with which a series of modes of derivative acquisition begins. The person relinquishing ownership expresses the existence of their freedom only in the other person, not in the thing itself. The person who relinquishes is free only within the will of the person who acquires. Indeed, the person who acquires cannot relinquish anything in this moment, but can only *deny* what is relinquished to them if they do not want to acquire it. Thus, the negative conduct of the relinquisher becomes positive  solely through the will of the acquirer. To be sure, the new owner is also free in their ownership of the thing, but the source of their freedom is nothing other than the existence of the freedom of another. Therefore, the existence of the freedom of each individual lies in the free will of the other. Freedom has progressed to a higher concept, and this higher concept is the contract.

### Contract

#### Contract Formation

While in property the will appears as the substance that recognizes in the objects it possesses as the many accidental manifestations of its freedom, this static relationship of substantiality becomes a causal relationship in the contract. The static substance passes into activity through its relationship to another substance, another free personality, both of whom exchange their accidental attributes, i.e. one of their objects or services, with each other. In the contract, two persons and two objects exist in relation to one other. The persons come to an agreement with each other before this agreement is applied to the objects. We must therefore consider, firstly, the subjective agreement of wills as the essence of the contract, secondly, the objects of the contract, and thirdly, the relationship of the agreement to the object, the performance.

##### Agreement

The first question to be asked is: what are the requirements, prerequisites, and conditions for the agreement of two wills? Secondly: what are the various aspects of this agreement? Thirdly: in what manner has the existing agreement become objective, that is, concretely realized through the actual conclusion of the contract?

The requirements for this agreement are, first and foremost, two persons, a condition that was not yet necessary in the context of property *as such*. In the context of property, if two or more persons happened to be present, their relationship was the simple, mutual recognition of property owners. Each person respects the property of the other, whatever that property might happen to be, and yet, each person does not yet explicitly *will* that *this thing* should belong to *this person*. Property was therefore only the *existence* of freedom in general, wherein the specific content remained a matter of indifference. For contracts, however, it is precisely this specific content that becomes the focal point. The persons *will* that *this thing* shall become the property of *this person*, thereby ceasing to be the property of the other, and vice versa for the other specific thing. The will of the individual therefore appears in a contract not as the universal, rational will, but as a particular, *arbitrary* will. Since this arbitrary will constitutes the starting point of the contract, and is the very agency that establishes property, the contract represents property in its *phenomenal manifestation*, a property realized through the agency of a particular will. The contract distinguishes itself from use, which shared a similar conceptual determination in this respect. Within the contract, the satisfaction of needs is no longer the sole objective, as it was in the case of use. Instead, property has now itself become the object of the arbitrary will, even if this property was originally established solely for the sake of satisfying those very needs. However, the condition of the contract is not only a particular will, but the unity of this particular will with another particular will. The mere commonality of wills has taken the place of the universality of the will in this instance. The individual relinquishes their particular will and wills what the other wills, it is the relationship between two persons who bind themselves exclusively to one another to perform a specific service. And for this reason, the agreement within the contract (*consensus*) establishes an obligation (*obligatio*), from which the name "Law of Obligations" is derived.

The two sides of the agreement are as follows. Since the existence of freedom is simultaneously present in each of the two wills, the contract possesses a twofold existence, namely, within the spirit of each person. The will of the one to relinquish their property in favor of the other is called the *promise* (*promissio*). The will of the other to make the property relinquished as their own is called the *acceptance* (*acceptio*). Unless these two sides coincide in a single, indivisible moment, no agreement exists. It is precisely in this agreement that the essence of a contract exists. For since the concept of the contract lies in the existence of freedom within another will, and since this existence is posited through the agreement, nothing further is required to bring a fully valid contract into being.

The conclusion of the contract is nothing other than the objectification of the subjective unity of the two sides contained within the agreement. The objective existence of spirit, however, is language. The agreement is therefore bound to language, without which the contract cannot yet be regarded as having been truly concluded. This solemn conclusion of the contract is the *stipulation*, without which the contract has merely been initiated (for instance, in so-called *punctations*, etc.). To determine precisely what specific form this should be is a matter for positive law. Philosophy can only claim that the solemn expression of the will must be embodied in language. However, since in a contract the very existence of my freedom is rooted solely in the will of another, the contract is deemed legally concluded the moment these forms are articulated.

##### Subject Matter

Since arbitrary will, referring solely to a specific content, constitutes the starting point of the contract, its subject matter cannot be an absolute right, nor the general existence of freedom *per se* (as is the case with property). Instead, it must itself be of a specific nature, either an individual external thing or a limited right. Furthermore, as we observed earlier, the arbitrary will may not dispose of the entire scope of one’s spiritual aptitudes, but only a portion thereof. The limitation of the subject of the contract necessarily entails a corresponding limitation of its object. The person as such can never be the object of a contract. The only objects that remain are the transfer of property, the use of a thing, or specific personal services. Hence, it is erroneous to regard ethical relationships, such as the state (as Rousseau did) or marriage (as Kant did), as contracts. In both cases, what prevails is not an agreement of wills regarding external things, but a unity of persons with respect to ethical duties and the general conditions of life. In the sphere of ethicality, the caprice of an arbitrary will and the purely juridical standpoint of contract are excluded. Positive obligations to others come to be once the bond has been forged between them, embodying a moral imperative and binding necessity that establishes the guiding principle for the individual’s actions that stands above their arbitrary will.

##### Performance

The application of the agreement to the object constitutes the performance, the manifestation of the essential content that was already implicit in the stipulation. Since the existence of the two wills, expressed in the spiritual medium of the word, already constitutes the perfected existence of the legal relationship, it follows that, by virtue of the agreement, ownership has already passed from the one person to the other. Original acquisition of ownership, by contrast, can only be effected *in re*, that is, through actual seizure, demarcation, and so forth, because ownership is the immediate relationship of the person to the thing. While under Roman contract law, the transfer of ownership typically took place only through the actual delivery of the thing. In truth, the performance possesses no other significance than this: to add possession and the right of use to the ownership of the thing. The difference is that whereas the agreement depended upon arbitrary will, the performance no longer does. This means that one is legally bound to render the performance, and failure to perform would signify nothing other than withholding from the other party what is already their property. But even though all contracts in modern law are consensual contracts, this does not preclude the fact that there are certain contracts where a specific obligation commences only upon actual performance (*in re*). It goes without saying that, in the case of a loan, a contract of deposit, or a contract of hire, the obligation to return the borrowed or entrusted object arises only after the object has been delivered. Nevertheless, the initial performance remains nothing other than the selfless consequence of the agreement. If someone has promised me a loan, I can compel them by legal means to provide it to me. On the other hand, the very nature of such contracts imply that the act of performance does not terminate the obligation, but that the fulfillment of one party's obligation serves to establish the obligation of the other. The Romans designate such obligations, which arise from the act of another as *real contracts*, in contradistinction to *consensual contracts*. Fichte’s assertion that every contract is a *real contract*, and that I am bound to render a counter-performance only by virtue of the other party’s actual performance, is unphilosophical, for it contradicts the very essence of a contract, which lies not in the act of performance itself, but in the mutual agreement of two wills. Prior to the commencement of the other party’s performance, Fichte maintains, the obligation is purely moral, not legal. But in making this assertion, the entire legal nature of a contract is fundamentally misunderstood. While keeping one’s promise is certainly a moral duty, it is not *only* a moral duty. If that were the case, there would be no legally binding promises whatsoever within the sphere of civil law. 

#### Contract Types

In order for the classification for contracts to be rational, it must flow from the very concept of the contract itself. Since we have distinguished the three aspects within this concept as the agreement, the object, and the performance, these three principles of classification will take place here. However, since the agreement constitutes the essential element, the relationship between its constituent aspects and its relationship to the performance provides the primary classification of contracts, while the various possible objects of the contract serves only to establish the subdivisions that recur within each main type of contract.

Firstly, regarding the constituent aspects of the agreement, the promise and the acceptance, a fundamental distinction among contracts takes place. In one type, the act of promising and the act of accepting are distributed between the two owners such that the one only promises while the other only accepts, which constitutes the formal or gratuitous contract known as the gift. In another type, each of the two owners promise and accept, but only with respect to different, specific objects, such that a change occurs in the external quality of their respective possessions. This second class of contracts comprises the real and onerous contract known as the exchange. Hegel rightly assigned them as real contracts because, in this instance, each party encompasses within itself the entire scope of the agreement. It is precisely this full positing of the constituent aspects of the concept that we term "reality" in the true sense. Each person is no longer a one-sidedness standing in opposition to the other and embodies the entire reality of the agreement.

Contracts fall into three subcategories based on their possible subject matters: contracts concerning things (property), contracts concerning the use of a thing, or contracts concerning a service. Within these three categories, however, less fundamental distinctions can give rise to a deeper subdivision of contracts. In the realm of property, the distinction between corporeal and incorporeal things (such as claims) comes into play. The distinction between fungible and non-fungible things, and between specific and generic things, find particular application in contracts concerning the *use* of a thing.

The third principle of classification does not concern further subdivision within the existing categories, but appends a third *main* division to the first two that were established by the first principle of classification. This third principle rests neither upon the subjective aspect (agreement), nor upon the nature of the subject matter, but the very relationship between the two in the act of performance. Since the connection between the agreement and the performance is essentially contingent, it is conceivable that the performance might fail to be realized. The safeguard against this contingency constitutes the third class of contracts known as securities, which also have subcategories following the second principle of classification.

It is in accordance with these principles that the classification of contracts must be undertaken. Any other classification is only of interest to the history of legal science, such as the aforementioned Roman division into consensual, real, literal, and verbal contracts. We have already seen that all contracts are originally consensual contracts, and that they only become real contracts when the nature of the subject matter requires it. The fact that some contracts are concluded in writing while others are concluded orally carries a practical significance, namely, that in the case of more important contracts, one that has a written form provides a greater guarantee of provability. The Roman distinction between *contractus* and *pacta* has vanished completely, and the latter was actionable only under the Praetorian Edict, not by virtue of statutory law. Finally, the absurdity of Roman contract law, according to which there also existed "nominal" and "innominate" contracts (the latter of which, strangely enough, still possessed names), must also be abandoned. On the basis of these principles, the classification of contracts is as follows:

##### Gift

A. Gift of Ownership

α. Gift of an Object — whether it be a specific item or
a generic one (money). This constitutes the *donatio* proper.

β. Gift of a Claim (*cessio*) — a monetary claim against my debtor is also my property, which I am entitled to give away.

B. Gift of Use — since the entirety of the use is tantamount to the object itself, only a limited portion of the use can be given away,otherwise, it is the ownership of the object that has been gifted. This use, limited in terms of both time and scope, constitutes the subject matter of the contract of loan, under which the lender retains ownership of the object.

α. An interest-free loan agreement concerning a specific object
is the *commodatum* (loan for use) proper.

β. If the object is a generic one, the contract is termed a *mutuum* (loan for consumption). A generic object, such as money, is not regarded as a *species* (specific item), but rather as a *genus* (generic category). Consequently, upon repayment, it is not the *species* of the object that is returned, but only the *genus*, the equivalent value in the same denomination. For money is, by its very nature, intended to be spent. The borrower becomes the owner of the *species*, solely in respect of its use, while the lender remains the owner of the value alone. 

C. Gift of a Service — included here are the contract of deposit (*depositum*), the contract of mandate (*mandatum*), and any other service rendered gratuitously to a third party, such as the public lectures of a teacher.

##### Exchange

A. Exchange of Ownership

α. Exchange of Things

a. Exchange of Physical Things — This is *permutatio* or true barter, an expression of the so-called *innominate contract* *do, ut des* ("I give, so that you may give").

b. Exchange of Claims — The most diverse legal claims may be exchanged in this context. What is of particular relevance here is the *compromise* (*transactio*), wherein each party relinquishes a portion of their legal claims in order to secure another portion. Thus, the compromise might well be classified among those contracts concerning uncertain matters. However, each party regards their own rights as certain, they simply lack the hope of being able to prove them.

c. Exchange of Possible Things — Just as the exchange of physical objects relates to the present, and the exchange involved in a compromise relates to legal claims from the past, finally must there also exist contracts of exchange concerning possible things or *expectations* that relate to the future. In the case of uncertain matters, either outcome of a gain or loss may occur, and so the expectation might fail to be realized. The subject matter of the contract here consists of a *gain* and a *loss*, for these constitute the expectations of the future in our ownership. This exchange of gain and loss may take place between two or more persons mutually, or between a third party.

aa. Games and Bets (*alea, lusus*) are examples of such contracts, wherein each party hopes for a gain and fears a loss. The parties mutually promise to pay out to one another any gain that accrues to them, while conversely bearing any loss incurred. It is not actual profit and loss, strictly speaking, that constitute the subject matter of the contract, but the hopes themselves. What is exchanged hopes that are mutually exchanged, even if they materialize only for one party while, for the other, it is the fears that come to pass. The hopes and fears are the elements that renders the game pleasurable, suspenseful, and passionately stimulating, whereas their actual realization does not. The true gamer plays for the sake of the game, not for the sake of money. Even if no payment were to be made afterward, provided that one is not acting as the banker in a game of chance, the true purpose of the game would have been achieved nonetheless. One plays to win *something* because if one knew beforehand that there would be no reward, the very emotions that give purpose to the game would not arise at all. Only the games of the intellect, such as chess, constitutes an exception to this rule. In that instance, the honor of winning the game suffices, and it is therefore a game *par excellence*. With respect to legality, the courts will not entertain any action seeking the enforcement of claims regarding profit or loss in a game. It is purely a matter of honor to ensure that the monetary sum, which serves as the material basis for these hopes and fears, is brought into existence. For this reason, a losing player may not legally seek to reclaim sums already paid out, provided the game itself was otherwise legal, for to do so would be for the courts to lend their support to an act of dishonor. For the same reason, bets cannot be enforced in court unless the stake has been deposited in advance, meaning that payment has already, in practical terms, secured.

bb. Partnership — When two or more persons pool their financial resources or labor in order to conduct a joint enterprise, their objective is not to profit at one another's expense, such that gains and losses are distributed unequally among the parties, but rather to render their losses and gains communal, that is, to share them among themselves. This constitutes the contract of partnership (*societas*). Gans, in his *System of Roman Civil Law*, erroneously classifies it among contracts of indemnity. Mutual insurance against loss does indeed come into play, much as it does in mutual insurance associations, where the mutual distribution and bearing of loss constitute the primary objective. However, in the contract of partnership, insurance against loss is not the original purpose of the undertaking, but the sharing of joint profits. This distribution must be effected in proportion to the respective contributions made, a principle best elucidated in Fourier's theory of association. Otherwise, the partnership is termed a *societas leonina*, an unlawful arrangement in which a single party receives the so-called "lion's share."

β. Exchange of a specific thing for a general thing.

a. To this category belongs the contract of sale (*emptio venditio*), wherein a corporeal object is exchanged for money, serving as the purchase price.

b. If the object of the purchase is a claim, the transaction constitutes an onerous *cessio* (assignment).

c. Finally, one may also purchase "hopes", that is, future gains, and conversely, secure monetary compensation against a future loss, the occurrence of which is inherently uncertain. The former transaction is the lottery, the latter is the insurance contract.

aa. In the lottery, the stake constitutes the purchase price. The ticket, as the embodiment of this potential hope itself, is the object of the contract, and the actual prize as such cannot be the object. Although the lottery shares characteristics with games of chance and betting, positive law has nevertheless treated it differently than those activities, to the extent that states have placed the lottery under its own official protection. The lottery is commonly referred to as a "game" in the same way that *agiotage* (speculative trading) is described as a "lottery on a grand scale." But if winnings from games of chance are not legally enforceable, then why do states lend the lottery the sanction of such a high official authority?
The year 1848 sought to wash away even this immorality, yet the mire of reaction has since washed it back ashore. The immorality lies on the part of the purchaser in the desire to win without working. It is the very same vice that Dante condemns in the usurer, wherein he characterizes labor as the companion of Nature:

And because the usurer takes another path,

He despises Nature herself and her follower [Labor],

Since he places his hope elsewhere. 

On the part of the seller (the state) the immorality lies in the fact that it accepts money, a certainty, while offering the easily beguiled gambler only an uncertainty in return. By contrast, games that consists in the exchange of equivalent bets is devoid of this immorality.

bb. Insurance marks the inception of the demand for mutual security, the guarantee of all by all, precisely as public law ought to provide it. The premium constitutes the purchase price of the exchange. The subject matter of the insurance covers the most diverse nature of contingencies. Alternatively, one may insure the life of a debtor so as not to lose the debt entirely. A parent insures their life to secure a capital sum for their family after their death, and so forth. Such a transaction is a venture only to the extent that one pays more than one receives back, or when the beneficiary may predecease the insured.

γ. The exchange of a generic thing for another generic thing constitutes the *exchange transaction*. 

Firstly, there is actual *money-changing* (the exchange of one currency for another), wherein one type of coin is always treated as a *specific* thing, while the other is treated as *money*: the former being the external currency, and the latter the internal currency. As a result, the foreign currency does not always have the same exchange value and may lose value relative to the internal currency. 

Secondly, the *bill of exchange* has become associated with this act of money-changing. This also involves an exchange of currency types, but in this instance, only the *difference* in value is gained or lost, whereas in reality it has evolved into a mere exchange of *monetary claims* that are legally settled rather than physically paid out. However, the *personal liability* that has come to be attached to this process does not belong in this category, rather, it falls under the category of *security contracts*, serving to reinforce an obligation and thereby bolster confidence in it.

B. Exchange of Use

α. The exchange of the use of a *specific* thing for the use of another *specific* thing constitutes an *innominate contract*, one that the Romans expressed by the phrase *commodo, ut commodes* ("I lend to you, so that you may lend to me").

β. The exchange of the use of a *specific* thing for a *generic* thing, which is essentially the *purchase* of a right of use. From this, certain non-essential distinctions arise:

α. If the use of an *immovable* property is purchased, the Romans termed this *locatio conductio*. We further distinguish between:

aa. The *rental contract* concerning an urban property, where the *rent* constitutes the purchase price. 

bb. The *lease contract* concerning a rural property, where the *lease payment* constitutes the purchase price.

b. If the object is a movable one, this constitutes the gratuitous loan contract (*commodatum*).

γ. The interest-bearing loan (*mutuum*) is the exchange of the use of a fungible thing for money (another fungible thing), but in exchange for the ownership of this money, considered as a specific object (*species*) and as a value. For instance, if four percent interest is paid, this signifies nothing other than that the use of one hundred coins for one year is worth the ownership of four coins.

C. Exchange of Services

a. Service for service is, once again, an innominate contract: *facio ut facias*. Between the exchange of things (*do ut des*) and the exchange of uses, intermediate stages may exist and so a multitude of contracts are conceivable, such as: *do ​​ut facias*, *facio ut des*, etc.

β. The exchange of a service for a fungible thing constitutes the wage contract (*locatio operarum*). It makes no difference to the matter at hand that the service relies more upon intellectual skill, as in the case of a paid mandate or deposit (*depositum*), the fee-based contract between teacher and student, etc.

##### Security

Security contracts (*cautiones*) never appear independently on their own, serving only to complete other contracts, ensuring that the performance necessarily follows from the agreement.

A. If one’s own person (body) is pledged as security, we encounter what is termed the status of a hostage in international law (*præstatio obsidis*). For the sake of the collective, an individual may sacrifice their liberty, provided that they have the liberty to make this sacrifice. But does this apply to civil law as well? Here, the pledging of the body is linked to the liability arising from a bill of exchange, and entails the personal imprisonment of the defaulting debtor, a form of security that is completely disproportionate to the value it is intended to secure. For if the debt is not discharged, such imprisonment for debt may endure for the debtor’s entire life. No one is at liberty to alienate the full scope of their freedom. As a result, as a matter of principle, we cannot endorse imprisonment for debt arising from a bill of exchange, even if such imprisonment was expressly consented to beforehand through the signing of the bill, even if we are unable to propose any other form of equivalent security. 

B. The pledging of an external object constitutes what is referred to as the pledge. The distinction here, once again, lies in the nature of the object that serves as the pledge.

α. If the pledge is a movable object, it is termed a possessory pledge, such as a trust.

β. If it is an immovable object, we designate the proprietary interest in the value thereof, created by the contract, as a *mortgage*. this mortgage, being itself a claim, may also be assigned to third parties.

γ. A claim that I hold against a third party may be directly pledged to my own creditor (*constitutio debiti alieni*), thereby granting them a right to the performance owed by my debtor.

C. Finally, a service may be directly pledged by the interposition of a third party between debtor and creditor, one who assumes the obligation in their stead, should the debtor fail to pay. This constitutes *suretyship*, retaining the right of recourse against the original debtor.

The purpose of contracts of security is to eliminate the element of contingency inherent in contracts, a contingency that stems from the exercise of an arbitrary will. Yet, this arbitrary will, acting as the particular will that posits the universal will, constitutes the very root of the contract. Since this will is capable of deciding in favor of one alternative or the other, it lies within the very nature of the contract that it may not be merely fulfilled, but also terminated.

#### Contract Termination

The various modes of the voluntary dissolution of contracts follows from the conceptual determination and classification of the contracts. The regular annulment of contracts, that which is posited by freedom itself, is fulfillment. In the fulfillment of a contract, the three aspects of the concept (promising, accepting, and performing) exist in harmony, whereas in the case of a breach of contract, this harmony is absent.

##### Unequal Exchange

Since an onerous contract, by virtue of the reciprocity of promise and acceptance, intends for each party to remain the owner of an equivalent value, it follows that the arbitrary will of one party violates the contract if the items exchanged are not equal in value. Therefore, such a contract may be contested by the injured party. However, since value is a quantity of a variable nature, or more precisely, since *use value* does not always correspond to the same *exchange value*, a specific threshold or limit must be established for this correspondence if the contract is not to be deemed null and void. In this regard, existing laws generally stipulate that a contract is void if the value of one item amounts to only half the value of the other. This is the doctrine of *laesio enormis* (enormous injury), originally introduced by Roman law. It goes without saying that it remains at the discretion of the parties to uphold the validity of the contract, and in such a case, a partial gift is deemed to have taken place. Furthermore, one party may, out of personal sentiment or attachment, overestimate the real value of an item (*pretium affectionis*), and if the other party is willing to part with the item only in exchange for this higher price, no objection may be raised against it.

##### Grounds for Non-Performance

Just as a contract may be rescinded due to a subjective defect in the relationship between the contracting parties, so too may the grounds for rescinding a contract lie, secondly, in its *object*, whether be a physical thing or something inalienable. In the former case, the object, being as contingent in nature as the arbitrary will itself, is subject to destruction or alteration. With the destruction of the object, the contract then becomes impossible to fulfill, for the freedom of both parties no longer possesses a concrete reality. However, the question always arises: by what means was the object destroyed? If the destruction was accidental, then positive law must determine who is to bear the loss (*periculum*). The general answer must be that, since ownership has already changed by virtue of the stipulation, the buyer must bear the loss. But if the object was destroyed through the fault of the seller, whether intentionally or by negligence, it goes without saying that the seller must accept responsibility for this fault and compensate the buyer.

Secondly, however, a contract may also be rescinded but because it fundamentally contradicts the very nature of a contract and is, by its nature, incapable of serving as an object of such an agreement. This category includes contracts concerning inalienable objects, such as contracting oneself into slavery. Likewise, an agreement is invalid if I promise to commit a breach of duty, sell my conscience, etc. The Romans collectively classified all such agreements under the rubric of *contractus contra bonos mores* (contracts contrary to good morals). These contracts are null and void because the arbitrary will cannot make itself the master of a higher ethical relationship, i.e. another will.

##### Arbitrary Non-Performance

Since the relation of the performance to the agreement constitutes the third conceptual determination of the contract, and since the arbitrary will is capable of holding these two sides apart, this separation provides a third reason for the dissolution of contracts. Firstly, both parties may agree to dissolve a fully concluded contract, to not allow the performance to follow upon the agreement (*mutuus dissensus*). Secondly, the right to withdraw from the contract may be left open to one or both of the partners (*jus pænitendi*). However, unless the very nature of the contract is to be entirely nullified, this right is usually accompanied by a small compensation of some kind, so that one party does not reap all the benefits from dissolving the contract while retaining no value whatsoever for the other. Yet even in this instance, the dissolution always takes place with the consent of both parties. Thirdly, the entirely arbitrary, unilateral dissolution of the contract, contrary to the will of the other party, takes the form of a delay (*mora*). This occurs when either one party refuses to fulfill their promise (*mora solvendi*) or the other refuses to accept the object (*mora accipiendi*). Delayed performance violently tears apart into a state of mutual hostility between two sides of the contract that had already been brought into harmony within the agreement, thus violating the existence of freedom not only in the external object, but also within the inner sphere of the will. Moreover, it is not just the refusal to fulfill that constitutes a violation of freedom, but also the refusal to accept. For having to gratuitously store something that the other party is entitled to claim at any time is burdensome. Through the non-fulfillment of a contract, the particular, arbitrary will enters into conflict with the universal will, rather than allowing the universal will to naturally arise from within itself. and this we term an injustice, a necessary stage in the development of the sphere of right, for only through conflict can the victory of right over wrong, and of freedom over arbitrariness, be decisively established, and the existence of the rightful will be rendered effective.

### Injustice   

It is not injustice as such that follows the deduction of legality from property and contract, but the restoration of right that comes into effect from injustice. The general existence of freedom, as it appeared in property, still completely excluded arbitrariness, or to put it another way, arbitrariness was entirely in service of freedom. The negative aspect of property, its use or utility, was simply a consequence of property itself, the manifestation of its essence. In contract, the negative aspect of use acquired greater independence in that each person desires to use the property of the other and is willing to exchange their property for the other's. Thus, appearance of freedom in property was no longer restricted and subdued by possession, but the appearance became identical to the essence, and the basis of freedom by extension. To this extent, arbitrariness is now the basis of right and stands on the verge of completely subjugating it, actually appearing as the violation of right instead of its establishment. For if the satisfaction of drives is the end, and property is the means, then it is natural to desire to appropriate the property of others as another means, especially when one's own property is lacking. The satisfaction of drives is then achieved at the expense of property. Arbitrariness takes the place of freedom by negating it, only to then, through its own dialectic, expose itself to be incoherent, thereby allowing freedom to secure itself as the true victor. 

The validation of right, as the positive result of the negation of negation, is the true justification of evil in the world. The pleasant arises only from overcoming the lack and deprivation, and enjoyment is the negation of this negating force, which must be there in order to be negated, but only then can affirmation be brought about. The same applies to to injustice and evil. Activity is the defining characteristic of spiritual life. Since it is the overcoming of a barrier, it requires a counter-movement to achieve its goal. The attainment of purpose requires the purposeless as something to be negated. Right is realized all the more victoriously from overcoming wrong just as good is realized from overcoming evil. Without this negation, neither right nor good would be proven right nor good. Likewise, this does not prevent the purpose of world history from appearing as an ever greater suppression of injustice in general. Each generation of individuals will have to traverse the path of injustice and evil anew. This condition of humanity will never cease, but will reproduce itself in ever new instances, even well after the purpose of world history has been achieved.

The moments of injustice differ according to development of its concept. Injustice in general is the semblance of freedom, arbitrariness that opposes freedom as its negation and violates it. Firstly, this semblance is implicit and has not yet appeared for the will. The right of a person has been violated, but the semblance of right occupies the place of the right such that the actor is ignorant of the semblance, which is the nature of the civil legal dispute. In civil injustice, only the manifestation of right is violated because the actor who violated the other's right mistakes the right to be their own. Secondly, as the converse of the first moment, the essence of the right itself is violated, and the actor knows and intends it. However, the actor makes it appear as if the particular manifestation of right is preserved. The semblance of right is thus no longer implicit, but is explicitly known to the actor. The actor knows they are only exercising a semblance of right, but wants to elevate this semblance to the essence of the right for themselves and others. But since the right itself is violated, the appearance of the right is still an semblance. This is fraud, the semblance of right imposed upon others as a right. Finally, injustice appears in its boundless infinity, explicitly known to the actor and others, without any effort being made to feign the semblance of right. This is crime, where both the essence and appearance of the person is violated. Thus the recognition of right, which was still present in fraud, has disappeared entirely in crime.

#### Civil Injustice

If it is my own fault that I have rendered myself unable to fulfill the contract, then I have infringed upon the other party's property rights for that particular matter. Although I can no longer fulfill my obligation to them, this does not necessarily mean that I have knowingly and intentionally violated their right to property. If I acted unintentionally, then I couldn't have violated their right because it was not my intention to begin with. Even if I damaged the object while I had it in my possession, this also does not necessarily imply malicious intent to violate the right itself. After all, I could have intended from the outset to compensate the owner for my mistake. Since I did not intend to violate the right itself, the only consequence of the damage I caused is the necessity of compensation, i.e. civil damages. If I cannot deliver the same item, I will have to provide another item of the same quality or reimburse the original cost of the item. But the question arises whether compensation must always be paid in the case of an oversight. Roman legal scholars rightly answer that if I am no longer obligated to safeguard the item and the other party has not fulfilled their part of the contract (mora accipiendi), then I can only be held accountable for an oversight I was responsible for (diligentia quam suis). Similarly, damage to another's property can take place in the absence of a contract, in which the case of an oversight is also fully settled by civil damages. However, in the case of malice or personal injury the matter would not be fully settled by civil damages because the right to property would be also be violated. Finally, it is possible that my property causes damage to another entirely against my will, e.g. my cat ​​bites a passerby. In this case, I am at fault for putting another person in danger due to negligence and must compensate them, even if I did not intend to harm them. On the other hand, if the damage arises entirely by accident, even if it directly involved my property, then it is not necessary for me to compensate them because I did not cause the damage.

However, contractual right, as the apparent right that is violated by arbitrariness, stands not only in opposition to arbitrariness, but can also conflict with another contract or even with an original mode of acquisition. Here, we have a collision of legal titles, indicating that there is at least one title that is unjust and arbitrary. For the origin of property, compared to its substantive content, is arbitrary, since it is only one isolated aspect of freedom that stands in contrast to other, higher aspects. The question arises as to which title carries the most weight and would resolve the collision of titles. This is the civil legal dispute, where each party makes a claim on a particular legal ground and only for that ground, e.g. that they found it, or bought it, or was pledged to them, etc. Here, two or more persons lay claim to the same object, but only one of the claims is the truth, i.e. the one has the strongest claim. The person who has the weaker claim is, in principle, unjust if they deny the object to the owner, but at the same, the person does not actually know who has the strongest claim and in no way intends to infringe upon the other's property rights or personal rights. Each person involved wants what is right, but they each mistake their own right to be what is right as such. Each does not deny the other's right to property as a predicate, only that this particular thing belongs to them. In Logic, this is the positive form of a negative judgment, not an infinite judgment. In a negative judgment, one particular is negated while the rest are affirmed, whereas in an infinite judgment, the entirety of the predicate is denied to the subject.

The impartiality of consciousness necessarily belongs to civil litigation, for if the party knows that it is in the wrong and nevertheless initiates litigation or allows it to be initiated, then it is truly in the wrong. Such an act is no longer a civil wrong, but constitutes a criminal offense and is called calumnia in civil litigation, which is impermissible. The actual decision as to which title is superior, however, cannot yet be made here. Possession, as presumed ownership, has all the more the prerequisite content of being the strongest title in its favor. At the same time, that possession can be a preliminary question means that it must be decided first so that one knows who bears the burden of proof, which can potentially negate the claim of the original possessor.

The various ways in which arbitrariness attempts to remedy civil injustice can themselves only be established by it, and thus, instead of remedying injustice, generate new violations of right:

In self-help, an arbitrary will attempts this to restore its own rights without regard for the rights of the other person. However, self-help remains unjust because the other does not agree with it and sees their freedom violated.

Hence, a second, higher form of remedying injustice appears. One arbitrary will, in order not to violate the other, instead seeks to enter into an agreement by way of contract. The civil dispute is to be settled by an arbitrary union of both wills. In this contract, each relinquishes a portion of its claims and transfers them to the other as a settlement. But the settlement does not actually resolve the original injustice. The right that was violated is completely obscured by the settlement and thereby serves unjustice. For whoever was in the right gives up part of their right, and the other, who had no right to begin with, receives a part. The disputed thing belongs to one person, and even if the thing is cannot be divided, each person receives a part of its value in the settlement. It is possible, however, that a right can require equitable distribution. But in this case, the settlement only establishes the right arbitrarily or by accident, not on the basis of law. The settlement is thus a new, a higher contradiction than self-help, because now both parties work together to violate the law.

To avoid this circumstance, the two arbitrary wills now refrain from making the decision themselves. They agree to impart the decision to an impartial third will, which is to examine their titles and determine their validity as an arbitrator. This is the compromise that each party binds and submits themselves to the judgment of the arbitrator, and may also stipulate a conventional penalty, which is forfeited if they do not accept this judgment. But even this threefold arbitrariness does not restore the violated right. The arbitrator decides ex arbitrio or arbitrarily, not according to the law. Likewise, those who submit to this judgment in the moment do not have to remain in agreement with the arbitrator's decision, but can arbitrarily deviate from it at a later time. The attempt to restore the law in this area only entangles it in greater contradictions. Indeed, the arbitrariness that violated the law is decided arbitrarily. Thus, the law is consciously violated and only the appearance that it exists has been preserved. This gives us the transition to the second standpoint of injustice, to fraud.

#### Deception

The semblance of right that arbitrariness seeks to maintain, despite the deliberate violation of the right in itself, is firstly, that the general aspect of property is violated, but its manifestation is not violated. Secondly, the right is evidently violated in its manifestation, but the appearance exists such that the general aspect of property is not violated. Finally, the appearance is established on both sides of the right, where the entire right is established as a semblance.

##### Fraud

If one person is induced by another, through false pretenses, to agree to buy something for more than it is actually worth, then this constitutes a violation of the law because the value for the seller and the value for the buyer are not equivalent. A contract based on fraud would already be null and void, and it goes without saying that civil damages must also be paid in cases of fraud. But this is not enough because the law was violated knowingly and intentionally, which always entails a punishable penalty. Even though there was an agreement of both wills, the agreement was still obtained in bad faith. The value for the buyer is supposed to be derived from the specific quality of the good, that the good is actually authentic. This supposed correspondence, without which the deceived party would not buy the good, is not actually present because the deceiver arbitrarily separates the two. The deceiver conceals the actual value of the good for the buyer and delivers only the appearance of value to the buyer. Even in this outward appearance, there is no essential correspondence between the two parties, but only an apparent one. For if one intends to sell a good for one hundred dollars when they purchased it for a dollar, the other believes the good is actually worth one hundred dollars. The specific quality of the good, which is supposed to be the apparent aspect of value, has not actually appeared, but has only been posited as a semblance of it. The actual right is the universal, the free will, which exists in external appearance, whereas in fraud, my right to contract is violated because in bad faith. If positive law declares fraud to be a misrepresentation of false facts for profit, then the first half of this definition contains the semblance of right, and the second half contains the violation of the right itself.

##### Violence

Just as in fraud, the appearance is created that in the manifestation of ownership the right is not violated, conversely, the appearance can also be created that the right itself is not violated, even if freedom in its outward manifestation is denied, i.e. violence. Here it is presupposed that the content and purpose of the violence itself is meant to be legal. Someone believes they are in their right, that is, that they have the right in itself on their side, and want to make this in itself manifest by, for example, snatching from another person a thing they consider their own, even in violation of that person's personal freedom. Violence, which is not yet a pure crime, can occur in self-help and is its highest form. Here, the appearance of right is not placed on the side of property, for this side has clearly been violated. However, when the perpetrator of violence asserts that only the form of their action is wrong, while in its content his action is right, they make the same fallacy as the deceiver, who likewise believes that the universal and particular can be separated, and that by violating one side, the other side has not also been violated. Just as the deceiver claims that they can violate the essential aspect of property without violating its appearance, so conversely, the one who uses violence believes that they can violate the apparent aspect of freedom without having violated its essential nature. But the actions of both the deceiver and the perpetrator are themselves appearances. Just as the appearance of counterfeit is seemingly untouched when only its essence is supposed to be attacked, so too is the inherent essence of freedom seemingly untouched as soon as its apparent aspect is attacked. The deceiver claims that only the universal, not the particular, is violated, whereas the perpetrator of violence claims that the particular and only the particular, not the universal, is violated. Both claims, however, are empty semblances, a false separation of essence and appearance, of form and content, which are always be connected in philosophy. 

Violence is always wrong, regardless of its content. The way in which violence is overturned, even without the assistance of a judge, is by its own doing. By claiming that they have only attacked the external thing, the person's body, and not the existence of their free will, the perpetrator reveals that they only see the person as a mere thing, not the existence of freedom. But the law that they have applied to others is now applied against them. By their own action, they permit the other to use force against them as well. This is the principle that force can be driven out with force (vis vi repellitur). The first act of force is not permitted, but the second is, since it is the negation that negates the negation. Heroes who found states appear to exercise an original form of violence, but it is a violence against the violence of the state of nature, which outlives its purpose the moment that the state of nature has ended. One can no longer attempt to salvage spiritual education as Nimrod intended and make excuses for state violence, much less permit state power to exercise violence permanently as a regular condition. Violence, like injustice in general, always destroys itself as this semblance of right. For the person who first acted violently will oppose the second act of violence with a third act of violence, and so on endlessly, ensuring that true justice can never be restored.

##### Coercion

Finally, if the appearance of right is based not only on the general aspect of property, but also on the apparent aspect, then we have coercion (metu coactus). Coercion is violence, but not the immediate form of it. Instead, it is a form where the appearance is simultaneously preserved, as if there were an agreement about the external matter, as in fraud. Coercion thus combines the concepts of fraud and violence, the actions of the fox and the hyena, violence concealed by deception. Coercion is the threat of violence where the coercer promises not to follow through with the threat on the condition that the person being coerced makes some sacrifice or performs some action. Both sides of the right are presented as apparent: first, the external act, which is coerced. The person coerced agrees to fulfill the demands of the coercive person against their will. The freedom of the coerced person is violated in its very essence. But because she consents, the appearance of the right is also established internally. The coerced person knows what they are giving up, whereas the deceived person does not know this. But the real contradiction She certainly doesn't need to be forced. Whoever doesn't cling to the external thing by which the coercer seizes them, but relinquishes it, remains free. But then the coercer violates precisely the external thing against which the one whom coercion has been attempted. In each case, an appearance of right is established, the right in itself is violated with the appearance that it is preserved, and the external appearance of voluntariness is present. Because in coercion, the unlawful will completely feigns the appearance of law, utterly undermining and erodes it, the greatest injustice is presented by it whether coercion succeeded or failed. In civil injustice, fraud, and violence, the true restoration of law was only a demand, and it is only a demand in coercion as well. Coercion is abolished by coercion, as we also saw in violence. Coercion proceeds from the violation of something external to the will, and this violation can only be abolished by punishing the perpetrator. Thus, every punishment can be seen as coercion against coercion. Suppose that the criminal violates the freedom of another. In order to compel them not to further violate the existence of freedom, coercion is imposed upon them because they have committed coercion, so that, because of this adverse consequence, they will not commit coercion again. The law has been called coercive, but it only becomes coercive on the condition that the law is violated. The law is not inherently coercive because it is not a positive obligation or command, but is only a prohibition. Therefore, only the person who transgresses the prohibition violates the law can be coerced by punishment. That said, it is at least partially true that the law requires coercion because it is precisely the external existence of freedom that alone permits coercion. Morality, however, as the inner nature of one's intentions, cannot be enforced because it eludes all external influence.

#### Crime

Once right has become a semblance, it has also completely dissolved into this semblance. Crime now steps forth freely and brazenly, without the slightest regard or restraint, and in direct defiance of the injured party’s intent, such that not even the faintest semblance of right remains. Crime is the unfolding of that which was already implicit in coercion. For in coercion, that which is not an existence of freedom is made to appear as an existence of freedom. Arbitrariness now steps forth with the presumption of being the existence of freedom. This is a great contradiction, so we must first set forth the nature of this contradiction through a closer examination of the concept of a criminal offense. Secondly, it will be shown that this contradiction is immediately resolved through retribution.  Thirdly, however, we shall see that, from the standpoint of legality, the realization of this retribution through revenge constitutes a new violation of right, meaning that on this basis, the contradiction remains irresolvable.

##### Criminal Offense

In crime, any semblance of right has vanished. Right is violated in its inner, intrinsic nature, and in its outer, extrinsic nature. In accordance with this twofold perspective, crime entails a twofold violation: one that is intrinsic and limitless, and one that is external and limited. From the latter aspect, the intrinsic nature of the former can be determined.

Viewed from the intrinsic side of right, crime constitutes an endless violation of personality, even if personality is violated only through the medium of an external object. In crime, the existence of freedom is consciously posited as the non-existence of freedom. To commit a crime is tantamount to declaring: "Although I know that your person is free, I refuse to recognize your freedom as the existence of your person." This constitutes a violation of personality in its endless nature, or what logicians term an infinite judgment. In the context of thought, the infinite judgment appears nonsensical. For instance, were I to say, "The table is an elephant," the entire sphere of the predicate is negated with respect to the subject. By committing a crime, even if they did not confess what they did, the criminal enacts this very absurdity. The criminal proceeds as if the person is only a thing and as if the capacity for rights did not pertain to the person. This practical absurdity is all the more heinous when the wrong actually perpetrated is of greater magnitude than one conceived in thought. Because this violation of personality in itself, as the violation of an intrinsic right, remains an endless violation regardless of its outer, phenomenal aspect, it is sometimes held that all crimes are equally wrong. One such mode of contemplation, which can be termed abstract since it disregards the aspect of outward appearance, is the Stoic view, according to which all vices are equal, and there exists only one virtue. According to this view, there is no quantitative distinction between good and evil because every evil act is deemed equally evil. From this same one-sided standpoint also sprang the Draconian legislation, which punished almost all crimes, the slightest no less than the gravest, with death. In every instance, the inner violation of personality was singled out as an endless transgression requiring expiation by an equally endless penalty, such that it has rightly been said of these laws that they were written in blood. Finally, it is this very same standpoint that underlies the medieval elevation of personal honor to a status so endless that even the slightest violation of it could be expiated by nothing other than death, as it was dictated by the (mal)practice of dueling, a relic of medieval barbarism, with its attendant principle of trial by ordeal.

However, crime violates not only the inner aspect of right, but also its outer manifestation. It is one-sided to regard the essence, personality itself, as already being infinite. The personality only becomes infinite only when its appearance corresponds to this essence. For this appearance is itself of a varied specific quality, just as it also varies quantitatively from the highest degree to the lowest. It is to this aspect that the inequality of crimes pertains, and since a crime can only be correctly judged when both of its aspects are identified, a graduated scale of crimes naturally arises, ranging from the most heinous to the most trivial. Thus, there are crimes that are not only an infinite violation of the inner concept of personality, but constitute an infinite violation of the person even in their external existence. Such crimes include murder, enslavement, the slave trade, religious coercion, and the like. These crimes violate rights that encompass the entire scope of the existence of freedom. The lower class of crimes, by contrast, consists of those wherein the violation, directed toward the person's existence as it is embodied in some external object, affects only a limited sphere of freedom. Thus, while the former constitute infinitely great violations, the latter are of a smaller, finite magnitude.

The manifestation of this quantitative aspect renders crimes comparable, much in the same way that money is the universal medium of exchange by means of which all commodities are compared with one another. A physical injury that impairs a single limb or induces temporary illness is certainly a much graver violation than a temporary deprivation of liberty, and the deprivation of liberty is likewise a much graver violation than an infringement of property rights. The inner self-determination of the personality, i.e. its personal freedom, remains the standard of value even in its outer manifestation, and the greater this inner essence is violated within that external form, the greater the criminal offense. The varying magnitude of such crimes can then be appropriately expressed through a shorter or longer deprivation of personal freedom. Yet it is never the external aspect of the damage, but always solely the violation of the personality, manifested here in quantitative terms, that serves as the standard for assessing the crime. This purely external aspect must also be taken into consideration. Every crime entails a certain magnitude of external damage, and if the perpetrator, quite apart from any criminal act, must bear the liability for the damage they caused, then it goes without saying that the criminal must make restitution for the damage. However, this alone does not yet satisfy the demands of justice, rather, the essential requirement is the restoration of the violated personality in its inner aspect. This brings us to concept of punishment.

##### Punishment

Since contradiction and absurdity eliminates itself, the question firstly arises as to how the dialectic that eliminates the crime through the inner nature of the matter itself, secondly, how retribution as an external manifestation presents itself in the quality of the punishment, and finally, how in the severity of a punishment, the substantive equality between crime and punishment is to be maintained, even amidst the qualitative difference between the two.

Since crime constitutes the existence of free will that negates the existence of free will, it thereby articulates the proposition that the existence of free will is capable of being negated. For the criminal, provided they are held legally accountable, is still a rational being capable of free action. The act of their arbitrary will to violate the right of another is therefore presumed to have originated from a rational agent, and in so doing, the criminal has, through their very act, proclaimed the violation of freedom as a right. But this right is none other than *their* right. Only the person who elevates their own arbitrary will to the status of freedom itself is subsequently restricted by the very same arbitrary will, the existence of which they had just declared to be vulnerable. The criminal articulates the negation of the existence of freedom *as* the existence of freedom, i.e. as a right. Therefore, an act of justice is done to them when the existence of *their* freedom is violated, and when they are treated in this manner, they are treated as a free being. The second violation of right, that which follows upon the first and is directed against the violator of right, ceases to be coercion, violence, or injustice in any general sense, but constitutes right itself. Therein lies the foundation of punishment: the second violation of right is not a second evil that is added to the first, it is a demand of right that is grounded in equality. Right demands that the criminal be punished, and through their deed, the criminal has given their consent to this. This is retribution, which constitutes the original basis for punishment, and was also first recognized as such by the Pythagoreans. Crime and punishment are not linked to one another externally, but are inherently interconnected. The second violation of right, conceived of as the negation of the negation of right that positively restores it, constitutes the dialectic of crime. To the extent that the criminal’s arbitrary will is turned back into right itself, the criminal is treated in accordance with their honor and thereby finds in punishment their pacification and reconciliation. The serpent-heads of the goddesses of vengeance that scourge their conscience once the deed is done are finally appeased by punishment, because through it, the eternal principle of justice has taken residence within them. Moreover, all peoples originally made retribution the foundation of their criminal law.

The manner in which the internal reversal of the crime manifests itself is the measure of retribution that flows from the concept of the matter itself. Since justice demands that the criminal suffer precisely what they have inflicted, the magnitude of the second violation of right is determined by that of the first, both qualitatively and quantitatively. This constitutes the so-called *jus talionis*, understood as pure retribution. The only question that arises is whether this equality must always be one that encompasses both quality and quantity, or whether it suffices to apply to punishments a general standard of measurement that quantitatively compares violations of right of the most diverse kinds with one another. Every crime would then be rightfully expiated by a corresponding deprivation of personal liberty, the duration of which would be conditioned by the greater leniency or severity of the confinement, if it is to be commensurate with the crime. The preliminary question that presents itself regarding this mode of punishment is simply this: why do we not adhere to strict retaliation? This is because the strict equality that retaliation demands is impractical. How can the injunction of the Old Testament, "a tooth for a tooth", be carried out if the offender no longer has any teeth? Furthermore, if a one-eyed man were to knock out another person's eye, the *lex talionis* would render him blind, which is a punishment of far greater severity. Athenian law anticipated the converse scenario, mandating that anyone who knocked out the eye of a one-eyed man be punished with the loss of both of his own eyes. Is the pain inflicted by the punishment not invariably doubled? Is mutilation as a form of punishment not, in general, more severe than the very same mutilation inflicted by the crime? Moreover, a crime is not, in its manifestation, an infinite act. Mutilation as a form of punishment, however, constitutes an indelible brand and is thus an infinite injury. A second mutilation would indeed be a second evil added to the first, one cannot punish rape with rape, and so forth. Thus, in many instances, the qualitative equality of crime and punishment is absolutely impossible to implement, which leaves quantitative equality as the only viable alternative. 

Here, the quality (the form of punishment) is a matter of more or less indifference to the law, whereas the quantity (the substance of punishment) must be determined by the right of retribution. But what considerations should influence the specific form of punishment? To this sphere belongs the different theories of criminal law, those that have not arisen from the ground of law itself, but from morality and society. For once the demands of law have been satisfied through quantitative equality of crime and punishment, then considerations of utility, drawn from the broader context of human relations, may determine the qualitative aspect of the punishment.

Once the right to punishment is established, society can then consider the right to rehabilitation:

According to the theory of rehabilitation, the person who committed the crime is seen as an end in themselves, and in this respect, there is nothing objectionable about it. But rehabilitation is not a right exercised by society, but by the family and the school have this right. Nevertheless, rehabilitation has the greatest influence on the manner of punishment. It excludes the death penalty, because death makes rehabilitation impossible. Only if society were to declare a premeditated murderer incorrigible would it permit the death penalty. Otherwise, sentences of imprisonment in reformatories, penal communities, etc., will be appropriate to the theory of rehabilitation. Labor is a particularly effective means of rehabilitation since the will is relinquished from the arbitrariness of its drives.

The incapacitation of the criminal is a greater severity of punishment, which can be offset by a shorter duration, but it is still particularly conducive to rehabilitation and must be applied all the more because the principle of retribution is also reflected in it. For the criminal, by violating the rights of others, has expelled themselves from society, proving their unsociable nature. Through their expulsion from society, they are reminded of justice, but at the same time, they learn to feel the evils of confinement and makes themselves to return to society.

Finally, as far as theories of crime prevention are concerned, it can and must be the purpose of society to protect its members from harm, including from violations of rights. However, the punishment of crimes is only a legal means to this end if the right to punish is already established. But to make punishment a means of preventing crime means to make a person a means for others, taming them as if they were a wild animal. Such a punishment no longer follows from the nature of the act, but is completely external to it. Nevertheless, most professors of criminal law have now turned to this theory, sometimes even wanting to add a touch of retribution. The preventive theory in modern natural law originated with Wolf and Fichte, while Kant established the true doctrine, which in antiquity was defended by Aristotle against Plato, followed by Seneca.

The theory of prevention rests, in its entirety, upon the police perspective, to the extent that the police in our modern states constitutes an institution that is external to the citizenry, serving to remove from society everything that is harmful to the state. This objective can manifest in various forms, thereby giving rise to a multitude of variations within the theory of prevention. We have already identified deterrence as one such form, for the prevention of crimes serves precisely to protect society. The sole question then becomes: by what means are crimes best prevented? Here, the specific form of punishment that most reliably brings about this prevention is favored and deemed to be the form of punishment that is rightfully justified. Proceeding from this premise, the renowned legal scholar Anselm von Feuerbach effectively shifted the entire discipline of jurisprudence into the realm of psychology, viewing the penal provisions within a legal code as a form of threat, one that establishes a psychological compulsion by which the potential offender is deterred from committing a crime. Anselm von Feuerbach states quite explicitly: "The right to punish is the threat." Thus, he termed his doctrine the "Theory of Threat." But from where is the right to issue such a threat derived? One is not at liberty to issue illegal threats. Only when punishment is recognized as a matter of right may the legislator issue threats of its application, otherwise they would abandon the very ground of rational law. The Theory of Threat would naturally favor harsher penalties on the grounds that the sheer terror inspired by such threats would render the citizenry all the more susceptible to being deterred from committing crimes, and so from this premise, the "Theory of Deterrence" appeared. Consequently, in his draft of a Bavarian penal code, Anselm von Feuerbach attempted to institute a system of laws that was almost draconian in nature, a system that subsequently required numerous amendments merely to render it even remotely practicable. Nor is it in very least true that the severity of punishment acts as a deterrent, on the contrary, it has often been observed that it breeds resentment. Against such psychological coercion, human beings can exercise a heroic freedom. As a result, every punishment actually inflicted serves as an indication that the penalty was insufficient, and that the threat itself proved ineffective as a means of psychological coercion.

##### Revenge

However, if we ask who determines the equivalence between crime and punishment, we find that the executor of right, the universal will, is none other than arbitrary choice, the particular will. The arbitrary will of the criminal may as well sense the wrongfulness of their act and their own guilt. But if the criminal fails to accept their punishment, the only recourse that remains is the particular will of the injured party. Since the offender has violated the drive for sociability, the wrath of the offended party is kindled and is elevated to the drive for justice, i.e. the offended party exacts revenge. In terms of its content, revenge is just to the extent that it constitutes retribution and is unjust to the extent that it follows from arbitrary choice. Moreover, since the arbitrary will is driven by passion, the revenge intended is prone to exceeding all measure (as if the slightest offense could be restored by an infinite penalty), ceasing to be a matter of right even in terms of its content. Through the very act of revenge, retribution itself reverts to a crime, bringing about a new violation of a different right.

Through this turn of events, the respective positions are reversed: the offended party becomes the criminal, while the offender becomes the injured party. The latter, in turn, now exacts revenge, acting out of their own arbitrary will and without observing any measure for the penalty enacted, and so the cycle continues. Every new act of revenge engenders a new crime. The roles of the two sides are perpetually inverted, and so we witness an endless progression of revenge, a cycle that is inherited from generation to generation. Vestiges of this vengeful spirit can also be found within organized civil society, specifically when crimes are not considered a public matter, but rather a private matter. That is to say, when it is not open to anyone to bring an accusation, nor may the judge proceed ex officio, nor is there a public prosecutor present, but only that the aggrieved party or their nearest kin are entitled to pursue redress for the wrong committed. Other crimes are, of course, by their very nature matters for individual complaint, e.g. defamation and adultery.

With the tendency of vengeance to spiral endlessly, the inherent contradiction of revenge is now brought to its absolute climax. While it is true that the arbitrary act of vengeance restores the principle of right at any given moment, in the very same moment that the right is restored, another right is violated and becomes a crime. Thus, within the sphere of legality, right cannot be restored by means of revenge. Here, right exists purely as a sentiment and drive for vengeance that dwells within the spirit. Right thus discovers an entirely different, novel foundation for its existence, namely, within the inner self of the acting subject. It is from the subject’s own arbitrary will that the universal reality of freedom appears, yet it remains confined within this "magic circle" of its own creation, unable to break through its boundaries. The objective freedom of the individual has now been supplanted by their subjective freedom — that is, by the standpoint of morality.

## Morality

In morality, freedom attains an existence that is appropriate to its inner concept. For property, the existence of freedom depended upon the ownership of external things, and for contract, the existence of freedom depended upon another will, which only ever posited freedom as an external existent. But for injustice, since the criminal arbitrariness of one generated revenge in the other, freedom now exists as the knowledge of freedom for spirit. Not only does the injured party, through the deed of the offender, attain an inner sense of justice, but, as the injured party carries out revenge, this inner existence of freedom also arises in the offender. The arbitrariness of both parties is transformed into the universal existence of freedom, which finds its object in itself, no longer in or as an external thing. The subjective freedom of the moral standpoint must now demonstrate its objective existence just as the objective freedom of the legal standpoint demonstrated its subjectivity. To this end, both standpoints must be compared more closely. For both standpoints, the free will manifests itself as the will that wills itself. Thus, the formal freedom of guilt and imputation cannot be absent from either standpoint. Without formal freedom, actions are neither legal nor moral. The difference between the two standpoints, however, is determined by the content of what formal freedom has as its object.

In legality, the content is drawn from nature. Freedom manifests itself in a thing that is not itself free, but serves the will, so the standpoint of legality is moreso concerned with the manifestation of freedom in the thing. Legal attribution is therefore different from moral attribution in that the former only depends on whether the harm contained by the intention exists or not. But since the entire content of the existence of freedom is now determined from within, the spirit seeks its freedom in its relation to its own conditions. Spirit is only free when it posits itself as free and liberates itself, purposing the unfree conditions in it in order to realize the objective existence of its freedom, i.e. the manifold of drives within the will. Since in legality only the formal intention is what matters, in morality the primarily concern is about the material intention of the action, the motives and purposes behind it, in short, the inner nature or character of the subject. The contrast between the two standpoints can thus be described in one phrase: if in the moral standpoint the impersonal existence of freedom is irrelevant, then in the legal standpoint the personal existence of freedom is irrelevant. Legality does not concern itself with the inner motivations and purposes within the will since it only needs to know whether a certain right has been violated. Hence, only now does it become clear that legality has nothing to do with affirmative commandments and duties. Apart from the external existence of freedom in which I am permitted and whose violation in the other is forbidden to me, regardless of the motives of my action, I am free to determine myself as I wish. However, if the will is to make itself the objective existence of universal freedom, it is incumbent upon it to elevate its drives to the universal content of freedom. This universal content, flowing from the creative source of its own inner being, is the Idea of ​​the Good, which strives to shed its subjectivity by making itself objective. The Idea of the Good generates the strengths of the moral standpoint, but also its weaknesses. The shortcoming of the moral standpoint is simply that, because the objectivity and generality of purpose is strictly subjective, it forgoes any external guideline, permitting any purpose to be arbitrarily chosen and discarded. The moral standpoint consists precisely in overcoming this contradiction to that it necessarily transforms and reconciles itself with objective freedom.

The path to this is marked by the threefold division of the moral standpoint. On the condition that the drives and their satisfaction in well-being are no longer indifferent to each other, as they were in legality, but are to be transformed into the universal existence of freedom to realize the good, then this can have no other meaning than the coordination of the drives by the rational will. The inner disposition of an agent that drives them to do good is virtue. The correspondence of the drives to reason still leaves the drives with an affirmative meaning. However, the drives preserve their independence from reason by demanding action. This command to do good solely for the sake of the good is duty. But since duty would be empty without the content of that the drives provide, they must be utilized as a means to an end of any dutiful action. As a means, however, the drive remains indeterminate and can be shaped in whatever way is the most amenable to achieving the goal. This indecisiveness must be eliminated, and so the final decision must be made by conscience.

### Virtue

As the elevation of the drives to reason, every virtue presupposes an action based on knowledge. We do not follow our drives as such, but with clear insight and firm values. But not every action that is based on knowledge is virtuous, only actions that have the universal existence of freedom as its goal. Any agent can easily set the arbitrary gratification of any particular drive to be their goal, which also requires knowledge to achieve. But when the drive is made to be an end in itself, the knowledge that serves this activity is obtained in bad faith and is all the more vicious the more stubbornly and consistently an agent carries out their goal. In virtue, however, the drive is determined by reason, nature is transformed into a higher, second nature that is called habit. Virtue is therefore a habit of doing good, a firmly established skill that emanates from the character of the person, a deliberate skill of determining oneself according to reason, and with the perfection of this skill, the inner disposition of the good is perfected as well.

But in order to specify how the drives are determined by reason as virtues, we must first see how they behave when reason does not determine it. As a pure drive, it has no determination in itself, but is determined only by something else, i.e. it is indeterminate, has an indeterminate determination, or in other words, it is determined only quantitatively. In this way, the drive is capable of increasing and decreasing infinitely and can be satisfied in infinitely many degrees, either too much or too little. The determination of the drive by the reason that governs it can therefore only consist in limiting off its indeterminate progression into quantitative infinity. Reason avoids the outermost limits of magnitude, the infinitely large and the infinitely small, so that every virtue is limited by two vices, the excess and deficiency of the drive. Virtue is thus the moderation of the drive, its reduction to a definite limit.

If we want to arrive at a more precise definition of virtuous skill, we must describe more accurately the mean that the virtuous person must strive for. In the conventional meaning, the mean is that which is equidistant from both extremes, and so is always one and the same. But the virtuous mean is only a mean in the relative sense. Between two and ten, six is ​​the unconditional, objective mean. But if two pounds of meat is too little to eat, and ten pounds is too much, it does not follow that six pounds is the right mean for everyone. If it is too much for one person, it may be too little for, say, an athlete. Furthermore, that extreme to which we are most naturally drawn is for us the furthest removed from the mean. Therefore, in order to find the right mean, which is not easy, we must move furthest away from this extreme. Thus, virtue is the deliberate skill that maintains the mean for us. But since the mean relates back to the drives, this implies there are as many virtues as there are drives.

#### Personal Virtues

##### Temperance and Courage

The drive for pleasure appears in a twofold form, depending on whether it presents itself from the positive or the negative side. One is the pursuit of pleasure, the other the avoidance of pain, and these two sides correspond to the virtues of temperance and courage.

Temperance relates to the sensual pleasure derived from the interactive senses of touch and taste. But even with taste, the vice of intemperance, which overindulges in pleasure, it is purely concerned with the pleasantness of the sensation, irrespective of its origin. Likewise, it is not the common feeling that the immoderate seek, but the intensity of feeling that gives them a rush. The other vice, the opposite of virtue, could be called dispassion or deprivation, which ignores or abstains from pleasure altogether. In Christianity, temperance is the highest virtue, because here only the excess of the drive is considered evil. Because we strive more for sensual pleasure than we avoid it, the excess of pleasure is a much worse condition than the lack of it, hence why the other extreme of dispassion is at least free from the endlessness of craving.

The virtue that relates to fleeing pain is courage. Because we fear pain, we flee from it. The correct mean exists between the passion of fear, and its opposite, fearlessness. The most terrible of all evils, however, is death, and the noblest of the dangers into which our lives are plunged is war, so the virtue of courage relates primarily to these and similar forms of death. There are also different forms that courage can take, such as heroism, resilience, etc. The excess of fear (or lack of fearlessness) is the vice of cowardice, while recklessness falls into the opposite faults of these two states. By itself, recklessness is somewhat closer to courage because fleeing pain is more natural to us than subjecting ourselves to it. However, it is easily and very often combined with the other extreme. Reckless people are hasty and impulsive before danger, but they flee when it actually appears. Brave people, on the other hand, are the reverse, they are persistent in danger and are calm beforehand. Courageousness encompasses resilience, but not vice versa. Courage further presupposes the good as its purpose and seeks to avoid shame, since the brave person would rather die than do something shameful. While temperance still included sensual pleasure as its purpose, the pleasantness of courage is of a much more spiritual nature.

##### Generosity and Magnanimity

The virtues that relate to the drive for possession no longer relate to immediate feelings and their degrees of intensity, but relate to the activity of receiving or giving possessions, an activity that becomes universal in the drive for money.

Generosity maintains the mean in the everyday expenditures of ordinary life, although it relates more to giving than to receiving, as the New Testament declares the former to be more blessed than the latter. However, giving only has this significance because of receiving, and being receptive to the gifts of others is generous. The vice of greed is an excess of receiving and a deficiency of giving. If the former predominates, it is pure greed or rapacity, but if the latter, then it is avarice or stinginess. If the spendthrift spends too much while taking too little, they eventually fall back into taking too much and spending too little, otherwise they would lack the means for excessive spending. Because spending is more dignified than taking, extravagance is somewhat closer to the mean and can be brought back to the mean more easily through experience and age, whereas avarice and stinginess tend to increase with age.

Magnificence differs from generosity in that its object does not concern the management of money in general, but rather relates to costly expenditures, especially on festive occasions. The love of magnificence thus involves expenditure on things worthy of it, but the grandeur is always proportionate to the circumstance. Thus, the mean is not merely an action, but as a relationship between the action and its object. Therefore, when evaluating this virtue, the actor, the place, the time, and the objects are all relevant. The vice of deficiency is also stinginess, the vice of excess is ostentation. The objects of this virtue are public monuments, celebrations, ceremonies, theatrical performances, etc., which were carried out by individuals in ancient times, for example, in the Athenian liturgies. The miser, even after expending large sums, spoils the beauty of the entire work abandoning it early and leaving it incomplete. They always try to spend as little as possible on it, and each time lament the expense and consider it excessive. The ostentatious person, on the other hand, will fund something excessive simply to show that they can, such as preparing an extravagant feast at a wedding just to show off their wealth.

##### Ambition and Humility

The drive for honor provides us with two virtues that have precisely the relationship between generosity and magnificence, in that one relates to personal honor, whereas the other relates to social honor.

The love of personal honor has to do with ambition or achieving things for yourself. We denote the vice of excess as overambition or lust, while the vice of deficiency we denote as underambition or sloth. But since this is only the negation of excess, it can also serve to denote the proper mean of ambition, just as the word ambitious can either imply an excess or proper mean of ambition.

The love of social honor, on the other hand, has to do with being recognized and respected by others as virtuous, whether on the basis of magnanimous or generous actions. Whoever loves social honor believes themselves worthy of great things, even if they are not actually worthy of them. Of the two vices, each possesses only one part of the aspects just mentioned. The pompous or arrogant person considers themselves worthy of greatness without actually being considered worthy, whereas the diffident person is considered worthy of greatness without considering themselves worthy. The humble person possess both elements of the relationship to the highest degree, but in terms of how they conduct themselves, they stand in the middle. The recognition and self-recognition of worthiness of the humble person are in balance. The extremes of excess and deficiency are, in the first case, the worth does not reach the appreciation, while in the second, it surpasses it. These vices, like those opposed to magnanimity, are also founded on a disproportion, but between feelings and actions on the one hand, and the objective worth of the person on the other. If the humble person considers themselves worthy of great things, they will desire them. The most valuable of good is honor, and so the magnanimous person will consider this honor to be the most valuable, followed by prestige, wealth, etc. The vice of diffidence is further from the mean than arrogance in that while the arrogant person is just ridiculous, the diffident person actively rejects recognition by making themselves appear uninteresting.

#### Social Virtues

##### Gentleness

The mean in relation to the passion of anger is gentleness, which may appear to be on the side of deficiency, but it is still considered the mean since the negation of anger presupposes its existence. Here, the excess and lack pertain to the inappropriate expression and responsiveness of anger. For excess and lack, we distinguish between a quick temper, which quickly flares up and erupts, but then easily dissipates again, from resentment, which slowly and privately builds up anger over a long time, but never lets go of it.

##### Wit / Friendliness / Honesty

Following the virtues relating to benevolence and its practice, that is, to friendship, these are called the sociable virtues, which manifest themselves in actions and speech.

The first sociable virtue, which has as its object the pleasant things that people want to do with each other, such as recreational activities and fun conversations during leisure, is wittiness. The jester takes this to an extreme by making fun out of everything and taking nothing seriously. By contrast, the boorish person taking fun out of everything and making nothing pleasant.

The second sociable virtue is friendliness, relating to the comforts of life that people obtain for one another in their interactions, seeking mutual advantage, the recognition of the other and the enhancement of their own self-esteem. Excessive friendliness consists in approving everything about the other person, never contradicting them, and always believing that one must grant everyone praise and advantage, even if it is accompanied by the greatest inconveniences on the part of the person acting, i.e. obsequiousness. Additionally, if a self-serving intention is added to this, seeking to gain an advantage for oneself through this behavior, then this is called flattery. The opposite vice consists of being unfriendly to people, always contradicting them, starting conflicts, excessive criticism, and generally causing people frustration and pain (whether they enjoy doing so or not). This is the character of a contrarian. The right mean we can call sociability or friendliness, it is the right way of interacting with people and comes closest to friendship, the only difference being that friendship requires loyalty and commitment in a way that friendliness does not.

The third sociable virtue relates to the devotion of one person to another through word and deed. Honesty holds the mean in the drive to communicate and is the correct relationship between speech and action. Boasting, as the side of excess, ascribes to oneself advantages that one does not possess. If a profit-seeking intention is added to this, then we have charlatanry and quackery. Irony, which is the side of deficiency, conversely denies the advantages that one actually possesses. This character already presupposes a finer sense of decorum than boastfulness. When used for good purposes, irony brings others  to the right mean of honesty by exposing their dishonesty. Socrates himself had a good purpose for this since he wanted to awaken in others the insight that they did not know what they thought they knew.

##### Justice

The drive for justice, in which personal and sociable ends are in perfect harmony, then forms the basis of the highest sociable virtue that is itself justice. This balance of the personal and the sociable presupposes the equality of the persons involved, and this equality is the first characteristic of justice. Justice is the virtuous disposition to treat others as equals in the satisfaction of their own drives. In this relation to the other, however, justice is not merely virtue in general, as in the skill and disposition of being good, but is the exercise of justice for the other. Since this virtue must benefit the other, it appears to be not only the good of the agent, but also the good of the one to whom the action relates. Finally, the fraternity that justice demands has its basis in the fact that it transcends the limitation of private law by relating individuals to the community and thereby to public law.

Since all legal rights and moral duties are to be exercised in civil society, justice is, first and foremost, the universal virtue that contains all other virtues, bringing the drives into harmony with reason. Justice in this sense consists in observing the laws of civil society that protect and promote the virtues of its citizens. Just as justice as such can be called legality, so too can its opposite, injustice, be called illegality, which encompasses all crimes and vices just as justice encompasses all rights and virtues. However, justice is also much more than legality, in that it not only strives to act in accordance with the law, but that this very striving arises from their own moral conviction.

The particular moments of justice, which are limited to legality, is the equality of two or more individual persons with respect to the objects of private law, applying narrowly only to property, contract, and crime.

Justice with respect to property consists in the fact that each person receives as much as they give. This form of justice is distributive justice. It is a proper ratio between the quantity and quality of goods. Its exercise consists in bringing about this proper ratio, not only for oneself, but also for another. Distributive justice requires at least four elements, two persons and two things, and determines the geometric ratio of them. If person A relates to person B, then thing C relate to thing D, so the mean is equality AD = BC. Distributive injustice consists of the distributing party either giving themselves or a favored party more than what they deserve (and implies the third party receives less than what they deserve) is indeed a twofold extreme of too much and too little. But these two extremes are not two different vices, but are only the two inseparable aspects of unjust possession.

If all goods are distributed according to the contribution, and the parties of a contract want to make an exchange, then justice lies in the equality of what is exchanged and injustice lies in the inequality of what is exchanged, that one gains from the loss of another. This form of justice is corrective justice. The task of corrective justice is to find the mean ratio between excess and deficiency in the distribution of things. Since distributive justice has already established the correct ratio, and it is now only a matter of restoring the disrupted ratio. Hence, the ratio here is not geometric, but arithmetic.

Finally, if a crime has been committed, then the task of punitive justice is to determine the correct punishment for the crime. It only remains for us to define this correct measure more precisely from a moral perspective. For it cannot now merely depend on the compensation for the external damage, as in reparative justice, but consideration must also be given to the intent from which the external deed followed, which is relevant in distributive justice. Retribution establishes the connection between distributive and reparative justice. Apart from the relationship between intent and action, Aristotle demanded an inequality of punishment depending on whether the wrongdoer has a higher or lower dignity, but this completely contradicts and defeats the purpose of the equality of all people before the law.

Having descended from general justice to particular justice, this particularity can also be conceived as something general, which must always be further defined and modified by the specific circumstances since all actions relate to the individual case. This justice of the individual case we call equity, or as the Romans say, ex æquo et bono. The law cannot take all individual circumstances into account and can only decide in a general way. Thus, the correct perspective that upholds the general in each particular case, even if it is legally contradicted, is equity, which substitutes a higher right for the lower one. Since equity corrects the drive for justice through pure reason, the drive as such has ceased to be the material basis of the virtues, and so we must consider the rational virtues of knowledge or reason itself.

#### Rational Virtues

The rational virtues will differ from one another to the extent that knowledge is, on the one hand, the source of the mean established by reason in the practical drives, and on the other hand, the pure activity of cognition itself. Thus, the rational virtues are partly virtues of the understanding, and partly virtues of reason.

##### Prudence

Just as justice proved itself to be a universal virtue conceived in its exercise towards others, the excellence of having this knowledge itself, by virtue of which it finds the right mean in all practical virtues, is prudence. This is also a universal virtue, though it is conceived from the perspective of understanding as opposed to action. Prudence is a complete inference of the understanding, it establishes the principle for doing good as the major premise, it recognizes the means that are useful for carrying out this end as the minor premise, and performs the action that realizes the principle as the conclusion. Prudence is therefore a certitude of character, as described previously in the drive for knowledge, that is necessary condition for realizing any practical virtue. For what good is a virtue if the motivation and ability that puts it into practice are lacking? If we then ask what vices opposed to prudence is, Aristotle summarizes it very well as an excess and lack of certitude. The unwavering certainty of character, even for evil purposes, is clearly the vice of excess, whereas as the lack of certainty of character, even for good purposes, is the vice of deficiency. The first is arrogance, the second is naiveté, and prudence is right mean between them.

##### Judiciousness / Counsel / Insight

If we distinguish more closely the different aspects that define prudence, this gives us the particular virtues of the understanding.

The correct understanding of a good purpose, stated as a major premise, is judiciousness. However, as only one aspect of prudence, it also differs essentially from prudence as whole. Judiciousness pertains to the evaluation and recognition of good purposes as opposed to their execution.

The skill of the understanding to find the right means for a good purpose is counsel. It recognizes that a means can only serve a good purpose if the means is itself good, disproving the proposition that the end justifies the means. Good counsel formids a bad means to be used for a good end just as it forbids a good means to be used for a bad end. Such person can give correct advice, but still be poorly advised.

Lastly, since in action, everything depends on the individual case and the correct judgment of the circumstances, the virtue of reason that knows the distinction of good from evil in each individual case is insight. Since all virtues converge in justice, and equity is the correct knowledge of what is just in each individual case, insight is nothing other than the correct judgment of what is equitable. Hence we can also use a related word, leniency, to indicate when a punishment should be waived for equitable reasons.

##### Wisdom

Finally, the virtue of thinking that no longer relates to the changing circumstances of human actions, but to the eternal ground of truth, is called wisdom. When Aristotle calls this virtue the most perfect, self-sufficient, and and blessed, this is justified by the fact that in it the harmony of personal and social virtues are most completely and universally established, and the standpoint of the unconditioned, where subject and object coincide, is grasped as speculative knowledge. All the drives and inclinations of practical life, everything that relates to the finite being of humankind, recedes into the background with this virtue. Its drive is the unconditioned drive for knowledge, and so this virtue relates only to the previous conditioned drives as the higher negation of them. The good is no longer a measure of desire, but struggles against desire as an obstacle to its realization. This is the standpoint of the doctrine of duties, as it is particularly developed in Stoic philosophy and Christian morality.

### Duty

Since the drive is no longer, from the standpoint of virtue in general, limited to a certain measure, but is completely negated so that the good may be accomplished, duty is essentially without content, and only becomes a system of duties when the content of the drives are introduced as a means to fulfill the duty for its own sake. But since many different means can lead to the same end, the choice of means under different circumstances and duties in casuistry comes into play here. To escape the conflict of duties, the insight acquired through training the drives toward the good must decide which duty is preferable in each case, i.e. asceticism.

#### System of Duties

Initially it may not be obvious how a multiplicity of duties is possible. The multiplicity of virtues arose from the diversity of the underlying drives, and without them, the good appears abstract and simplistic. Duty for its own sake is only one, hence why the Church Fathers considered the many virtues of the ancients to be vices. The virtue of being angry at the right time has now become a vice, so if anyone wants to take your shirt, you hand over your coat as well (Matthew 5:40). Magnanimity bows before altruism, temperance gives way to abstinence, and so on. Thus, the satisfaction of drives or happiness are no longer in beautiful harmony with the highest good. The harmony lost in this life is to be restored in the next, making happiness in the next life the consequence of virtue in this life. But this idea ignores that morality itself already contains inner satisfaction as true happiness. When one turns to a specific duty that gives a general duty its content, then one is faced with nothing but a maxim for happiness, i.e. the duty gives a purpose to the drive. Duty rejects the basis for its own existence, which is the true reason for the immorality of this moral standpoint. Since duty for its own sake remains in the indeterminate generality of inactive contemplation, whereas morality necessitates action that relates to a drive, pure duty cannot do without drive. Since universal duty aims to carry out the good, the purpose of reason, all rational beings, myself and others, are all objects of duty. This is reflected in the division of duties into personal and social. 

##### Personal Duties

The personal duties, or the duties to oneself, states that, since the individual is a means to the end of reason, they should preserve themselves as such and continue to develop physically and mentally in this regard. The duty of self-preservation is therefore not different from the drive of self-preservation in content, but in its motivation. The same applies to the relationship of the other duties to their corresponding drives, so that we can be briefer here overall, because no new content is introduced. Just as the classification of personal drives is threefold, so too are three duties distinguished here: to preserve one's life, one's property, and one's honor.

Firstly, the duty to preserve one's own life follows the prohibition of suicide. Although life is a means to the end of reason, it may nevertheless be sacrificed in save the life of another. Otherwise, to kill oneself means to destroy the purpose of reason to which the individual belongs as a participating member. One might call suicide braver than bearing the evils of this world because they are too cowardly to accept the pain of death, but this completely ignores that the person endures suffering for the sake of duty, which shows that they have courage that far greater than courage to face death. The negative duty not to destroy one's life corresponds to the positive duty to prolong it as much as possible through a with a good diet, exercise, health care, temperance, etc. Secondly, the obligation to maintain and preserve one's property commands industriousness, diligence, thrift, and good management. Finally, what remains is the duty to preserve one's honor. Yet, honor rests on the worthiness of a person to be a suitable means to the moral law, and so the drive for honor becomes here the duty of self-respect. A person must respect themselves enough not to act immorally. But since the person is also aware that they need to have the drive to act morally, and that the drive is simultaneously inappropriate to the moral law, the converse duty of humility arises from this fact.

Furthermore, if person is not as they should be to be a means to the end of reason, they must first make themselves suitable for this purpose, i.e. improve themselves. The nature of self-improvement varies from physical improvement, through training of the body, to intellectual improvement, through training of the mind, and finally to moral improvement, through the development of good habits in general. The activity of self-improvement as a whole becomes the epitome of happiness, which must now itself be used as a means to fulfill duty. This is the duty of preserving one's happiness, connecting the first two moments of personal duties to each other. One duty that accomplishes this is to endure happiness, so as not to make the pleasure it provides an end in itself. This is no doubt one of the most difficult duties since pleasure as such can be misleading. Moreover, the other duty that accomplishes this is patience, composure, and steadfastness in misfortune, so as not to be deterred from fulfilling one's duties. Thus, even in misfortune, a person will retains their happiness, as the contentment that flows from the awareness of fulfilled duty, and prepares one for the fulfillment of new duties.

##### Social Duties

The social duties, or duties to one's neighbor, are partly negative and partly positive, the latter of which relates either to the physical well-being of others or to their spiritual well-being.

The negative duties toward one's neighbor are duties of justice or coercion: to neither infringe upon their life, health, and freedom, nor to damage or injure their property, nor finally to harm their character through slander, lies, defamation, mockery, etc.

The positive duties towards one's neighbor are duties of love, which as positive ones, are meant to promote the well-being of the other, are called duties of conscience or imperfect duties: these include benevolence, service, truthfulness, gratitude, appreciation, sympathy, harmony and peaceability, forbearance, tolerance and reconciliation, even love of enemies, and finally courtesy, approachability, etc.

Ultimately, the social duties as a totality must only be seen as means to demonstrate the moral law to each individual. Everyone thus has the duty to promote the moral improvement of others, partly through admonition to the good, and partly by setting a good example.

##### Moral Universe

Since everyone has the idea of moral perfection of all as their goal, the standpoint of God as the moral world order is established. As the third link in the system of duties, this is duty towards God. However, God is the object of all duties in that the creation of the moral world order is the end goal of each and every duty. There can be no special duties toward this unconditional world order, unless we were to count our own adequacy / inadequacy to the moral world order to be a duty to love / fear God. The confidence that the individual can conform to this ultimate purpose by the power of their own will is to trust the divine world order, as the belief that reason will prevail more and more over time. But since this is merely a belief, the will can be deceived in certain cases. Likewise, the fulfillment of a certain duty as a means to an end must yield to another duty when the latter proves itself to be a better means and thus renders the former immoral. The conclusion of the general system of duties is casuistry, the notion that no one duty can endure on its own, and in their mutual struggle, one eventually supercedes the other.

#### Casuistry

Because the specific content of duty is added externally, it can only be determined by external reasons whose content is included for each case. Individual cases are thus presented, and since the external reasons are as manifold as the external content itself that is to be elevated to the concept of duty, the intellect has a broad scope here to elevate the specific content to the concept of duty. This is casuistry, a method first invented by the Stoics, taken to its extreme by the Jesuits and cast the latter's morality in such a detrimental light because, like the Sophists, they were enabled by their sophmoric education to present any content whatsoever as a duty. Here it becomes apparent that the negation of the drive is not taken seriously, but on the contrary, inclination and drive are precisely made the determining factor for duty. Since duties against the divine world order cannot be subject to casuistry, as it purely concerns the general and ultimate purpose of duty as such, there can only be struggles between duties to oneself, between duties to one's neighbor, and finally, the struggle between both.

##### Conflict of Personal Duties

Regarding the conflict of personal duties, self-preservation takes precedence over perfection because it is its necessary condition. Self-fulfillment, since it is the consequence of the other two duties, is secondary to and derived from them. However, since it is also a means to fulfilling duties in general, it also takes precedence. If we consider the specifics, life takes precedence over all other matters because it is their ultimate condition, with the only exception that life may be sacrificed to save another life. But to sacrifice one's own life for the sake of honor is absurd, since death does not prove honor or dishonor. Honor depends on circumstances that are accidental and typically forgoes the contemporary recognition in bringing about a better future, so the person must prefer life to honor, since it is the means to fulfill their destiny, which is to bring about this future. But when backward-looking ministers, place honor in being unpopular with their people for the sake of restoring the past, this defiance of recognition becomes ridiculous. Kant rightly wants to allow the duty of thrift to apply only when all other duties to oneself, including happiness, have been fulfilled. In the pursuit of self-improvement and happiness, the intellect can more easily decide what is right for it. 

##### Conflict of Social Duties

In general, regarding the conflict of social duties, we can say that the question of to whom I have a duty towards can be answered in very different ways. If we compare the social duties to one another, the duties of justice take precedence over the duties of love, since no right intention does not excuse wrong action, such as when Saint Crispinus stole leather to make shoes for the poor. Likewise, one must pay what they owe before giving gifts to others. The duty to one's neighbor, though it is itself subordinate to the duty of justice, nevertheless takes precedence over the duty of love. For instance, an act of charity should not be performed if one knows that the recipient has bad intentions. Charity causes the worst kind of casuistry because is often impossible to know if your donation will benefit someone or not, as well as how much you should donate.

As for the struggles within the individual duties to one's neighbor, it is unproblematic that I can trespass the property of my neighbor to save them if their life was in danger. But what about when honor and life come into conflict? Are we permitted, in the so-called "lie of necessity," to save another's life with a lie? Saint Augustine does not permit it, not even for the good of all humankind. Fichte claims that we should mislead someone who is pursuing a hidden person with drawn weapons, in order to lead them astray from the trail of murder. Fichte replies that one can indeed say, "I do not know his whereabouts," even if the pursuer's fury turns against the speaker. Socrates permits a lie tha would lead an army to victory, just as he permits stealing weapons so that a friend does not commit suicide. But with certain social duties, such as the duties of love or friendship, one wonders whether it is even necessary to lie at all. Should one tell the truth to a poet who asks for our judgment, even if knows that this will anger them to the utmost? Who, says Kant, always has the wit of an evasive answer at hand? Kant, knowing the fluctuating nature of casuistry, remains silent on the matter. But if one considers that one telling the truth would dissuade the poet from writing terrible poetry, then being honest about what you think would actually to the poet's benefit and so it would not even be necessary to lie.

##### Conflict of Personal and Social Duties

When personal duties and social duties collide, we must first decide, assuming equality of the object, what duty takes precedence for the person. If two people cling to a plank after a shipwreck, and the plank can only support one, it cannot be decided whether the duty to oneself or to one's neighbor takes precedence because both shipwrecked people will perish in either case. To answer this question is to measure the life of each person as worth more or less than another's, which is itself an arbitrary and selfish judgment on part of the measurer. There is always the option to refuse to take action and leave the outcome to chance, even if it contradicts the proposition that the moral law must command something at every moment. Of course, it can be argued that the moral law in this case commands you to do nothing (or that it forbids you from action). One's own and another's perfection can never be in conflict, and both explicitly coincide in the duty to set a good example. With regard to various duties to oneself and to one's neighbor, the duty of self-fulfillment will undoubtedly take a back seat to the duties of justice. For instance, it is not permissible to enjoy a pleasure that inflicts injustice on others. Thus, only three kinds of collisions remain: first, between self-preservation and justice, second, between self-preservation and love, and third, between self-fulfillment and love.

We have already described two cases in which the conflict between self-preservation and justice was decided in favor of the former, partly in self-defense to preserve my own life, and partly in theft during an emergency. Furthermore, there is also the case in the beneficium competentiæ, where the creditor must leave their property to the debtor in order to secure latter's livelihood. The Jesuits even go so far as to declare the life of another person inviolable if they are protecting their property. Conflict between persons brings not only one's honor and life into conflict, but also the honor and life of another. Here it must be noted that the honor of the insulted party is not completely restored by taking revenge on the insulter. The action restores outward honor, or honor in the eyes of others, but not inward honor. With this, casuistry is once again granted considerable scope. If someone attacks my honor, the Jesuits permit going as far as lying and slander. Fichte only allows the slander of the other to be exposed, even at the expense of their honor.

In the struggle between self-preservation and love, it is clear that depriving oneself of necessities is not a necessary condition of doing good for others. But if one can only give from the surplus of what one already has, then what qualifies as a surplus? The indeterminacy of this concept has been exploited by casuists to almost completely banish the duty of giving. Kant presents a more playful collision between self-esteem and courtesy in that traditional gestures and phrases of politeness can seem like dishonest groveling. However, he notes, that this can become serious, as "he who makes himself a worm cannot complain afterwards if people step on him."

When self-fulfillment and love finally come into conflict, the former seems to take a back seat, since we even sacrifice our own property for the well-being of the people we love. But here the antinomy is that no one can partake in pleasure as long as there is another human being in pain, whichever is the case. If the rich only satisfied their necessary needs, the poor could live in abundance from their surplus. It is also argued that the advantage self-fulfillment has over love is that the peace of our conscience cannot be sacrificed for the well-being of others. But since the peace of conscience belongs self-fulfillment, it would be very wrong to eliminate this element from self-fulfillment. This case study shows that reason is not always able to give a definite answer about what must be done and what must be omitted. Since the specifics of obligatory action are also subject to reasoning, they must be traced back to their source and grounded in virtue as the habit of goodness. Only someone who has acquired virtue through habit will also be certain of it in their actions. This habituation of virtue is asceticism.

#### Asceticism

The cultivation of good habits are only a means to achieve the fulfillment of duty as such. By making a habit of doing something good, it becomes part of the person's character, allowing the good to arise as a habit of its own accord. The purpose of asceticism is to make morality a habit so that the right decision can be made even without reflection, just like any other mechanical habit. To attain this habit, the individual must first know themselves, identify their weaknesses and inclinations, so that they know where they are lacking. From this, the individual must secondly eliminate and negate bad impulses and inclinations through practice in order to prepare themselves for dutiful action. Thirdly, from the preparatory rejection of sensuality arises the knowledge of the good and its expression to others.

##### Self-Knowledge

Even the ancients elevated self-knowledge to a primary rule of life in the maxim *γνῶθι σαυτόν zu* - "know thyself." Just as careful observation of one's own body largely serves to diagnose it, so is self-knowledge, to an even greater degree, a diagnostics for the spirit. To determine what desires I must guard against most vigilantly, I must closely examine the ones are most strongly ingrained in my nature. For since, in the realm of duty, a drive is not an end in itself but is only a means to an end, I must refuse to allow that specific drive, the very one bestowed upon me by nature, to assert itself for its own sake. Through our deeds, the judgments others pass upon us, and comparing our actions to theirs, our self-knowledge is objectively enriched, whereas Rosenkranz rightly designates the former mode of self-knowledge as *subjective*. Thus, through these two modes of self-examination, vigilance and reflection, a clearer picture of our character will emerge. We uncover the methods and maxims by which we are accustomed to acting in order to discover what we are like. Rosenkranz terms this "unconditional self-knowledge." This comprehensive self-knowledge constitutes the surest path to self-improvement, for the first step toward betterment, as the proverb tells us, is to recognize one's own faults.

##### Practice of the Good

However, we must subsequently act in accordance with this insight, specifically by suppressing the very drives we are accustomed to making the principles of our conduct. In general, we must impose upon ourselves certain deprivations in order to habituate ourselves not to elevate our drives to the status of ends, but to master them as means to our ends. The Stoics expressed this renunciation of sensuality in the formula *ἀνέχου καὶ ἀπέχου* (sustain and abstain), and in the Indian religions, just as in the Christian Middle Ages, this practical asceticism was carried to its utmost extreme. One renounced every pleasure and every sense of self in order to give expression, within oneself, solely to the Divine, the Eternal, and the Moral. By completely withdrawing from all earthly affairs, the bonzes, monks, and hermits acquired the reputation of sanctity, transforming themselves into dwelling places for the spirit. From this standpoint, reason and sensuality stand in an inverse relationship, although the extreme practicesof ascetics, such as spending one’s entire life atop a pillar, sleeping while standing, and so forth, may dull reason no less than they dull the senses.

##### Knowledge of the Good

In truth, however, the negativity of asceticism inevitably turns positive. For whoever becomes a master of their sensuality attracts the admiration of the world, and whoever lives in constant penance will find the commandment of duty ever clearer and more distinct before their soul. Even theoretically, they will have gain this knowledge for themselves in a purer form and so will have attained true self-knowledge, the unconditional Idea of ​​the Good, that now constitutes their innermost being. This is the standpoint that Schleiermacher adopts in his "Monologues," and on which he claims to have attained the highest morality, directly articulating moral duties without being hindered by the collisions of casuistry. To share this knowledge with others is the highest gift that a person can offer. Those who interact in this way form a society of morally striving individuals, fulfilling the Stoic demand for a paraenetic ethics by morally instructing and strengthening one another through sharing their moral experiences and discoveries. The fruit of this association is that everyone finally recognizes the moral law so completely as the inner essence of themselves that they relate to it as to the eternal personality of the spirit, and perpetally finds in it the unwavering basis for their own dutiful deeds. The Idea of ​​the Good is now so intertwined with the personality that it becomes the drive to do good in general, i.e. conscience.

### Conscience

Conscience is duty that has become a virtue, skill, and habit, where inclination and custom are no longer excluded from the Idea of ​​the Good, and so can be expressed as the satisfaction of the drive to do good. In conscience, morality has reached its highest stage of development. The infinite subjectivity of conscience completely derives the objective content of the moral purpose from within itself as its own good conscience. However, since the infinite subjectivity of conscience is groundless, it has the freedom to decide against the good, asserting their own good to be greater than the good itself, which is the true origin of evil. Finally, the irrationality of evil destroys itself, taking its opposition to the good with it, and the natural drives become willing instruments of the primal drive to do good, which is the standpoint of ethicality.

#### Good Conscience

Because conscience appears as the drive to be conscientious, it also possesses the quantitative nature of virtue. The mean of this drive is conscientiousness, whereas the extreme ends of insufficient conscience either appears as scrupulousness or unscrupulousness. The mean of conscience must, however, lead to dutiful action, while the extremes either act contrary to duty out of recklessness or fail to act out of embarrassment. Good conscience creates the entire content of duty from within itself, resolving the division between different duties. Conscience does not reflect like the casuist, nor does it remain in contemplation like the ascetic, but in the midst of its actions, the unconditional independence and certainty of its content shines through.

Conscience knows itself to be the master over every duty-based content and accepts the law because it knows that its content cannot bind it. Jacobi took this standpoint of Socratic insight, elevated above the law, to its highest point when he said that he would pluck ears of grain on the Sabbath, even if only because he was hungry. Jacobi calls this most sacred certainty that man possesses within himself the right of pardon for all crimes against the letter of pure law, the true right of majesty of man and the seal of his divine nature. Christianity is quite right to call conscience, as the inner court of reason, the voice of God in man.

However, the unconditionality conscience turns into its opposite. For on the one hand, the voice of reason presupposes an unconditional objectivity, and on the other hand, conscience has to draw all its content from its own subjectivity, so the two sides come into conflict. Since the subject's content consists solely of drives and inclinations, good conscience fears that this content does not correspond to the voice of reason, and so it becomes a wavering conscience. But because this uncertainty is the very opposite of conscience, it must move to the unwaveringness of action, thereby exposing itself to the danger of doing something wrong. Finally, by acting with the awareness of the possibility of error, it cannot escape its own self-division, i.e. the conflict that transforms its unconditional self-certainty to self-doubt, leading conscience to the uncertainty that it is good or evil. Conscience questions whether its arbitrariness places its own good above the universal principle of the good. But since the decision based on one's own good is precisely the principle of conscience, this formal subjectivity of conscience is always on the verge of being evil.

#### Evil

Since the fulfillment of moral freedom requires the unconditional power of self-determination, which it the power to actualize any possibility it chooses, the origin of evil begins here. For freedom to become self-certainty, it must have explored all possibilities, even if only, as Scripture praises Christ, through inner temptation. Therefore, evil does not have an independent starting point from the good, as the Zoroastrians and Manicheans claim, but is rooted in the self-determination of good, whose inverted aspects are evil itself. These aspects are: firstly, the unconditional self-determination of subjective reason, and secondly, the inherent objectivity of universal reason. If we separate the objective content of reason from the subject's self-determination, the former becomes the natural drive and the latter becomes formal arbitrariness. For the agreement of both sides to arise as the victory of the good against evil, their struggle and conflict must precede it. The stages of this development are as follows:

##### Origin of Evil

Before the will makes a decision from within itself, contrary to the objective content of reason, it lives in the immediacy of the natural drive. For virtue, the natural drives are good, but for duty, the natural drives are evil. But the natural drives are neither good nor evil by themselves, so the human being originally lives in a state of innocence that is unconsciously in simple accordance with nature, which must be abandoned precisely because its being is completely dependent on nature, just as the literal etymology of the word paradise is a walled enclosure. The only state that is appropriate to human beings is self-conscious reason, so the standpoint of pure naturalness must be abandoned. Because the self-determination of reason is first and foremost good for human beings, the natural determination of the drives is evil for them. There is always the possibility of being evil if the natural drive as such is seized with formal arbitrariness. In biblical terms, this means that human beings, although innocent by nature, are nevertheless born with original sin.

Because man is supposed to be a realized instance of the self-determination of reason, he goes beyond the naturalness of being determined by instincts, thus seizing arbitrarily this or that instinct instead of being guided by the constraints of nature. In this way, he leaves the state of innocence, where the instinct is given, and follows his arbitrary impulses, which derive their content from his particular subjectivity. The external aspect of the natural drive in itself is internalized and becomes formal self-determination. Thus, not only does the natural drive become evil because it is no longer pure natural, but arbitrariness becomes evil as well because it has been severed from the reality of nature. This is the true meaning of the Fall. It is a giant leap for mankind, as Schiller says, because man has progressed from a natural being determined by nature to a spiritual being that determines itself. But since the content of what man is destined for is dependent on his will, he now perceives the incompatibility between what he wants to will and his current state. He recognizes good and evil, and has indeed attained the likeness of God in doing so, but also the awareness of his own sinfulness.

With the knowledge of good and evil, however, the possibility of return is also established. Man is evil both in himself and through his reflection when he willingly opposes the primal drive to do good. But man is neither intrinsically nor reflectively evil. On the contrary, it is because man reflects on the difference between good and evil that the very act of reflection is the beginning of justification. Whoever recognizes that they have done wrong is already on the path to improvement, and the repentant conscience is the first step towards redemption. The same elements of self-determination and natural drive in evil are also in the good. However, self-determination and the natural drive are in such agreement in the good that reason appears as the identity of both, in that reason is naturally driven to determines itself as the being of being. Conversely, in evil, formal self-determination is estranged from reality and the natural drive is servile to nature, bringing both into conflict with reason. However, the return of the repentant conscience from this conflict creates a new conflict.

##### Justification of Evil

The return to objective, intrinsic reason is from a moral standpoint is also a return to the intrinsic nature of the natural drive. This means that every action, even if it is good on the one hand, is also an evil action on the other — good and evil are inextricably linked. Because every action, no matter how good, also satisfies a drive or an interest, the action is also contrary to duty. If duty commands one to serve one's friends, then the inclination and interest that I feel in doing so appears precisely as a selfish act. I act while fulfilling a duty with the awareness that I am not virtuous, but evil. In short, I act with a guilty conscience. Conversely, since the concept of an evil action includes the requirement that it be done with a guilty conscience, the Jesuits sought to deduce the non-evil nature of the action from the absence of a guilty conscience in their morality, i.e. an action can only be considered evil if it was undertaken with full awareness of its unlawfulness.

Since the selfish interest that appears even in a dutiful action is only apparent to the actor, who alone knows their own intention, the action appears as one that seems good to others, but is undertaken with a guilty conscience for the actor. In other words, the moral action carried out is hypocrisy, the appearance of good for others while the actor themselves has lost the sanctity of conscience. This hypocrisy is not merely an accidental form of evil, but the necessary result of the contradictions of the moral standpoint, since the actor always carries the bad conscience of their own self-interest with them.

Since the others for whom the action is intended have had the same experience, what is presented to them as a good is also for them a conscious evil. Every action is evil both for themselves and others. The two sides that were distributed between two persons in hypocrisy are now united. Everyone now acts with a bad conscience to the extent that they are also deceiving themselves with the appearance of good. This is probabilism, one of the cornerstones of Jesuit morality, as Pascal calls it. Various reasons can be given for any action, such as selfish, benevolence, and the like. The point of probabilism is to derive every action, for the actor and for others, from a reason that has a probability of being good. The Jesuits were very flexible in this regard and considered the slightest probable reason, e.g. the reputation of a Jesuit writer, sufficient to appease conscience. The first teacher of probabilism, however, was the serpent in Paradise, which cited the fruit of knowledge and godliness as the reason for human disobedience — a reason that even God acknowledged to be valid when He says: “Behold, Adam was born, as one of us, knowing good and evil.” Even the less probable reason sufficed for the Jesuits' acquittal, and thus the new mode of salvation, which Pascal alludes to, has taken a wider turn. Of the evil act, nothing but its good side remains and has been elevated to the whole.

##### Dissolution of Evil

With this, the reversal of evil into good is initiated. For conscience, which alone decides from this standpoint, can find any reason to make an action good. This reversal, however, is at the same time the highest point of evil, and is the subjectivity that dissolves all content.

The second principle of the Jesuits, cited by Pascal, presents itself here first: the direction of the intention with which they complete their redemption. Of the many aspects that appears in an action, one aspect is singled out as the aspect that makes the action good. One has good intent because their action proves, if one only focuses on this good aspect and pushes the others into the background. Thus, every bad action can be justified: the struggle with the duty to uphold one's honor, abandoning one's duty to protect another, etc. The concern for our lives is the positive aspect, especially when one has a wife and children to feed at home. Such positive content is considered an ultimate end to which the means are subordinate. For the sake of a good end, even a bad means become good if it is connected with the direction of the intention.

Secondly, however, one can ask why so much of a fuss is made over finding the right justification for an action. Even Fichte could not overcome this trend by saying: “Do what your conviction tells you is your duty.” The next consequence of this is that if my conviction is false, then I have made a mistake, not a wrong. But we have already quoted Aristotle that a false understanding does not excuse the action, but is the reason why the action is wrong. To act from conviction, there is now the added awareness of the inescapable necessity of falsehood. According to Kantian philosophy, if the implicit essence of things and thus also of the good is unknowable, then we act from conviction with the awareness that our conviction could mean nothing. By contrast, Fichte demands the knowledge of the objective end purpose in order to escape the purely subjective standpoint of conviction.

In this way, evil culminates in the subjectivity that knows itself to be the highest, which acts even though it knows that the principle of its action is false, is the standpoint of irony as it appears in Fichte's philosophy. While the irony of Socrates degraded the person, it was Schlegel, in his Lucinde, who placed the ironic self above all content, unable to realize the good, yet acts in order to know itself in the foaming waves of its deeds, and is this constantly changing Proteus, who in all action enjoys only themselves. Convinced of the futility of their actions, the self then comes to the conclusion that it can only assure itself of its goodness in the sacred silence of genuine passivity and inaction, just as one does in politics by remaining idle and leaving the future of history to the world spirit. A finer form of this inaction is the beautiful soul, which Goethe so excellently describes in his Wilhelm Meister. It is the self-satisfaction with one's moral worth that everyone, as in Schleiermacher's monologues, expresses against themselves and others, seeing in this assurance of their conscientiousness and good intentions the fulfillment of the moral end itself. That the completeness of the good in the beautiful soul is not lost, even though the ego devotes itself to a single drive, is the standpoint of ethicality, the true overcoming and reversal of the evil into goodness.

#### Ethicality

The reconciliation of legality with the blossoming of moral life is truly achieved by ethicality. While the beautiful soul still considers self-determination impure, ethicality accepts the objective power of the ethical relationships it has with others without relinquishing its own subjectivity. It is now irrelevant that morality and ethicality mean the same thing. We take ethicality to be morality filled with objective content, reflection that is intrinsic to substance. In ethicality, the Idea of ​​the Good, as the highest pinnacle of subjective freedom, has been united with the objective freedom of right. So in ethicality, we will now have to distinguish its three aspects of objective, subjective, and absolute freedom. The first aspect is ethical relationship, the second aspect is ethical self-consciousness, and the third aspect is custom.

##### Ethical Relationships

To the extent that man, through the satisfaction of his drives, knows his freedom to exist in and for itself, good is now identical to right. The freedom in which I know myself to be free is not an external one, as in the case of property, but the real connection I have with other persons, i.e. my partnership with them. The system of drives appears as a circle of ethical powers, and the duty that binds the individual appears as something universal that transcends the individual's personality, which is at the same time the highest right and existence of the individual's freedom. This brings us to the standpoint of the Greek epigram in which man is addressed thus: “What you desire, you turn into a god.” It is, however, the desire for an ethic in which right and duty are inseparable. This moralization consists in the fact that the actor is no longer the absolute master over the content of freedom, but is a member of a whole whose freedom is their own. When we describe these relationships, we can also call them duties, like the system of duties described in morality. While the latter are general human duties, those to be described now are the specific duties of family members, parents and children, teachers and students, leader and follower, etc. Schleiermacher rightly calls these relationships (of marriage, country, art, church, and science) goods since only they, as objective goals of the spirit, are truly good. But since they are also subjective drives, the pathos of individuals, we must consider them from this perspective as well.

##### Ethical Self-Consciousness

Ethical self-consciousness is the true ethical conscience, possessing the form of certainty that is identical with its objective content, whereas previously we described a conscience that was ignorant of evil. Following ethical duties in no way infringes upon our freedom, for they are already inherent to our way of life. What is curtailed is the impulsive gratification of natural drives, as well as the external reflection that assigns duties to a person for arbitrary reasons. Nor can ethical self-consciousness be purely designated a virtue, for even in the latter, the appropriate measure of the natural drive is determined subjectively. The perfectly free disposition that derives the content of the good and right from the concrete circumstances at hand is *righteousness*, the virtue of the person who does good in their actual relationships with others, and is a living example of this virtue.

##### Custom

The harmony between the subjective and the objective aspects of ethicality is *custom*, where good and right actions have become habitual in a society. The particular actions of all its members appear as the total activity of the national spirit, which expresses itself vividly within them and through their own freedom. To educate a human being ethically, as an ancient philosopher rightly observed, one must make them a citizen of a good commonwealth. The individual then acts merely as the representative of the general will. In times of moral corruption, during the dissolution of political life and of the emergence of a new spirit, the existing order is no longer good and the custom has vanished. In such times, those of Socrates and the Roman Emperors, or indeed our own, the *moral* standpoint necessarily takes the place of the *ethical* one. Man must seek the good within his own insight, for it has retreated from the existing social conditions. Hence the criticism Aristotle leveled against Socrates: that he abolished custom and natural inclination (*tò páthos kaì tò êthos*). Yet in such times, one can no longer simply follow the prevailing inclinations, habits, or customs. With the rupture in reality, the good takes refuge in the realm of conscience. However, because this conscience contains within itself no objective criterion for its decision, we witness the appearance of partisan strife characterized by the most monstrous bitterness, a bitterness whose very essence lies in the fact that the concepts of right, duty, and the good are mixed together, with opposing factions interpreting them in mutually contradictory ways, while each accuses the other of dishonesty. 

However, this historical development cannot be anticipated here. At this moment, we are purely concerned with the good as it immediately presents itself to the individual in the form of custom. However, the legal person has become the moral subject, and this unity of the moral subject with the legal person is precisely the ethical human being. This ethical person, while remaining an individual, constitutes the highest manifestation and consummation of individuality, is the family, the third and highest standpoint of private law.

## Family

### Marriage

#### Solemnization of Marriage

As regards the conditions of marriage, the original condition is the autonomous decision of two legal persons to enter into this relationship. However, since it is a matter of contingency which specific persons are brought together by love, it is the arbitrary nature of their mutual agreement that constitutes the solemnization of the marriage. Marriage thus originates as a contract, which may indeed be concluded in writing in the form of a formal marriage vow. Since, however, the free choice of the lovers constituted the other essential element, the persons independent of one another cannot be so closely related as to already constitute a natural unity of persons. For the unity in question is intended to be not a natural one, but a unity mediated through freedom itself. Since marriage, according to this view, is a ethical relationship, one that is merely preceded by a contract, the marriage vow cannot compel the marriage itself. Instead, should one party withdraw, it can at most entail financial compensation, just as stipulations aimed at preventing financial loss in the event of a potential dissolution of the marriage may indeed be established within it.

However, if the marriage is not yet regarded as concluded solely by virtue of the marriage vows, then secondly, wherein does its actual conclusion consist? Even sexual union as such does not yet constitute the consummation of the marriage, however one might choose to label such a relationship as "natural". In truth, however, the merely natural union of the sexes is termed *concubinage*. Kant effectively reduces marriage to this very level when he defines it (in *The Metaphysical Elements of Justice* § 24) as a "union of two persons of different sexes for the lifelong mutual possession of their sexual attributes." The best proof that the laws themselves do not regard even consummated sexual intercourse as a fully valid conclusion of marriage lies in the fact that even a pregnant bride cannot sue for the actual solemnization of the marriage based merely on a promise to marry, but instead acquires only the rights of a divorced wife should the groom refuse to honor his vow. However, if the marriage is to take place, then as a symbol of the moral unity of the persons contained therein, a formal conclusion of the union before the two assembled families, representing the pre-existing ethical relationships from which a third relationship is now to emerge, is required. The marriage is entered into the records of a family council, not into those of the municipality or the church, and it is this act alone that renders it fully valid and legally recognized. Marriage is a civil institution, this form alone is obligatory, whereas the religious solemnization by a member of the clergy is left entirely to the discretion of the individuals involved. Although marriage possesses a religious aspect, in its essence it remains a purely civil institution.

The fact that marriage is not a mere contract is demonstrated by its ethical purpose. Property has been termed a *real* right, and the contract a *personal* right, although both, albeit in different ways, partake in both characteristics. Marriage, like all familial relationships, is intended to be a *real and personal* right. This does not mean, however, that a person may be treated like an object, a notion which appears to be implied in the aforementioned Kantian definition of marriage. Instead, whereas the object of contracts concerns only isolated performances by persons, in marriage it is, strictly speaking, an aggregate of performances. Indeed, the entire substance of a person becomes the object of another's freedom, and yet this occurs without thereby infringing upon the freedom of the former, for that person receives in return the entire substance of the second person as the object of their own freedom. From this follows the principle of monogamy and the timelessness of marriage. For if one party were to be married to multiple partners, the latter would forfeit their freedom due to the unequal assignment. Conversely, limiting marriage to a specific duration would entail an imperfect union of persons. Thus, in marriage, as the Romans defined it, established the *mutuum adiutorium* (mutual assistance) and the *consortium totius vitae* (partnership for life). While marriage is not precluded by the cessation of its natural purpose of love, it is nonetheless generally to be assumed that it is entered into *liberorum procreandorum causa* (for the sake of procreating children). The timelessness of marriage is, however, admittedly only its *purpose*, a moral imperative. Clearly if the ethical unity of spirits has vanished, it would be immoral to keep them shackled together.

#### Rights and Duties of Spouses

From the ethical purpose of marriage, the rights and duties of the spouses now also emerge. The common will of spouses constitutes a single personality directed outward toward third parties, for it is precisely within this will that the incomplete persons become a single, unified ethical personality. This in no way precludes a shared process of deliberation and decision-making, since, depending on the specific matter at hand, the preponderance of insight may lie now with one spouse more than the other. Particularly with regard to property, the distinct rights and duties of the spouses emerge most clearly in the context of its acquisition and preservation. For in this context, the shared means of subsistence, constituting the couple’s assets, has taken the place of individual ownership. Since spouses constitute a single legal person, the most ethical arrangement is a community of property. However, if in view of the possibility that this marital unity might dissolve, then pursuant to such a contract, each spouse may indeed reserve their own assets and retains the right of free disposal over them even during the marriage. In such a case, one spouse cannot act as the other's external representative in any matters pertaining to the assets that are reserved by them. In this regard, however—speaking from the standpoint of philosophy—we must here call upon positive law to grant the wife that equality of rights which she currently still lacks. Nevertheless, even where separate ownership between spouses has been expressly stipulated, the use thereof will invariably be shared. For each spouse will, after all, be required to contribute from their own assets toward the household expenses, and generally speaking, love restores the marital community of property that the marriage contract had abolished.

#### Dissolution of Marriage

The possibility of the dissolution of marriage lies in the fact that the family is a relationship of natural ethicality, yet the aspect of naturalness inherently bears within itself the capacity for change. This mutability concerns, on the one hand, the sentiment of love, which precisely because it rests upon natural sympathy, it is possible for it to fade away. On the other hand, love extends even to the entire natural existence of one of the spouses, which may be terminated by death. In the former case, the Catholic Church has adhered consistently to the consequence flowing from the concept of indissolubility, permitting no divorce. The *inconsistency* of the Protestant Church, however, constitutes a *higher* consistency. For to refuse to sever the bond when the sentiment has vanished is, in truth, no different from servitude. Even the current practice of Protestant orthodoxy, which recognizes only adultery and malicious desertion as grounds for divorce, while refusing to remarry a spouse divorced on other grounds, demonstrates the necessity of civil marriage. This necessity is demanded even more imperatively, moreover, by the insistence of the Catholic clergy upon the Catholic upbringing of children in mixed marriages. The commission of a criminal act, such as an act of physical abuse, truly demonstrates that the ethical unity of the two persons has already been dissolved. And the final ground, that there exists insurmountable aversion, is by no means the worst. Even the Catholic Church cannot entirely evade this higher logic, and it therefore permits, at the very least, separation *a mensa et thoro*. But in doing so, it commits a further inconsistency: failing to legally dissolve a marriage that has already been dissolved *de facto*, and thereby prohibiting either party from remarrying during the lifetime of the other, a policy that has nothing but immorality, adultery, and concubinage in its wake.

However, if death dissolves the marriage, the question arises as to where the shared assets should go. We shall answer this question by assuming, for the moment, a childless marriage, without also formulating the answer in such a way that it would become inapplicable were children present. If community of property between spouses exists, then answer is simple: the survivor receives one half as their own property, because during the lifetime of the deceased, they already held it as their joint property. The question becomes more difficult only when a separation of property existed between the spouses, and thus the transfer of ownership to the survivor must be explained by recourse to the concept of inheritance. However, the rationality of the right of inheritance is a highly contested matter, so this institution therefore stands in urgent need of justification. To this end, one proceeds from the concept of the acquisition of property, as if the right of inheritance was only a mode of acquiring property that we had simply overlooked among those we enumerated earlier. Roman jurists even term the right of inheritance a *universal* mode of acquisition, in as much as one acquires through inheritance not only a single item of property, but an entire bundle of rights. Yet the right of inheritance can never constitute an acquisition of property, neither an original nor a derivative one, and therefore has no place whatsoever within private law. For where are the agreement and the transferential will that are requisite for a derivative mode of acquisition? The deceased cannot effect a transfer *mortis causa*, for they are literally no longer a person, and as long as they *are* a free person, they have no desire to effect such a transfer. Thus, regarding inheritance, the only remaining option would be to posit an *original* mode of acquisition, as if the relatives typically gathered around the deceased were to acquire the now ownerless estate through *occupation*, a principle which the laws would then have established as the rule in all cases. The flaw in such explanations, however, lies in treating the family assets as the *individual* property belonging to a head of the family. Instead, they constitute the *common* property of the family members, assets which, upon the death of one of the co-owners, become subject to actual partition among them. In other words, to substantiate the rationality of the law of inheritance, we must invariably fall back upon the rationality of the *community of property*, even in instances where positive law, or the arbitrary will of the spouses, has formally excluded it.

### Parentage

In as much as the unity of persons, which exists in itself and appears within marriage as the ethical disposition of spouses, is realized in their child as the objective existence of another personality, it follows that that the moral will of the child is intended to be nothing other than the manifestation of the unified will of the parents. This constitutes the concept of parental authority over children, an authority which we must now examine in terms of its origin, in terms of its existing rights and duties, and in terms of its dissolution.

#### Origin of Parentage

Whereas in marriage, naturally distinct individuals constitute the immediate precondition of the parental relationship, the result of this relationship is the fusion of the differences of spouses within the personality of the child, and in whom these differences cease to possess a separate existence. Therefore, parental authority first comes into being through procreation.

However, even if the child represents the enduring neutrality of the parents' opposing natures, they nevertheless take after one of the two natures. In its spiritual aspect, since every child inherits as much of the father's characteristics as of the mother's, every human being is a unity of the two sexes, transforming the physical traits, temperament, and character of both parents into a new personality. Hence, within the relationship of parental authority, even at the very moment of its inception, the element of arbitrariness that played role in the contract of marriage is now excluded. The unity of the persons involved is no longer arbitrary, but is brought forth through a necessary activity of nature that has itself been given an ethical significance. The actualization of the parents' spiritual unity, which had originally existed only as a possibility, the child initially possesses its freedom only within the freedom of their parents. Having been designated by nature itself as the actual representative of their will, the child constitutes the object of their activity. 

But just as marriage can confine itself to the purely spiritual aspect, within the *consortium totius vitæ* (partnership of the whole of life), so too can parentage arise without any natural unity of blood (without procreation), through free will and purely spiritual love. For in any ethical relationship, the spiritual aspect is to be regarded as the primary element. This is the nature of adoption. In such cases, naturally, the separate property of the adopted child are merged into the common property of the family.

While in adoption, the exclusively spiritual aspect may very well compensate for the absence of a natural connection between parents and children, conversely, the natural fact of procreation by no means automatically entails the ethical relationship. Through illegitimate procreation, the natural father or mother acquires no rights of parental authority over the child whatsoever, for they do not have any spiritual connection person with them. The spiritual consecration of the relationship between parent and child is established solely through marriage. However, since the mother originally exists in a natural unity of person with the child through the very act of nature itself, illegitimate procreation can still bring about a moral unity of persons between the mother and child. But it is immoral to attempt to create a highly ambiguous relationship between father and child through paternity suits and demands for maintenance payments and upbringing costs. Napoleonic legislation put an end to those judicial proceedings, which invariably gave rise to scandal, through the principle: *La recherche de la paternité est interdite* (The search for paternity is prohibited). But this does not preclude the reservation of the free will of the father and mother to add the spiritual dimension to the natural one, such that the father combines natural procreation with adoption through the legitimation of illegitimate children, whether indirectly through subsequent marriage to the mother (*per subsequens matrimonium*) or through a formal proceeding before a council of both families. The *levis notæ macula* (stigma of slight disgrace) attached to illegitimate children is a relic of the crude mentality of the Middle Ages, an era that fancied itself truly religious precisely when it treated the natural aspect, the very element requiring integration, with nothing but contempt.

#### Rights and Duties of Parents and Children

If parentage has arisen in one of the three ways indicated, the second question that arises is: what constitutes the content of this ethical relationship during its existence? To this end, we must first elucidate the nature and status of the parties involved in this relationship, then specify the influence that this difference exerts upon family property, and finally, we must examine their reciprocal conduct that maintains the relationship itself.

##### Parents and Children

Within the family in general, the natural individual initially appears as one-sided, deficient, and imperfect, a being that is, in the context of the ethical relationship and through mutual assistance, supposed to mature into a complete ethical person. This is also the case in the relationship between parents and children. However, their natural difference must evolve into a spiritual one, and ultimately, their one-sided positions must be balanced out precisely by learning from each other.

In the relationship of parent and child, one party is the active, generating element, and the other is the passive, generated element. From this natural distinction, both parties combine these two determinations only from different perspectives, in as much as the parents are themselves children of their own parents, and the children will become parents by having their own children. From this arises a progression that unfolds within the infinite succession of generations that mutually generate and presuppose one another. The family thereby represents the unity of the human race as a species. The unequal position of each individual link in this chain, in their relation to one another, constitutes the natural source of their differing rights and duties. The children, as the potential existence of free personality, grows in strength precisely as the strength of the parents begins to fade.

The spiritual difference between parent and child lies in the fact that, precisely because of the helplessness of children who have yet to mature into free personalities, parents exercise authority over their children in order to bring their free will to fruition. For children are, both naturally and spiritually, dependents (*alieni juris*) and not masters of themselves (*sui juris*). The development of spirit consists precisely in the task of acquiring freedom, something that is neither immediate nor natural. To this end, precisely because of their helplessness, children require the support of the parents' already developed strength of will. To the extent that parents assist in the maturation of their children, knowing full well that with this maturation the requirement of their support begins to wane, lies the selflessness of parental action, whereas the children, in their act of receiving support, manifest a certain egoism, grow in strength due to the sacrifices their parents' have made for them. Nevertheless, parents act in this selfless manner because they seek to bring their own unity of will to objective existence within the child, to perpetuate their spirit through their children. For this reason, parents love their children as their own rejuvenated selves, upon whom they wish to bestow the benefit of all their life's experiences.

Although the relationship between parents and children is, on the one hand, of a higher nature than the relationship between spouses, this relationship is also, on the other hand, of a lower nature than the marital bond, into which both parties enter with perfectly equal rights. A third relationship arises out of the two one-sided relationships of the marital bond and parentage such that the highest form of family relationship is thereby attained. In this bond, the parent and child exchange their natural and spiritual attributes with one another, such that each party ultimately arrives at the very point from which the other originally set out. The more strength that children acquire through the self-sacrificing care of their parents, the more that the strength of the parents begins to wane. In their old age, they become just as helpless and dependent as their children were in the beginning, and the moral duty inherent in the family bond, one that transcends natural necessity through a shared unity of action, is now passed on to the children. The child now cares for and protect their parents, thereby giving back to them in their old age precisely what they themselves received from them in their youth.

##### Family Property

If the parental will finds objective expression in the person of the child itself, it finds an even greater expression in the child's property. Just as the property of spouses is held in common, this must also be the case with the property of the children, as long as they have not yet attained consciousness of their own personality, and with it, the legal capacity to dispose of property. Admittedly, gifts can be made to the children by strangers or relatives on the express condition that the parents have no power of disposal over them. These are stipulations that come into full effect only after the family has dissolved, for it is only then that the children receive, as a prior allotment, the assets reserved to them, and having been released from parentage, are thereby free to manage and dispose of them independently. However, so long as the child is still a dependent, the family retains the right of usufruct and the right of administration.

However, precisely because the purpose of the family is to raise children into free persons, this unity of property must necessarily dissolve as the children become increasingly independent. Just as a husband may bestow a morning gift upon his wife, so may a father provide his daughter with a dowry, or give his son the means to establish himself in a profession and to embark upon the endeavor of personal independence. By virtue of the child subsequently acquiring assets through their own independent efforts, they become a legal person independent of their parents, and is thereby an owner of property in their own right. 

##### Childhood Education

The means by which children become autonomous, explicitly articulated as a duty of the parents, is the education of children. At the same time, this is one of most significant of rights that follows from parentage. Since the task of education is to elevate children from a potential personality to an actual personality, the body itself must first be nurtured to this end, secondly, the natural drives must be disciplined, and thirdly, the individual must be elevated to freedom through the actual cultivation of the spirit itself. Physical, moral and intellectual education are thus the three necessary components of every true theory of childhood education.

###### Physical Education (Nurture)

The mother's primary task following the birth of a child is, as Aristotle states, its *nourishment* (θρέψαι), whereas its *formation* (παιδεῦσαι), its physical upbringing, is the primary task of the father. The nurturing the child by the mother constitutes the prerequisite for all subsequent physical health, just as physical health, in turn, serves as the prerequisite for all sound intellectual development: *mens sana in corpore sano* (a sound mind in a sound body).

As a further aspect of physical education, Aristotle places particular emphasis on *movement*. Indeed, the question arises as to whether Plato’s counsel to let children bounce and dance in their arms might not also possess a philosophical dimension. Nourishment, such as the intake of milk, amounts only to an augmentation of *substance* or bodily fluids. But true health also entails *digestion* and *assimilation*, that is, physical education itself possesses an aspect wherein the natural or material element must be *negated* in order to be properly processed. That this process is best facilitated through physical movement becomes self-evident in light of natural philosophy, which defines movement as the negation (at least in a spatial sense) of the material body. After all, nothing aids digestion more effectively than a brisk walk after a meal. Movement as such, however, is not the only means of exercising and strengthening the body. Through the art of gymnastics (the *gymnastikē* of the ancients), the body also becomes agile and dexterous, enabling the individual, in all circumstances, to recognize their mastery over nature and to conduct themselves as a free person in relation to it. Swimming, jumping, running, wrestling, fencing, and all other sports fall under this category. When Rousseau protests against the swaddling of infants and the binding of their hands and feet, he is entirely justified in his objection to this restriction of movement. But if one were to adopt a diametrically opposite approach, leaving the baby entirely unswaddled, surely a happy medium ought to be struck. For precisely at that stage when the baby is not yet capable of autonomous movement, this primary source of physical instruction must not be withdrawn too abruptly.

Just as the negative aspect of physical education comes to the fore in movement, so too does the spiritual aspect. Even the acquisition of physical skills through movement, which is the primary task of gymnastics, also has a spiritual dimension. With the agility of the body, the agility of the spirit develops as well. If the purpose of education is to raise children to become free spirits, then spiritual activities must be stimulated early on and during the very course of their physical education. Physical movements must serve as means to gradually prepare children for the spiritual pursuits that are destined to constitute the substance of their lives once they have reached adulthood, i.e. play. The child freely expresses themselves in play, imitating the activities of adults as they unfold upon the stage of civic life. For the child, imagination compensates for whatever is lacking in reality. In play, children envision themselves as grown-ups. But education is not complete with play alone. An education that confines itself to play, one that seeks to impart everything to children solely in a lighthearted, comical manner, does not achieve the essential negative aspect inherent in education: the necessity of freeing children from the naturalness and immediacy of their desires.

###### Moral Education (Discipline)

The child originates in a state of selfishness, seeking to satisfy their natural drives at the expense of everyone else, and by whatever means necessary. This selfishness has its positive aspect, for in the realm of morality, our drives are purified rather than suppressed. In physical education, this selfishness is taken into account, and the individual’s physical self is developed. But in equal measure, the individual must also acknowledge that there are other selves, and that they must sacrifice their own particular, selfish drives to the extent that they conflict with reason. However, because the child is unconscious of their own reason, it initially appears to them as something external until they have become conscious of it. The pleasure to which they surrender themselves must be met by a corresponding displeasure. The enjoyment they have indulged in that is followed by pain serves as a sign that such pleasure should not to have been indulged because it is inhibited and curbed by this very pain. This constitutes the concept of *chastisement*. Here, punishment does not carry the significance of retributive justice (as in criminal law), but of moral improvement and deterrence, though justice is a equally necessary element here as is in the former context. The archetypal model of chastisement, as envisioned by Rousseau, can be termed *objective chastisement*, whenever pain follows the child’s selfish act as a matter of natural necessity, e.g. a child upsets their stomach and makes themselves sick because they have eaten too much.

In his *Emile*, Rousseau quite consistently insists that chastisement can never appear to children as an arbitrary act on the part of their parents, for children would all too easily deduce from this the principle that arbitrariness and injustice are the forces opposing their own actions, and that their own arbitrariness is just as legitimate as that of their parents' discipline. One logical consequence of this approach is the complete abandonment of corporal punishment. Carrying out this punishment, such as in the form of a blow or a slap, is physically harmful, damaging their delicate muscles, bones, and brain. Since it is the necessity of reason, rather than arbitrariness, that must be pitted against the child's capricious impulses, it follows that even in instances where nature does not punish immediately, the parents' disciplinary action must be presented as an *indirect* extension of nature's own immediate punishment, so that the parents' *unmediated* exercise of authority remains concealed. In other words, Rousseau calls for a constant conspiracy involving parents, neighbors, and others. Whenever a child does something wrong, they must be exposed to its negative consequences until they realize that they would have been better off if had they heeded their parents' advice.

Rousseau’s doctrine is, in principle, entirely correct. It is the inherent logic of the situation, not the arbitrary will of the parents, that must become apparent to the child. They have to recognize that they depend upon necessity, and they are bound it as to a higher power. Necessity cannot confront them as a blind force, but only as free reason, reason that is destined to become the child’s own—and as the true nature of things. That said, from the fact that it would likely prove difficult to conceal the discipline of the parents at every turn through a continuous conspiracy, it wouldn't even be desirable for the parents to do so. For once the child has recognized their parents’ counsel as sound, they begin to place their trust in them. Reason gradually becomes embodied in the will of the parents, and in that will, the child recognizes not arbitrariness, but necessity.

To the extent that rationality reveals itself to children as a will distinct from their own, one that is not yet *theirs*, they come to the realization that their own self-interest is not the highest good. They sense that they are not yet what they ought to be. They learn obedience as a result of discipline, precisely so that, through this very obedience, they will not need it anymore. Their obedience is an enlightened obedience, not a blind one. And here as well, educational theory has once again raised the question of which approach is best. Admittedly, if one chooses to base one’s upbringing on the attempt to guide children through didactic reasoning, one must accommodate oneself to the level of their intellect. One must present them with a limited perspective, one by which they are to be persuaded to act or to refrain from acting. However, an opposing and equally limited perspective readily presents itself, arguments beget counter-arguments. The child’s intellect, within its own limited scope, is remarkably acute, grasping counter-arguments with perfect accuracy, and often leaving us in astonishment. The child counters the parental argument with one of its own, and the educator finds themselves at the end of their wits, unless he chooses to resolve the matter by decree. Such a decree, however, comes too late, for the children perceive the standpoint offered to them as a "final" one as being, merely the *last* in a sequence, something they are done with and have already moved on from. Through this method of upbringing, their obstinacy, conceit, and spirit of contradiction have been awakened, and in the rejection of their own arguments, they see nothing but parental arbitrariness. 

Children easily perceive the childishness of the arguments presented to them, they know full well that they do not represent anything actually permanent or absolute. Such an approach to child education has forfeited all trust in the eyes of the children themselves, with some even come to despise such educators. With this, however, obedience naturally vanishes, and the result is the utmost confusion and disorder. Therefore, when I speak of *enlightened* obedience, I refer to one that is validated by experience, one in which children, whether through natural circumstance or deliberate design, have learned to recognize that their parents' counsel was sound. In other words, the parents' rational will must prove its worth not only with words, but also with deeds. In their parents' will, the child comes to love their own better selves, becoming one with their parents as free spirits. Taking the place of blind obedience, a joyful obedience joins itself to this enlightened form.

From this obedience, however, follows a reverence for one’s parents, striving to emulate them and become grown-up and rational as they are. One must not stoop down to the level of the child without simultaneously lifting them up to one’s own level, and these two aspects must not be separated. The act of stooping down serves as a means of gaining the foothold by which one draws them upward. If this reverence, or the very possibility of it, is lacking (for instance, if the parents are unworthy), then domestic upbringing becomes impossible. Instead of serving as a guide toward virtue, the home becomes a nursery for vice.

###### Intellectual Education (Instruction)

If upbringing is intended to foster sociality, specifically by negating selfishness, thereby preparing the child to enter the spiritual community of humanity, there finally exists a drive that requires neither transformation nor refinement to develop into spiritual freedom. Instead, in its very naturalness, it already encompasses the entire fullness of spiritual existence. This drive is the drive for knowledge, which is satisfied through instruction. The natural curiosity of children elevates itself to a noble thirst for knowledge as it directs itself (and is guided by instructors) toward every object of natural or spiritual existence. This constitutes the positive aspect of education, which despite being spiritual in nature, nevertheless remains in harmony with the nature. Through teaching and instruction, the individual is meant to internalize the entirety of the natural and spiritual world. However, notwithstanding this act of reception and passive absorption, the individual is destined to rise to the level of free, active thought. Here, the natural drive itself furnishes the individual with spiritual content.

In public education, the limitations of domestic education are manifest, limits that come into play partly because of the contingency that parents may very well be morally incapable of instructing their children, and partly due to the necessity that scientific instruction in all subjects vastly exceeds the capabilities of any one parent. Yet the boundary of the family, specifically that of parentage, is not merely established in this external manner. Instead, the parental will sets its own limits, in as much as it also, through the very process of upbringing, works to render itself superfluous. Thus, we must now consider the dissolution of parentage brought about by the completion of upbringing itself.

#### Dissolution of Parentage

The basis for the dissolution of legal parentage may lie partly in external circumstances and partly in internal contingencies. Thus, three modes by which legal parentage ceases is emancipation, death, and the attainment of majority.

Should the parents wish to dissolve the relationship through emancipation, either another family must be granted guardianship of the child, in which case the negative act of emancipation is counterbalanced by a positive act of adoption, or the child must have already attained a degree of independence sufficient to enable them to support themselves. An emancipation of this latter type may not take place prior to the child’s twentieth year, precisely because it is presumed that, by that time, the primary objectives of the child’s upbringing have already been achieved. Thus, only after the fulfillment of this ethical duty is free scope granted to individual discretion. Furthermore, emancipation does not sever the ethical bond between parents and children, rather, even as free individuals, they remain ethically bound to one another, a fact that will become evident, for instance, in the consequences pertaining to inheritance. 

Opposed to this dissolution of paternal authority, brought about by internal contingency of free will, stands the dissolution resulting from an external contingency of nature. The dissolution of parentage through death entails, once again, that each child receives their specific share of the common estate for actual use. Previously, by contrast, for as long as the unity of the family subsisted, the child possessed a share of the common estate only as an ideal portion of the bare ownership. For the right of inheritance is not a transfer, nor a form of derivative ownership, but rather, in the very act of transition, at the same time an original acquisition. This is because the heirs were, by virtue of the original unity of persons, already the original owners. As Plutarch expresses it so aptly: they were *in ownership* (ἐν κτήσει) and now enter into *use* (ἐν χρήσει), a state for which the Roman jurist Paulus employs the term *administratio*.

In considering this mode of original acquisition of property brought about by the external contingency of nature, if we conceive of internal arbitrariness (i.e. the exercise of free will) as being entirely excluded, then we arrive at the law of intestate succession for children. Here, it is not difficult to determine, in accordance with the inherent rationality of the matter, what the children ought to receive. If one of the spouses has already passed away, the children divide the entire inheritance among themselves in equal shares, in as much as no one child represents the deceased testator any more or any less than another. However, if one spouse still survives, to whom one half of the estate falls, then the children divide the remaining half between themselves in the very same manner as they would have divided the whole estate in the previous instance.

The arbitrary will of the testator, standing in complete opposition to the ethical relations of intestate succession, constitutes the law of testamentary succession (*hereditas secundum tabulas*). In a testament, the arbitrary will of one property owner appears to transfer assets to another property owner. Thus, we seem to have fallen entirely back into the sphere of legality, placing ourselves outside the ethical sphere of the family. Indeed, the testament has been subsumed into purely legal relations in such a way that it was construed as a benevolent contract, specifically as a gift, by being classified alongside the *donatio mortis causa*, the counterpart of which being the *donatio inter vivos*. However, the difficulty we previously raised in the context of marital law, regarding inheritance law in general simply as transfer of property, reappears with even greater intensity in relation to the testament. Moreover, the rational justification based on the unity of persons, which served as the foundation for intestate succession, does not appear to be applicable in the case of the testamentary heir. We refer to children as heirs by nature, whereas the Romans called them *heredes sui* or heirs of themselves. Assuming that the testament is not intended to be a mere documentary confirmation of the right of intestate succession (in which case it would be superfluous), the simple question can be posed: Is it rational for a property owner who has no natural heirs to relinquish their assets to an *extraneus* (a stranger) by means of a testament? If it is rational, then the basis for this rationality must originate from the same principle in which it was found in the case of intestate succession. In other words, just as adoption creates an artificial filiation in the absence of a natural one, so too is the testament such an adoption effective upon death: an *adoptio mortis causa*, not a *donatio mortis causa*. Therefore, the testament is the declaration that a purely spiritual circle of friends has stepped in for the absence of the natural family, and thus becomes entitled to share in all of the family's property rights. Consequently, it is the "family of the spirit" that is intended to inherit through the testament, thereby bringing to realization the ethical principle that all things are held in common among friends. The share of the inheritance designated by the testator should, in this light, be understood as a measure of the degree of friendship, the unity of persons that existed between the heir and the testator.

Finally, the ethical dissolution of parentage is the attainment of legal majority by the children, a state which, by virtue of their intellectual and physical development, renders them capable of leaving their original family to create one of their own. The very basis of parentage originates from the inequality between children and parents, an inequality necessitating that the children rely upon the superior will of the parents for their guidance, in as much as they were not yet capable of guiding themselves. Now as the parents themselves have, partly through the fulfillment of their duty of upbringing, eliminated this inequality, and Nature, for its part, allows children to mature of their own accord. Therein lies precisely the ethical dissolution of parentage as unity of the natural and the intellectual grounds for its cessation, whereas in the act of emancipation, only the intellectual ground is present, and in the event of death, only the natural ground for cessation came into play. However, the exact age at which legal majority commences is a matter of positive legal determination, one that falls outside the province of philosophy, depending instead upon geographical latitude, the physical constitution of the people in question, and other such considerations. The Romans, for instance, whose laws generally tended to favor the retention of parentage, did not recognize this threshold until the completion of the twenty-fifth year. Generally speaking, however, the Romans did not conceive of legal majority as constituting an ethical dissolution of the family unit at all, but merely as the termination of guardianship. As long as the father or grandfather was still alive, even a fully grown son, even if he was a Consul, remained subject to parentage. While the Romans thus merely oscillated between the two extreme poles of alienation and servitude, German law arrived at the ethical resolution of parentage without the familial bond thereby being severed. In Rome, by contrast, the daughter’s marriage did not even dissolve parentage unless she lived with her husband in a strict form of marriage (*in manu mariti*), in which case, rather than remaining the father’s slave, she became her husband's. Since, in this manner, the independence thus gained by individuals does not yet constitute the complete dissolution of the family, but rather the family persists even amidst its dissolution, we must now, thirdly, examine the familial relationships that pertain to the family in this state of dissolution, i.e. extended kinship.

### Kinship

With the members of the family becoming independent, the dissolution of the family is already pronounced, just as marriage itself, by virtue of the independence of the legal persons entering into it, was only the family *in the making*. Parental authority constitutes the family that has attained full actuality and enduring existence. However, since the independent persons did not begin their existence as free legal subjects, but arose from the ethical unity of the family, they represent the family *in the process of dissolution*, a process that is itself a form of family relationship. Specifically, this dissolution is, in the first instance, a process *in the making*, occurring within the actual persistence of the family bond itself, i.e. the relationship of siblings. Secondly, we encounter the family *during* its dissolution, a state in which the original unity is reduced to a mere possibility, i.e. the relationship of relatives. Finally, there is the family that has been actually *dissolved*, a state where even the bond of blood kinship has ceased to apply, yet which remains a genuine family relationship among independent persons, i.e. the relationship of neighbors.

#### Siblings

If we consider the sibling relationship in relation to the parental one, the question arises whether, upon the death of a brother or sister, the surviving siblings must share the estate with the ascending line of relatives or whether they succeed to the inheritance exclusively. Naturally, were one to measure the right of inheritance strictly by proximity of kinship, parents stand in the first degree of kinship, whereas siblings stand only in the second, so according to this, the former would take precedence over the latter. But in positive law, we observe that the siblings inherit *per capita* alongside one another, which is a more reasonable arrangement. For parents and siblings are not only related to one another, they partake in a unity of personhood and blood, wherein no distinction of degree exists. Just as the testator stands alongside their children as a single, co-equal legal person, so too is the property of a childless testator regarded as if it still constituted their common property. In this scenario, the surviving spouse would likewise receive one half of the estate. The testator, together with their parents or the grandparents and their siblings, constitutes a single collective personhood, every member of which possesses an equal, ideal share in the common spirit of the family. It is entirely in order that full siblings take precedence over half-siblings, for they have necessarily belonged to said family unit. However, when half-siblings do succeed to an inheritance, it is entirely immaterial whether the deceased’s estate was derived from the parent shared by them or from the parent not shared by them. The inheritance pertaining to the shared parent has already been settled, so the matter at hand solely concerns the deceased’s independent estate, which may just as well have acquired through their own efforts or received from a third party. Conversely, half-siblings must accept the necessity of sharing the inheritance with an ascendant whom they do not share with the deceased, for by virtue of their own blood relationship with the deceased, and the deceased’s blood relationship with his ascendant, an artificial unity of persons must be presumed to exist between that ascendant and the half-siblings, which may as well have actually existed in the manner previously described.

#### Relatives

Whereas in the sibling relationship, the unity of persons within the family bond has certainly existed as a reality, in the relationship of relatives, by contrast, the commonality of family life and of parental authority is only a *possibility*, a presupposition that chronologically precedes the individual members, and is only actual on the condition that their shared grandparents are still alive. We can conceive of this situation as a reality in cases where the parents have predeceased the grandparents, and so their children find themselves under the parentage of their shared grandparent(s). 

In as much as the relationship of relatives has its basis in the sibling relationship, representing the further development of the latter through the progression of successive generations *ad infinitum*, so too must the associated right of inheritance be traced back to the very same source. Here, the testament, or the actual, spiritual family, invariably prevails over the potential, natural family. Furthermore, in the law of intestate succession, collateral relatives always inherit *per stirpes* (by stocks) or by lineage, never *per capita* (by heads). The basis of the inheritance is no longer the equal entitlement of individual persons within the unity of the family, but their common descent from siblings. Thus, it is not individuals, but rather the individual *stems* that appear as co-equal heirs. The stem of a deceased sibling holds equal rights with that of a surviving one, just as in direct succession to a grandparent's estate, the grandchildren of a single child collectively receives only one share. For here as well, they do not inherit as individuals, but as a *stem*, a representative of the deceased that takes their place and constitutes them as a single legal person in relation to the testator. Where the right of inheritance is cut off by law due to excessively remote degrees of kinship, Trendelenburg in his *Natural Law* advances the sound idea that the inheritance should not be appropriated by the public treasury, but should devolve to the local community to be utilized for the endowment of charitable purposes.

#### Mentorship

The outermost form of family relationship, in which the natural blood relation derived from a common lineage ceases to exist, is between the mentor and apprentice. This relationship announces itself as the very boundary of an ethical relationship by the fact that its members not only enter into it by means of a contract, as is the case with marriage, but that the relationship simultaneously remains a contract throughout its entire duration. However, there are certain ethical duties attached to this contract, even if they cease to exist the moment the contractual agreement is no longer in force. Thus, this relationship constitutes a remarkable blend of an ethical relationship and a contract. Moreover, it also combines the defining characteristics of the first two family relationships, marriage and parentage, in an even more interconnected manner than the sibling relationship itself. Alongside the complete personal freedom of both individuals involved, there is simultaneously a relationship of dependency, similar to that found in parentage. However, whereas this dependency is, in the case of parentage, a condition established by nature, here it is a dependency that is voluntarily, and therefore morally, willed by the apprentice themselves. A free legal person voluntarily places themselves under the parentage of their mentor, yet they do so not in such a way that the reciprocal return is deferred until a later time, but rather in a manner where the exchange always exists simultaneously and reciprocally. The neediness of the individual, which is intended to be compensated for by these moral services—is no longer a natural need, but stems from an inequality of intellectual cultivation, whereby the mentor, by virtue of their superior education, delegates certain duties to the apprentice, while the apprentice, by undertaking these duties, is at the same time destined to share in the higher education and prosperity of the mentor. 

To the extent that the ethical relationship is preserved within the mentorship, notwithstanding the dissolution of the family, we have thereby moved beyond the family without abandoning ethicality. Instead, ethicality has assumed a higher form by having transcended its natural origins. It is free persons who are involved here, persons who do not, as in marriage, re-enter a state of ethicality by lineage, but rather, ethicality remains purely spiritual, mediated solely through the free will of the individuals involved. To put it another way, in the mentorship, free legal persons facing one another persons who not only pursue moral ends of mutual assistance in their social dealings with one another, but also, in their personal lives, pursue moral ends by mutually assisting the members of their own family. This same duality of ethicality, directed both inward and outward, also arises from the two preceding relationships, which itself belongs to the dissolving family structure. As brothers and sisters found new families, a process that is repeated among their descendants and more distant kin, the common lineage expands into a larger community that comprises numerous families. Externally, the different families initially confront one another primarily as strictly defined legal entities, and yet, because they are driven by the internal ethical imperative to preserve their own families, the same families simultaneously form an ethical bond with other families. Through the reciprocity and exchange of services, they collectively ensure the preservation of their families by satisfying their manifold needs, constituting a free association of families formed for the specific purpose of satisfying the self-interested ends of each individual family. Thus, we have effectively transitioned from private law to public law, elevating the human being out of their isolation in natural law into the social being of political science.
