## Consciousness

The I is spirit distinguished from the external world. But since the I is external to the external world and the external world is external to itself, the I and the external world are essentially related to each other. Consciousness is this essential relation of the I and the external world, whose development necessarily appears as a development of consciousness and its object. Initially, the object appears to consciousness as an immediate experience and the consciousness that is conscious of this immediate experience is sensuous consciousness.

### Sensuous Consciousness

Sensuous consciousness is essentially distinguished from awareness in that consciousness distinguishes itself from what it is aware of and relates to it as an object. The object of sensuous consciousness is still an awareness, but it is an awareness that is separate from the I as a resisting object. In sensuous consciousness, the I is conscious of an immediate object and is certain of its existence because it is conscious of it. To sensory consciousness, awareness of the object is proof enough, for at this point it has no reason to be uncertain. The object is because it is.

The object of sensuous consciousness is, at first, an immediate, individual thing. The individual thing is simply a This. However, every other thing that is not this This is also a This. This is not an individual, but is a universal. If, however, one prefers to distinguish this This from every other This by designating it as a “not This, but This,” then one no longer has it as an immediate thing, which it should be, but rather as mediated by the negation of These. The same applies to the spatiotemporal determinations by which one wants to fix the object by designating it as a Here and Now. For if every place is here and every moment is now, then it becomes clear that Here and Now are universals. Even if one wanted to alternatively determine them as “not here, but here,” and “not now, but now,” they would still be mediated by the negation of other Heres and Nows. 

The uncertainty of the object as a This causes sensuous consciousness to take the I to be the source of certainty, fixing its awareness to a specific object that it sees, hears, etc., that is the object for this I. But the I is also not, as it initially appears, an immediate, individual thing. For since the object is not for the seeing or hearing of the I, but for the I in general that sees, hears, etc., it is a simple universality of many awarenesses. Furthermore, since the I distinguishes itself from other Is by referring to itself as "not you, but I," it presents itself as mediated through the negation of many others. The I also presents itself as something mediated when it designates itself with a name or describes what it is.

Finally, when the object and the I are not considered separately, but are placed in relation to each other, then their meaning is mediated by their placement, and yet the certainty of it also disappears. For since, in the flux of things, this object is immediately no longer this, but this, no longer here, but here, no longer now, but now, it becomes clear that it is a This that presupposes many These, a Here that presupposes many Heres, and a Now that presupposes many Nows are negated and contained in the object by behind been superseded by it. The object is now a multiplicity of qualities that occupies a place containing many Heres, and whose Now (time) contains many Nows. Consciousness must summarize this multiplicity in order to grasp the object as it truly is.

The consideration of sensory consciousness in all its moments therefore leads to a form of consciousness where an I, which is no longer an immediate individual, but a simple universality of many awarenesses, perceives an object that is a simple universality of many qualities (sensory, spatial and temporal) in a way that goes well beyond mere sense certainty. The transformation of sensuous consciousness into perceiving consciousness actually occurs in a naively unconscious way every time the object that sensory consciousness refers to is identified.

### Perceptual Consciousness

The perceiving I is a simple universality that, having suspended its sensory awarenesses, possesses the various forms of sensory consciousness. Likewise, the perceived object is simple universality that possesses a multiplicity of sensory determinations which occur as suspended properties of a thing. Consciousness of the object is no longer immediate, but is the mediated connection of the object and  sensory with universal determinations a taking in its truth. In the transition to perception, the chaotic discernment inherent to sensory consciousness is eliminated because multiplicity appears in the One. If sensory consciousness can only discern by pointing, then description finds its place here as expressed perceptions. This standpoint of consciousness will therefore take precedence in empirical descriptions. Things, properties, matter, etc., as the categories of this standpoint, are thought determinations. They are also categories of mediation because this standpoint has transcended immediacy. As such, they are also subject to logical discussion. 

Just as before, the object is not produced by the I, it is only passively aware of it and the certainty of its perception seems to be derived from the object itself. However, the object of perception is a contradiction. On the one hand, it is a simple universality as a thing, but on the other hand, it is a unity of many properties that is simultaneously distinguished from the properties it possesses. At the same time, however, each property is a predicate of it, in that it is a this and also a that. In other words, every manifold of properties a thing has is simultaneously excluded from (neither-nor) and included by (both-and) the thing. In turn, the manifold of properies a thing has are contradictory in that they do not exclude each other because they all exist in the thing, but at the same time, each property negates and is exclusive from all the others. Finally, the same contradiction is shown in the relation of the properties to the thing since the properties only appear in the thing and yet are also distinct from the thing as independent entities that can appear in various other things.

The contradiction of the thing and its properties is a question a scientist can easily answer because they are used to applying categories descriptively, such as what properties something has or what it is made of. This is a question about what the categories actually are, and since it does not affect the content of physical phenomena at all, it is not specific to physical questions. The person who seeks to describe nature, even if they so much as make only one claim, will inevitably encounter difficulties and contradictions in what they describe.

If the object of perception is contradictory, then the same applies equally to the I that perceives. The perceiving I is supposed to be a simple universality that is also a unity of many perceptions. If, on the one hand, the many perceptions are described as the faculties that the I possesses, then, on the other hand, the opposing view which fixes the many perceptions and lets the I consist of them is just as justified. The contradiction in the concept of the perceiving I corresponds entirely to the contradiction in the concept of the perceived object.

It is precisely because the same contradiction of unity and multiplicity is contained in both that two options are presented to consciousness as ways out of the contradiction. In the first option, the object is a unity that is only perceived as a multiplicity by the I. In the second option, the object is a multiplicity that is only perceived as a unity by the I. Both choices are equally impossible. The first explanation is impossible because it implies that anything that has the same properties is the same thing, whereas the second is impossible because a change in one property causes other properties to change. From one explanation, one is therefore thrown back to the other, endlessly alternating from this to that.

Whether the object is taken in such a way that it is one thing in itself and multiple only for the I, or is many things in itself and one thing only for the I, it is in fact always taken in these two ways, in that its being in itself is distinguished from its being for itself. Thus, we now have a consciousness that no longer has for its object only what constituted the object of perception, but considers the object and its understanding of it. It distinguishes both at the same time, going beyond perception by taking its object twice: once as it is for the I, and the second as it is for itself. Since consciousness here relates to its understanding of the object, but at the same time does not want to remain with its understanding in that it seeks the essence behind the appearance, it is reflective consciousness. And because the state of being, as it is, is simultaneously the understanding of the object as it is for consciousness, sensuous and perceptual consciousness unite in reflection to form a higher unity.

### Reflective Consciousness

Reflective consciousness names the object in such a way that the object as it appears to consciousness is distinguished from the object as it does not appear to consciousness. To posit the object in-itself as unknowable is, however, a tautology. Despite this, consciousness has no other choice but to make this distinction because it is conscious of what is and is not for itself. This first trace of reflective consciousness characterizes the natural curiousity of the child in that they know that things are more than what they appear to be and want to know what is hidden behind the appearances.

However, by knowing appearance as appearance, the appearance is untrue, whereas the essence is true. But if essence is true in contrast to appearance, then in fact the two are not only distinct from each other, but each is the opposite of the other, meaning that essence is precisely the opposite of appearance. This standpoint of the inverted world is also justified as a transitional point. One adopts this standpoint when what one believes is seen as the opposite of the actual state of affairs, and this is made into an absolute standpoint with the pretension of being religious, when earthly life is taken to be a vale of tears and the afterlife as the diametrical reversal of this life, etc.

But if each is the opposite of the other, then neither is conceivable without the other, for each already contains the other within itself and is identical to it. Thus, consciousness has one and the same object twice, and that each differs only in appearance. If it was first known that something was an object both times, and that both times they were different from each other, then both are united here. To be conscious of one and the same object twice, that appear different each time for consciousness, is an explanation which, the more concise it is, the more it contains only tautologies. Not only nominal definitions of things, but also real explanations for them that only say one and the same thing twice. For example, that all bodies fall at the same speed is explained by the fact that they are attracted by gravity in proportion to their mass. By equating mass with weight and weight with attraction (mass = weight = attraction), the definition reduces to the tautology that bodies are attracted in proportion to their attraction. The more terms are equivalent to the definition, the less the definition is defined.

Reflective consciousness explains by presenting the object as distinct, but continually negates the difference that makes it distinct. On closer inspection, however, it becomes clear that this distinction does not affect the object itself, that the object can be explained in different ways. The duality of the object is therefore only a duality for consciousness. Because it is consciousness and not the object that provides the explanation, one takes great pleasure in the explanations they provide, as well as in the activity of explanation itself. The explanations appear impressive to others because of the rhetorical power that reflection has in commanding respect, not for the thing itself, but for the person who asserts what they believe with great conviction. But if consciousness only reflects on the duality of the object, and this duality is grounded only as the duality for consciousness, as proof that the object can be conceived in different ways, then the every object of consciousness is an appearance for consciousness. That consciousness always accompanies the object as an object of consciousness shows that consciousness is consciousness of itself, i.e. self-consciousness.

## Self-Consciousness

If the object was essential for the I as consciousness, then for the I as self-consciousness, the object is inessential, for the I alone is essential. Consciousness therefore relates to self-consciousness as sensitivity relates to embodiment. The former resists the object by bracing itself against it, whereas the latter resists by eliminating the resistance. To establish itself as the essential against the inessential object is the goal of the process by which self-consciousness becomes what it is.

### Desire

To establish itself as what it truly is, self-consciousness must relate to objectivity in such a way that, in and through this relationship, it experiences itself as essential and the object as inessential. To this end, self-consciousness relates to the object in such a way as to make it inessential, i.e. to eliminate it. Hence, self-consciousness is desire, and desire is only satisfied by the act of eliminating the object. This immediate, first form of self-consciousness is also becomes what it is in the stages of life. The child's desire to consume or destroy things is the first moment in which self-consciousness becomes conscious of its own independence.

The justification for the destruction of the object lies in the fact that is an obstacle that stands between self-consciousness and its independence. However, if the object were truly and completely destroyed, there would be nothing left for self-consciousness to be independent from. Therefore, the elimination of the object to satisfy a desire creates the very lack that causes another desire to take its place, which in turn can only be satisfied with the elimination of another object, and so on. To escape the endlessness of desire, self-consciousness must negate the object in such a way that the independence of the object is negated, but its existence is still preserved for the satisfaction of self-consciousness. This is realized when self-consciousness transforms the object and turns it into a tool. In this process of transforming objects into tools, self-consciousness develops itself. In the stages of life, the child's impulses to consume or destroy things is superceded by playing with them as toys, wherein the the child imaginatively transforms the object into whatever it wants to be. Taking toys apart belongs here as well, since it is also a kind of transformation. In the development of humanity, labor is formative because in it the transforming self-consciousness reveals itself as mastery.

This process of transformation points to its goal and its truth by showing that every object bearing the stamp of the I is subject to it. At this point, however, self-consciousness has finally become an object to itself, that it possesses objectivity. But once the subjectified object stands opposite to the objectified subject, the process of transformation reveals the truth that self-consciousness desires itself, i.e. another self-consciousness. Thus, self-consciousness seeks to satisfy its desire in the desire of another self-consciousness. But since each self-consciousness desires the desire of the other, it can enters through struggle against it. All rules of instruction can only have this as their goal: to bring self-consciousness to such a point that it instructs itself automatically and does not need to be instructed by another. The desire for recognition appears in the child's desire to give personality to their toys, empirically demonstrating that individual self-consciousness educates itself through interpersonal conflict. The point here is that the concept of self-consciousness cannot be fully grasped without other self-consciousnesses.

### Recognition

The process of recognition is a struggle of life or death. For every self-consciousness is absolute in itself and makes itself its own end to which external objectivity must yield, but at the same time, each self-consciousness appears to the other only in this form of an external thing or object. They have not yet tested their freedom against each other and therefore do not yet relate to one another, rather, each takes the other as they present themselves according to their sensory appearance. Each does not yet recognize a free self-consciousness in the other, but sees them only an object which they consider suitable to serve as a means to their ends. However, this treatment incites the anger of the other, they makes the first person feel the absolute rigidity and inflexibility of their ego by repelling the attack on their being with the full energy of their own power.

However, this relationship is not the end, but is only means to expose the inner spiritual relationship from this outer shell. For I cannot recognize myself in the other, know them as a self-consciousness identical with myself, and thus acknowledge their absolute freedom, as long as they remain a sensory object to me. Seeing the other as an object is therefore the obstacle that must be removed in order to bring about the contact of spirit, so the self-consciousness seeking recognition therefore aims at the annihilation of external being, i.e. the death of the other. Likewise, this individual cannot know themselves as free if they cling to the immediacy of their sensory existence and does not also know this externality as something to be overcome. Instead of finding their sense of self in pleasure, as is the case with desire, they risk their own life in order to attain absolute freedom through their own endangerment. Since freedom requires that everyone regards the immediacy of existence as indifferent for themselves and others, and the absolute proof of this at this stage is death, each individual seeks to take the life of the other at their own peril in order to destroy all that resists the self.

What has just been described is, according to Hobbes, a war of all against all, the state of nature of humankind which belongs not to natural law, but to psychology. Natural law has the rational nature of the human mind as its presupposition, whereas psychology posits its natural beginning in time. In psychology, we must take man as he comes from the hands of nature, and thus describe the natural history of his mind. The mythical archetype of this state is the story of Cain and Abel in the Old Testament, for what is presented there in the first human family is the state of natural man in general, which has since been repeated countless times. The struggle ends with the death of one of the combatants, but this result has not advanced the purpose of recognition. Through death, the person's sensory existence is indeed abolished, but the possibility of recognition is abolished with it. The one who is killed does not exist as recognized, but has instead become a lifeless thing, whereas the killer recognizes their error in seeking the internal essence of self-consciousness, which exists in and for itself, by abolishing its external appearance. The surviving self-consciousness therefore has no object of recognition to recognize and is themselves not recognized either. Recognition requires the reciprocal interaction of two self-consciousnesses recognizing each other, which death has made impossible. This failed outcome of the process is limited to the state of nature, where individuals relate to each other only as means to the end of their own survival and know no other relationship than this. 

In civil society, in the state of law, the life or death struggle for recognition no longer makes sense. The individuals here have a third party, the law, which connects them and in which they are inherently recognized. As a citizen, I recognize the other a free person and know that I am recognized as such by the other. In this relationship, the individuality of my consciousness is elevated into universality, and only through this universality is it truly free. The general recognition, which was to be brought about only through struggle, is already present here in and of itself and is the absolute prerequisite of society. The duel, even the judicial duel in the ordeals of the Middle Ages, is a regression to the state of nature, which still had not yet disappeared in the rule of might of those times wherein the historical justification of those customs is contained. But in general, it always remains a matter of chance whether the victor was in the right, even if the cause itself had granted unexpected strength to the weaker party.

Death is only the negative resolution of the contradiction through which the victor has simultaneously created a greater one, for instead of achieving the intended recognition of their freedom through genuine self-consciousness, they now see themselves recognized only in a dead corpse. Since the combatants have come to the realization that life is just as essential as freedom and recognition is impossible without life, one individual chooses life, preserving themselves as an individual self-consciousness, but relinquishing recognition by the other by submitting to the other's will to avoid being killed. The will of the other, who held the first individual's life in their hands, is now the universal, and the individual retains their freedom and finds recognition in the other without the other recognizing them in return. This is the relationship of mastery and servitude, the first step out of the state of nature. 

What has been gained through the relationship of master and servant is, first of all, the suppression of selfishness. The self-consciousnesses that are in this relationship are still naturally opposed to each other, but at the same time, each already cares for the other because a community of need binds them together. The master is sustained by the labor of the servant and finds their self-consciousness in the services of the latter, since the servant is reduced to a means of subsistence by being in the other's possession. Yet, this living possession is very different from inanimate property. For since the life of the servant is just as necessary as that of the master for the latter's recognition, the master must in turn sustain the servant in order to sustain themselves as their master. Here we see not only the individual satisfaction of the needs, but the general provision and reciprocity of satisfaction.

The deficiency of this relationship is the inequality still present within it, which will be overcome through its inner dialectic. If the advantage initially seemed to be on the side of the master, it will likewise be found on the side of the servant. The master's existence is sustained by the servant, namely, by means of providing for and satisfying their every need, for the servant gives up enjoyment in order to serve the master's selfishness through hard labor. But in this way, the servant gains back from universal self-consciousness what they had lost from recognition. By working for another, they have relinquished their own ego, their selfishness, and through obedience becomes capable of participating in and realizing universal ends. Through the surrender of their ego, the servant stands beyond the master who is still a prisoner to selfish ends. Because the servant forgets themselves in the other and lives only for them, even enduring the excesses of the master's whims, they have attained universal self-consciousness and thus true sociability. The negation of individuality becomes a habit for them through work, and the fear of the master is the beginning of wisdom. Since, through this harsh pressure, the servant has also attained universal self-consciousness, which originally belonged only to the master, the feeling of equality between the two individuals arises in their shared concern for their needs, and the equality between them is the source of their mutual recognition.

### Friendship

The conclusion of the recognition process is friendship, where each individual has given up the demands of their ego, shows goodwill towards the other, and makes the other's well-being their own goal. Friendship is thus, according to Aristotle, a mutually active goodwill. Only in friendship is the spiritual unity of two self-consciousnesses established in that each recognizes the other as free and an end in itself. Just as friendship has its basis in equality, it is also maintained the longest by this equality. If each person does for the other with what the other does for them, then the friendship is lasting. 

The first type of friendship has personal ends as its object, but whose satisfaction has been transformed so that each seeks personal ends not only for themselves, but also for the other. This friendship is the friendship of pleasure or recreation. But since the wills recognize each other as spiritual beings, the goal is not merely pure sensory pleasure, but the pleasantness of social interaction. Such friendships are particularly common in childhood and adolescence, but they are not long-lasting as the basis of the friendship often changes, and as the years pass, different things captivate the mind so that one soon moves on to new friends. Nevertheless, in this friendship one loves the other for what they are and for their own sake, not for what they do or give to each other.

As the person grows older and reaches adulthood, they begin move away from seeking enjoyment and pleasure as such and make the acquisition of lasting possessions as their object. In securing a lasting existence for ourselves through possessions we encounter the second type of friendship, which can be termed the friendship of benefit or utility since possessions in general are what we can use as a means for our self-preservation. Each person does not love what the other is, but loves what they accomplish, so the friendship dissolves as soon as circumstances change and one no longer hopes for a reciprocal service commensurate with their own contribution. In this type of friendship, regular social interaction is no longer a necessity because each person only looks out for their own advantage, but at the same time, disputes and conflicts more easily arise among such friends. Since each is inclined to consider what they have received as less valuable than what they themselves have given, such friends tend to reproach each other for unequal contributions, even though the equality that constitutes the essence of friendship has not been violated.

The true satisfaction of desire in friendship is that one friend becomes the purpose of the other, not according to an isolated incentive, such as a social or material advantage, but that each desires the well-being of the other because it is their own well-being. Both self-consciousnesses have merged into one another by being for one another, which forms the basis of the community of needs. A true friend, as the proverb says, is one soul that dwells in two bodies. Self-consciousness is now universal self-consciousness, and this unity of personal and social ends is the basis for all legal and moral relationships. Aristotle, in his Ethics, understands this as encompassing various kinds of friendship: marital friendship, sibling friendship, civic friendship, etc. In this context, the commonality of needs has become such an absolute necessity that the labor each person performs for their own preservation benefits all others by increasing the products that satisfy them. Despite this, personal and social ends are not unified by necessity in friendship, for their unification is only an incidental consequence of the satisfaction of desire, which varies contingently from person to person.

## Universal Self-Consciousness

The contingency of desires in friendship is due to the particularity of individuals and the circumstances under which recognition takes place. Apart from this, the particularity of desires in friendships is always suspended on the condition that the self is conscious of its identity with the other self. In being indifferent to its difference, each self is objective to the other and recognizes themselves in them. This self-consciousness that recognizes itself in the other is universal self-consciousness, the common bond of reason that establishes their identity as self-consciousnesses. Moreover, since universal self-consciousness is directed toward finding reason in the other, the desire for recognition has now become the desire for truth. In knowledge, self-consciousness unites with an object, which is no longer a simple, sensory thing, but is the universal existence of reason itself, which constitutes the essence of the subject just as much as it constitutes the essence of the object.

It is only in knowledge that personal and social ends are perfectly reconciled by necessity. For instance, when I share something with my friend, even though I would gladly let them have what I have, I am still only able to enjoy it indirectly through their enjoyment. For knowledge, however, the division between personal and social ends ceases to exist since you can never be deprived of what you know, no matter how many people you share it with. The knowledge that one person has never diminishes the knowledge of another, on the contrary, knowledge grows all the more as it is communicated from one person to the next, and the common striving of several allows them all to enjoy the same thing all the more readily. Therefore, the proverb docendo discimus is also correct: the more I impart my acquired knowledge to others, the more clearly, distinctly, and richly that knowledge grows within me. 

The absolute interconnection of personal and social ends, however, does not occur merely in relation to subjects, but also with regard to the object of knowledge. Knowledge relates to objects in a manner that is self-serving, incorporating them to increase its comprehensiveness as knowledge, but it is also equally sociable, preserving the freedom of each as the freedom all. In the desiring knowledge, the subject relinquishes its personal ends in favor of the object, which does not alternate from one specific content to another, but is completely adequate to the development of the object as a universal. The specific content of the object itself has been extended to universality, but has thereby mastered the entire activity of the subject, and thus bound the subject to itself.
