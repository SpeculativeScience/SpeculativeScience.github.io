# Quantity

## Pure Quantity (Geometry)

```
Continuous ( Discrete ) = Geometry
[ Continuous → ( Discrete ) ] ⟹ Unit
[ Continuous ← ( Discrete ) ] ⟹ Amount
[ Continuous ⇄ ( Discrete ) ] ⟹ Number
```

Quantity is the sublation of the One and the Many.

A Continuous Quantity is the Attraction of the Many within the One, whereas a Discrete Quantity is the Repulsion of the Many within the One.

Discrete Quantities that comprise a Continuous Quantity is an Amount, whereas a Continuous Quantity that is comprised of Discrete Quantities is a Unit. The determined relation between an Amount and a Unit is a Number.

For instance, we can think of the number 12 either as an amount of units (e.g. 12 of 1 unit is 12) or as a unit of amount (e.g. 1 as 12 units is 12). This can be made more explicit in divisors of numbers. In the case of 12, if you were to determine its unit of amount as 6, its corresponding amount of units is 2 (6x2 = 12).

## Quanta (Arithmetic)

```
Unit ( Amount ) = Number
[ Unit → ( Amount ) ] ⟹ Extensive Quantity
[ Unit ← ( Amount ) ] ⟹ Intensive Quantity
[ Unit ⇄ ( Amount ) ] ⟹ Ratio
```

A Number which is an Amount of Units is an Extensive Quantity, while a Number that is a Unit of an Amount is an Intensive Quantity. 

Extensive Quantities are cardinal numbers, they can comprise or fit into each other exactly without remainders (e.g. 3 fits into 9 three times). Intensive Quantities, on the other hand, are ordinal numbers, they derive their meaning as degrees on a scale (e.g. 3 and 9 are 6 degrees apart).

An Intensive Quantity can have a value at a point in space, whereas an Extensive Magnitude cannot. For instance, ask you what the temperature (intensive) of a metal plate was at at a specific point, you could calculate the exact temperature at the point I asked for. However, if I were to ask you what the mass of the metal plate was at the same point, my question wouldn’t any sense since mass is an Amount of Units, not a Unit of an Amount. 

We can make this more explicit by way of example: 
Take r to be a real number. We have two ways of interpreting r, as a magnitude. One way is to take it to be an element of the real line. Another way is to take r to represent the size/cardinality of the interval [0, r]. This interval is extensive, in the sense that it refers to the total collection of its members, but not "itself" (the analogue to this idea in set theory is called a fusion). However, if you just take the upper boundary of this interval, you have isolated its intensive magnitude which refers to itself by excluding itself from other numbers (and this very act of exclusion determines what it is!)

Quantitative Change must either move towards the infinitely small or infinitely great. The infinitely small Quantitative Change that moves towards, but never reaches 0, is the Infinitesimal. The infinitely great Quantitative Change that moves towards, but never reaches ∞, is the Infinite Series. This negation process is negated by the Ratio, a quantitative relation of two Numbers that are self-external to each other (and not endlessly towards an indefinite beyond).

## Ratio (Algebra)

```
Ratio ⟹ C = y/x
C ⟹ Quality
A/B ⟹ Quantity
(Quantity - Quality) ⟹ Measure
```

The Ratio brings together two Numbers and expresses their quantative relation as a third Number. 

The Direct Ratio is of the general form [y = Cx], where an increase in one Number results in a proportional increase in the other Number. The proportion in question is the constant [k]. This is made more explicit if we were to divide both sides of the equation by [x], giving us [C = y/x].

The Inverse Ratio takes the general form of [xy = C]. The ratio of two Numbers are said to be inverses if an increase in one Number results in a proportional decrease in the other Number. This is because both Numbers are limited in their relation to a constant [k], which must remain unchanged by definition. Although, if we were to divide one Number from both sides of the equation, giving us [y = C/x], the constant [C] is now an Amount divisible into a number of [x] Units equivalent to an Amount of Units [y]. In this arrangement, neither Number can ever be exactly equal to [k] because such a result would be inconsistent (division by zero) or trivial (if y = C, then C = C/x, so x = 1).

It is only in the Ratio of Powers [y = C^(x)] that a Number becomes a different Number in and through multiplying itself with itself (exponentiation). The Number, in becoming another
Number, constitutes the Ratio as an enduring Quality that determines the alteration of Quantity within itself. This immediate relation of Quality to Quantity is Measure.
