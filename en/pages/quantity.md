# Quantity

> This webpage is a direct translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Encyclopedia Logic* (1876) 

The contradiction of quantity lies more precisely in the fact that, because of the independence of the many ones, their boundary with respect to one another is restored, and at the same time, because they are all equal as ones, that their boundary also remains abolished. Finitude reappears in infinity and at the same time disappears again. The concept of quantity is therefore to be a limit that is not limited, the limit can be set anywhere but it is indifferent to wherever it is set. A meadow remains a meadow whether it is a hundred or fifty feet long, its quality as a meadow is the same whether it is great or small. If quality constitutes the being of something, then quantity is indifferent to this being. The common definition of quantity or magnitude, as that which can be increased or decreased, contains this correct moment of the indifference of the limit, but is deficient in that it already includes what is to be defined as a part of its definition. To define magnitude, one must not say that it is that which can be increased or decreased, since one has not yet explained what increasing or decreasing means for quantity. Instead, quantity must first be considered from the side of infinity as pure magnitude, then from the finitude as number, and lastly in the relation of both sides to each other as the ratio.

## Magnitude

```
Attraction ⟹ Continuous

Repulsion ⟹ Discrete

[ Continuous → Discrete ] ⟹ Unit

[ Continuous ← Discrete ] ⟹ Amount

[ Continuous ⇄ Discrete ] ⟹ Magnitude
```

The first thing to be emphasized about quantity is the indifference of the limit. For this is the determination that has resulted from the ideality that all ones are one and the same. But even in this abolished limit, the two sides of attraction and repulsion that were connected in quantity still remain in it. On the side of attraction, the unity of ones is its continuity, while on the side of repulsion, the plurality is ones its discreteness. Yet, both sides are again inseparably linked to one another.

### Continuity

If the many is not distinguished from the one, but transcends limitation in being one and the same one, then we have the continuity of quantity. Because limit is indifferent to where it is possible to transcend it because nowhere is there a final, absolute limit set for quantity. Quantity is, in this respect, infinitely great. But this infinity is only implicit, because in order to be able to go beyond every limit, there must always be something beyond the limit. If there were nothing beyond the limit, then to go beyond it would indeed be something limited or finite. The infinitely great is therefore only a negative concept that prohibits the existence of a greatest quantity, since every assumed greatest magnitude posits an even greater quantity beyond itself.

### Discreteness

On the other hand, holding to the side of finitude, each one stands apart from the other distinguishing itself from it, forming the moment of discreteness in quantity. The infinite unity of continuity becomes an infinite multiplicity of limited units. Yet, each unit is the one and the same unit. Every discrete one is not a fixed limit that would exclude the indifference of the limit, for otherwise it would cease to be a quantity. Because every one is still quantitative, it encloses a multiplicity within itself. Within each of the many, as a quantitative one, is again another many, and so on ad infinitum. Thus, there is no smallest quantity, no matter how small a thing is there is always something even smaller. The infinitely small is just as much a negative concept as the infinitely large was before. It is therefore completely correct to regard the infinitesimal as not a quantity at all since there is no quantity in existence that is equivalent to it.

### Specific Magnitude

But if pure discreteness, like pure continuity, is the complete cessation of the limit as well as its sole positing, then what remains of quantity? Only the unity of discreteness and continuity exists, but not as an ideal unity of mere moments of quantity. No matter how long one might want to proceed out into the infinitely great or small, every stage at which one stops is a reality where continuity can be found in discreteness and discreteness can be found in continuity. Thus, there are no discrete or continuous magnitudes in and of themselves.

## Quanta 

```
[ Unit → Amount ] ⟹ Extensive Quantity

[ Unit ← Amount ] ⟹ Intensive Quantity

[ Extensive Quantity → Intensive Quantity ] ⟹ Infinitesimal

[ Extensive Quantity ← Intensive Quantity ] ⟹ Infinity

[ Extensive Quantity ⇄ Intensive Quantity ] ⟹ Ratio
```

That discreteness is posited in every continuity implies that every magnitude is limited and therefore finite. There are only finite magnitudes because there exists no maximum or minimum quantity. Finite magnitude is therefore a quanta, the quantitative exists only as a quanta. Just as the one and the many generated the moments continuity and discreteness, so too will the moments of quanta generate its own mediation of categories.

### Extensive Quantity

The quanta, as the finite or limited quantity, cannot do without continuity or unity of the many. However, since quanta must set a limit to this continuous progression, this implies that its continuity is interrupted by discreteness. A quanta that is continous as an amount and discrete as a unit is an extensive quantity. But if discreteness is what limits the extension of extensive quantity, then each limit that separates and determines the extension is itself a quanta. In other words, the limit is discrete as an amount and continuous as a unit, an intensive quantity.

### Intensive Quantity

In intensive magnitude, the limit to quantity has become a quantity itself. If in extensive magnitude, continuity as a moment is lost in discreteness, the moment of discreteness is now lost in continuity. The limited quanta is still a simple unity, yet it immediately follows that extensive and intensive are no more absolute opposites than discretion and continuity were before. Every magnitude is both extensive and intensive at the same time, the only difference is whether it is more or less. Extension and intension thus inversely determine to each other, an amount of units can be a unit of amount and vice versa. 

### Degree

The mutual determination of extensive quantity and intensive quantity is the degree. A degree of temperature is an intensive quantity that is qualitatively different from other degrees. But at the same time, each degree of temperature presents itself on a thermometer as a certain extension of the mercury column, and this extension, conversely, determines the degree of temperature. Thus, the degree is the first dissolution of the concept of quantity. For instead of the limit being indifferent to quantity, remaining a determination entirely external to being and quality, the quantitative limit has now itself become the determining factor of being's quality. The twentieth degree is only this because it stands midway between the nineteenth and the twenty-first, and is determined by them. But this external determination is rather its determinateness in-and-for-itself. The dialectical nature of quantity has been reached in degree because the external determination has become its own determinateness and indifference is now immanent to it. At the same time, quantity has transcended itself, passing from an external determination to an internal one. However, immanent determination is something contradictory because it arises solely from an external limit, it is remains indifferent to that which determines it. On the other hand, however, the external determination in turn remains indifferent to what is determined immanently since the extension established by the intension is not determined in-and-for-itself (e.g. the twentieth degree of mercury expands differently than a column of alcohol, due to the difference heat capacity).

The concept of degree can be made even more explicit by way of example: Take r to be a real number. We have two ways of interpreting r, as a magnitude. One way is to take it to be an element of the real line. Another way is to take r to represent the size/cardinality of the interval [0, r]. This interval is extensive, in the sense that it refers to the total collection of its members, but not "itself". However, if you just take the upper boundary of this interval, you have isolated its intensive magnitude which refers to itself by excluding itself from other numbers (and this very act of exclusion determines what it is!).

## Ratio 

```
Ratio ⟹ C = A/B

C ⟹ Quality

A/B ⟹ Quantity

Ratio ⟹ Measure
```

If according to the first contradiction, the immanent determinacy is changed by an external determinacy, while in the second contradiction, the immanent determinacy remains unchanged, then in order to resolve both contradictions, we now have to recognize how they are interconnected. The answer requires the concept of externally determined quantity that remains what it is despite quantitative change. The concept of quantity that remains the same through quantitative change is the ratio. For if the external quanta of a variable series, each two in relation to each other, exhibit only the same quantitative determination relative to each other, then their ratio remains the same regardless of their change in series, whether as 1 : 2, 2 : 4, 4 : 8, etc. 

The Direct Ratio is of the general form [y = Cx], where an increase in one Number results in a proportional increase in the other Number. The proportion in question is the constant [k]. This is made more explicit if we were to divide both sides of the equation by [x], giving us [C = y/x].

The Inverse Ratio takes the general form of [xy = C]. The ratio of two Numbers are said to be inverses if an increase in one Number results in a proportional decrease in the other Number. This is because both Numbers are limited in their relation to a constant [k], which must remain unchanged by definition. Although, if we were to divide one Number from both sides of the equation, giving us [y = C/x], the constant [C] is now an Amount divisible into a number of [x] Units equivalent to an Amount of Units [y]. In this arrangement, neither Number can ever be exactly equal to [k] because such a result would be inconsistent (division by zero) or trivial (if y = C, then C = C/x, so x = 1).

It is only in the Ratio of Powers [y = C^(x)] that a Number becomes a different Number in and through multiplying itself with itself (exponentiation). The Number, in becoming another
Number, constitutes the Ratio as an enduring Quality that determines the alteration of Quantity within itself.

The exponent is in the other in-itself and for-itself as a quantum. In it, therefore, immanence has become externality, and externality has become immanence. Quality is restored in the quantitative in the midst of its loss. This unity of quality and quantity, this ideal quantity and quantitative ideality, an externalizing, self-realizing quality, is the measure.
