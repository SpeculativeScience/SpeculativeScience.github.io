# Astronomy

Note: This webpage is an adapted translation and commentary of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Encyclopedia Nature* (1876) 

The falling body has indeed already taken a step toward astronomy, since it had abolished the externality that still clung to bodies that repelled one another in dynamics. However, since the free fall has brought it back to the unity of the primary body, it has only given attraction precedence over repulsion. Thus, free fall has not yet been able to completely eliminate the externality of the two immanent determinations of gravity with respect to one another, and has therefore left them with the appearance of independence without being able to balance them. But because they have now regained their innermost concept in astronomy, the primary body not only attracts the body removed from it, but also removes it through its own action. With this, distances also cease to be accidental and are determined by gravity as well. Indeed, the distance of the body at the periphery is also a repulsion from the center, determined by its own gravity, through which it also attracts the center. Center and periphery are now equal in that all points both attract and repel all points in all directions, which necessarily takes the shape of a sphere. But instead of the centers of activity then, in the mere concept of gravity, being merely mathematical points, they are now the real bodies of dynamics which, as spheres, have the relationship of attraction and repulsion to one another. We can now properly call this a celestial body, which are themselves active participants in the eternity of the world. 

The mathematical concept of gravity has now immersed itself in actual masses and gravity has thus fully realized its concept and risen from the ideality of striving to concrete, actual activity. The division of its concept is such that we have at first the general concept of gravitation, second the specific laws governing it, and the totality of celestial bodies and gravity united in the harmony of the universe, the study of which is cosmology.

## Gravity

The possibility of celestial motion seems to follow from the multiplicity of celestial bodies that now arises, in contrast to the unity of the universe. If we were to designate only a regular sphere, floating equally in all directions, its perfect equilibrium seems to permit no other state than that of absolute rest. But even the multiplicity of regular spheres seems to be incapable of producing any change. For, because of their regularity, all their points would be equally related to the general center as if they did not belong to different spheres, and so it would be absolutely incomprehensible how these spheres could be drawn into the flow of motion.

Regardless of the multiplicity of masses, the difficulty of introducing motion into this stagnation seems to be the same. The individual bodies of the same system, through the reciprocity of their repulsion and attraction, are not only suspended, but are also in restlessly circulating movement. The question, however, is precisely where this "restlessly circulating movement" comes from and why the individual bodies "do not only remain suspended." This is the difficulty presented by the three body problem. But the mathematical explanation of motion through the assumption of three bodies is completely invalid. Even three or more bodies, wherever they may be, if they are regular spheres, would always have to attract and repel each other uniformly at equal radii, as if they were one system, and thus would never be able to achieve real rotation from the striving for movement. In addition, the inclusion of the third body to deduce the motion is completely unnecessary, because binary stars, where the third body is therefore missing, nevertheless orbit around their common barycenter.

The question for philosophy now is, first of all, to provide evidence of the possibility of absolutely free motion. The key to the solution is not the multiplicity of the celestial bodies, but the inherent irregularity of their structure. Celestial bodies are not perfect spheres, they all have a slight preponderance of mass toward one side or the other, so the spherical bodies can rather be more accurately called spheroidal. Therefore, the apparent stasis of gravitational bodies has immediately passed over into its kinematics, just as in dynamics, the inequality of masses is what produced motion. For the spheroids now gravitate restlessly in the direction in which their larger half, and thus their heavier weight, lies, no matter how small the excess. Only in this way is gravitational motion possible, because an equilibrium of mass would immediately bring immanent motion to a standstill. Since in this way an external reason for the cessation of the movement is lacking, and an internal reason for its continuation is present, the movement is eternal. 

The second question that must be raised, however, is where this movement takes place. If the irregularity of the many spheroids within themselves only demonstrates their movement in general, then the direction of the movement will emerge from the comparison of the given spheroids with each other. They are not only irregular in themselves, but also different from each other, even if initially only quantitatively as masses. That which has the greatest mass will protrude itself into the center, toward which all the others gravitate. But since they are not merely attracted to their center, like dependent bodies in falling motion, but repulsion and attraction are in absolute equilibrium, they will repel from the primary body just as much as they are attracted to it. As they approach it, they will also move away from it. The celestial bodies must not represent the identity of attraction and repulsion as a smoothly floating equilibrium through a mere paralysis of both directions, they must represent it as a concrete identity, constantly restoring itself from the difference. Thus, the contradiction of opposites is no longer merely broken, but has in fact developed into a higher unity through living mediation. This concrete unity of convergence and divergence, however, is only attainable in a curved line returning to itself, which, as such, already forms a surface in itself. The circling of the primary body by the body of the periphery signifies, as attraction, its dependence, the return from this circling to itself, as repulsion, its independence. Since both opposite directions, as in the curve, are one, therein lies the balancing of independence and dependence. However, the repulsion would still be incompletely expressed if the peripheral body gravitated only toward the center of the system. For the repulsion to be fully effective, all points of each individual body must also gravitate around their own center. And they can only do so because the heavier half of the spheroid leads the others. This is the axial rotation in which every body, just as it represents its being for others in the eternal search for its external center, so it asserts its being-for-itself in the eternal finding of its own center. Not only does the body of the periphery move around itself and around the primary body. Yet since repulsion and attraction are just as much the immanent determinates of the primary body as of all others, it too rotates both around itself and the center of the entire system.

What remains is to further define gravitation, according to its moments, thus as space and time, in reality. The task, however, is that although attraction and repulsion must never prevail over one another, they should nevertheless express their opposition in motion. This can only be achieved by thinking of gravity toward the center as an approach to it, and the return of the peripheral body to itself as a distance from the center. However, in a circular orbit, such a thing cannot occur at all, since all points on the periphery are equally distant from the center. The alternating occurrence of approach and distance can also be empirically justified and demonstrated without disturbing the balance between attraction and repulsion. For when, on the obliquity of the ecliptic,
the peripheral body, with its greater mass directed toward the primary body, has closest approached it, the highest energy of the bodies' gravitational attraction toward each other, as if taken to its extreme, transforms into greater relaxation, since the strongest attraction, as a moving motion, also includes the strongest repulsion, as a stationary action. By turning the head away from the primary body, thus by the relative release of a larger mass of the peripheral body from the influence of the center, the body now gravitates more powerfully toward itself, enters, as it were, its own trajectory, and thus moves further away from the center. It throws itself into a state of repulsion. But just as previously, at the greatest proximity to the center, gravity leaped from its highest energy to gradual relaxation, so now, at the greatest distance, the relaxed activity intensifies itself again to ever more intense energy. The peripheral body leaves its resting motion to return to moving motion and strive toward the center. 

The irregularity of the shape of a moving body and the irregularity of its trajectory are thus inextricably linked. Just as the space of celestial motion, as a trajectory, cannot have the one-sided regularity of a circle, just as little can the second moment of motion, time, possess such uniformity. If we were to assume, contrary to the principles, an impossible motion of celestial bodies in a circle, then we would have to restrict both space and time to a completely uniform regularity. As in mathematics and dynamics, the law of motion would then read: "Equal spaces are traversed in equal times," because the complete indistinguishability of attraction and repulsion would not allow any difference in speed. Rather, celestial motion differs not only from the aforementioned completely unfree motion, but also from the conditionally free motion of falling. For when a celestial body undergoes moving motion or attraction, it must rush toward the universal center with greater speed; when it undergoes stationary motion or repulsion, it must retreat to its own center with greater slowness. This completes, even for the appearance in time, the opposition of the two moments of gravity, attraction and repulsion, with the unwavering identity of their essence. Now, however, it is clear that while in the case where only attraction appears as immanent, the acceleration can only be uniformly accelerated, in absolutely free motion, where repulsion also appears as an equally legitimate source of motion, the manifestations of both activities must also appear in time as opposite: i.e. the uniformly accelerated and deccelerated motion alternate with each other, so that within the non-uniformity and irregularity, the uniform regularity of time is restored, but as a concrete return from the opposite. Having developed the general concept of gravitation up to this point, it is now a matter of presenting it in its realization geometrically and arithmetically as the phenomenon of celestial motion, in space and time, as well as in the relationships between the two. Kepler demonstrated this in his three laws of orbital motion, which is the subject to which we now turn.

## Orbital Motion 

Kepler's laws of orbital motion state that, first, the shape of an orbit is an ellipse with the primary body at one of the two foci, second, the radius of the orbit and the perpendicular velocity of the orbiting body are inversely related, and third, the square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis.

### Law of Orbits

The first of Keplerian laws establishes the ellipticity of orbits, which is the basis for the irregularity of space. The spheroidal shape of the celestial body need not introduce any irregularity of motion, since the entire mass as an undivided system, completes its rotation around itself. But because there exists the dual tendency in motion around the primary body, the relationship to itself and the relationship to something else, the question now arises as to how this contrast of approach and distance, of acceleration and decceleration, which is excluded in the case of a circle, is represented in the true line of revolution. Since the body of the periphery refers first to its primary body and then again to its own center of gravity, two foci must fall within the trajectory, the first of which represents the center of the approach brought about by accelerated velocity, the other the center of the distance brought about by decceleration. Such a curve with two centers and a longest and a shortest radius, calculated from one of the centers, and therefore also with one larger and two smaller axes, is called an ellipse among conic sections. Kepler therefore established as his first law that celestial bodies move not in circles, as Newton wanted, but in ellipses around their primary body. If Copernicus, standing midway between Newton and Kepler, still retained the circular line but asserted the eccentricity of motion, then one half of his assumption contradicts the other, because there can be no eccentric orbit in a circle. 

But if astronomy in its present form does not yet come into being a posteriori, there is no limitation on the deduction of to these higher functions a priori. For instance, if an orbiting body approaches the primary body with accelerated speed, the curved line of its trajectory must be different from the one by which it slowly moves away from. For that case, it must swirl around it as in a spiral, while in this case, it must stretch out its path as in a tangent. But the striking velocity of the contrast must also be expressed in the different curvature of the trajectory, as before in the approach and the distance, in the acceleration and the decceleration. One half of the ellipse must be closer to the circle, the other more oval, more eccentric, so the two minor axes must therefore be of unequal length not only with respect to the major axes, but also with respect to each other. This is found in the ellipse, or the ovoid line. The truer shap of the orbit is an ellipse, and from this it follows that the spheroid that moves along this ellipse must also have this eccentricity with respect to its shape. The ellipse, with its round and pointed ends, is therefore the most optimal path for absolutely free motion.

### Law of Areas

The second law then states the relationship of orbital areas to periods. Namely, if the velocity goes from a maximum through a mean to a minimum, and then increases again, the question arises: what is the law of this decreasing and increasing velocity, i.e. what is the relationship between space and time in any given part of the trajectory of one and the same body? Kepler's second law, which states this relationship, can be derived from the first. Since the body of the center is located at the single focus of the ellipsoid, the further the spheroid of the periphery moves away from this center, the shorter and more rectilinear the arc must become in the same period of time. The closer it approaches its center, the larger and more curved it becomes. Since the smaller the arc traversed, the greater the distance of the body passing through it from the center, a smaller arc corresponds to a larger radius vector, and a larger arc to a smaller radius vector. The mutual, indirect relationship between the two elements of the curve is uniform, since the increasing speed and slowness were also uniform. Arc and radius are thus in a constant relationship to each other. The longer curved arc traversed in the same time is related to the shorter straight one as the radii vectors of the latter are to those of the former. 

Kepler expressed this law as follows: Equal sectors are traversed in equal times. The irregularity returns to uniformity, but as uniformity, not as abstract equality. If we develop the mathematical content presented in this analogy, then, as in the law of falling bodies, it is a multiplication of time into space. However, this multiplication takes on a completely different form here. Time is indeed squared here as well in space; for a sector, as a surface, is the square of a line. However, the squaring is no longer a mere straight line, as in the falling motion, but since a curved line immediately becomes a surface, time is squared not only arithmetically as a numerical power, but also geometrically in the surface. The fact that the power does not rise above the square, just as in the law of falling, is due to the fact that in this Keplerian analogy, similar to Galileo's law, an empirical factor is still present. Just as there the first second appeared on the surface of the Earth as 15 feet, so here is the accidental tearing out of an arbitrary piece of arc, thus the arbitrary assumption of precisely this specific distance of the peripheral body from the central body. That this last contingency also disappears, the arbitrary distance of the peripheral body from the center and the relationship of all orbits to their times, is the task of the third law.

### Law of Periods

For this third law, if we now seek not the empirical numbers, which the great astronomer Kepler had discover, but rather the speculative necessity of their relationship, the question arises thus how the total orbital period of one peripheral body must relate to that of another. Since the curve of the orbit has a constant relationship to the radius vector, it is proportional to the distance. The orbit will therefore turn out to be proportional to the mean distance. In the orbital period, time is no longer bound to an empirical part of space, but flows as the totality of time purely from the concept of motion itself, and as such relates to the totality of space. Since it is now entirely determined by the concept of motion, it must henceforth appear as absolutely free or self-determined. However, since it must also retain its nature in motion, its quantitative relation to itself is what is expressed by the square. The term of the relationship thus becomes the square of the orbital period. These are now supposed to relate to space as producing it. The squares of the times that become spaces could well be the biquadrates, for the square squaring itself is the fourth power. But this is not the relationship discovered by Kepler, and it should not be enough for us to reassure ourselves, like Lagrange, that the nature of this relationship cannot be demonstrated. We must prove the necessity of the cubic, and this lies in the nature of time as well as of space. Time does not deny its inherent nature of simplicity. Thus, it multiplies its square only by the root and space, as a multiplied totality, is also completely satisfied with the cubic, for this is its third dimension and it does not go beyond this third. The law that Kepler discovered from the comparative calculations of all times and orbits of the various celestial bodies is thus this: "The squares of times are related as the cubes of mean distances." Thus, for example, the cube of the mean distance of the Earth is related to the cube of the mean distance of Venus as the square of the orbital period of the Earth is related to the square of the orbital period of Venus. 

It is indeed an undeniable merit of Newton that his formula for the law, according to which "the force of gravity is inversely proportional to the squares of the mean distances," offers great advantages for mathematical treatment. It is clear that, while according to Keplerian law one has to calculate with the cubics, in Newton's formula one only has to deal with the second power; which is a significant simplification with such large numbers. Then, Newton's formula contains a generalization in that he reduced the law of celestial motion and the law of falling bodies as expressions of one an the same law, thus making gravitation the fundamental law of all nature. Finally, he actually added one to the discoveries falsely attributed to him, which can be counted as his third achievement in this field. This is the principle of perturbation. When one tried to calculate the actual orbits of celestial bodies according to Kepler's law, the numbers did not agree perfectly, but only approximately. And Newton very correctly explained the reason for this by saying that not only one peripheral body is attracted by the central body, but all spheroids are attract each other, and thus their path is prevented from being mathematically accurate. These perturbations even led to the discovery of the planets Uranus and Neptune. For he conceives of gravity only as attraction, as falling. His formula therefore only intends to express the falling, which is precisely attraction, and that which is attractive in celestial motion, namely the falling of the Moon into the radius line of the Earth. Without knowing it or wanting to, however, he has also expressed the entirety of celestial motion, as it appears as the identity of attraction and repulsion. Newton's formula thus proves most strikingly that the centripetal force is also the centrifugal force.

The concept of perturbation implies that the movement of celestial bodies is not merely a mathematical interplay of circumference and center, but that, since all spheroids exert this activity of attraction and repulsion against other spheroids, their entire mass always participates in gravitation. Thus, gravitation interferes with the movement of each individual body, interconnecting and causing them to undergo certain modifications. This includes the perturbations of pure elliptical orbits, as well as the deviations in the orbital periods. It is no longer a question of the relationship between two or three celestial bodies gravitating against each other. Rather, all celestial bodies are bound together by the laws of gravity into one individual, which acts in all its members according to one general law. These disturbances go so far that even the self-returning curve seems to disappear, since the disturbances prevent the individual celestial body from returning to the point from which it had started before the complete reversal. However, this does not prevent the entire universe from being regarded as a self-gravitating individuality, which must now be considered in the next subject of cosmology.

## Cosmology

If we previously conceived the circumference as a curved line in which a peripheral spheroid moved, and the center as a mathematical point in which a central spheroid rested, these mathematical figurations have now become real systems of bodies into which the universe is divided.

### Nebulae

The planetary nebulae, exhibit an equally mild intensity of light in all their parts, and by ejecting their materials outward, provide for the origin of planetary systems. They are regarded as haze-like rings, orbiting the center of the nebula through the force of attraction, later clumping into spheres as their cooling and solidification progresses.
