# Imagination

Note: This webpage is a direct translation of Karl Ludwig Michelet's *Anthropology and Psychology, Vol. II* (1840) 

The imagination, which stands midway between sensibility and thought, processes the material that sensibility supplies to it, just as the craftsman shapes the raw materials obtained by the farmer. As the intellectual faculty of raising sensuous material to thought, the imagination brings to it form and universality. The imagination shares with sensibility the dependence on the given content, and with thought the freedom of self-expression. Within the imagination, progress can only consist in increasingly shedding dependence on the object of sensibility and allowing thought to break through ever more clearly. Accordingly, the capacities of the imagination are summarized under the following three powers. The images of sensible things that are reproduced in the mind are combined, as long as they are of the same kind, into a general image that can be recalled at any moment by the faculty of memory. Secondly, the mind gives the representations objectivity by relating them back to familiar images, associating the abstractions with each other in the form of fantasy. Lastly, in task of the faculty of signification is that this existence of the representation does not revert to sensible individuality, but rather remains in the form of universality as a sign or symbol, thus being a suitable expression for conveying of meaning.

## Memory

The perception, as the linking of the manifold into a unity through space, thus becomes an image of the object within me, created by the mind, which I deduce from the matter of this object. This image, through its transplantation into the realm of the intelligible, has acquired a more permanent duration than it previously possessed. The image is no longer dependent on the transience of the physical impression. This has a twofold cause: either the perishing of the object or my withdrawing my presence from it. Even if I turn away from the object of my perception and thus abandon the spatial and temporal connection in which I stand with it, I can still hold on to the perception I have had and prolong it beyond the duration of the actual impression. This imagining of the perception in the mind is the first activity of the imagination. The image is no longer before me, but exists solely in my mind, it is torn from the objective connection of space and time that it had with the other objects of perception. However, this does not abolish every relationship of the image to space, rather as something viewed and perceived, it must remain in spatial and temporal connection. By taking it from the time and context of the outside world, I place it in my space and time.

The basis of memory is the accumulation of images within the mind, but in such a way that the conscious nature of this image also disappears again. For since the mind has had an infinite number of experiences and is forced by attention to focus on only one, it must also let this infinity of images fall away again, as one displaces the other. These images, forgotten and suspended, are also equally preserved, albeit unconsciously. Once they have been in the mind, their property remains unlost. Nature loses everything it produces, it has no memory and must always begin again after every setback. In the infinite wealth of the spirit, however, all its acquisitions are counted and recorded, although what has been stored up for a very long time and not refreshed can never be brought to the surface of consciousness, or only through extraordinary circumstances. The spirit can thus be compared to a deep shaft, whose depth is unfathomable and whose contents are therefore inexhaustible.

But how is this wealth, when forgotten, is as good as none, exploited again? To this accumulated pile of images, which the mind cannot even count because of its infinity, a multitude of new images are added daily and hourly, indeed, one might say, at every moment. How do these new perceptions relate to those old images? Among the former, some will be found that the mind has already had before, others will at least present similarities to the images already acquired. This affinity of the new perceptions to the old is the reason for the attraction that the new perceptions possess, since their presence in the mind recalls similar images to the mind's consciousness. This ability to recognize old images in new perceptions is precisely what we call preconceived memory. When I have a new perception, I don't stop at merely making a new image out of it, rather, such a new recognition arises in the old ones within me and thereby makes them usable for the first time. The external stimulus allows me to return to my inner self, to internalize myself there, and then to compare the images evoked by the memory with my own perceptions. While thinking can generate the content of cognition without external assistance, the imagination must at least acknowledge this independence from the sensuous world by interpreting its material. Thus, the first synthesis was that of perception, as Kant also clearly saw, and we are on the way to achieving the second of the syntheses he considered, the synthesis of the imagination. Memory is, in fact, the path to it.

The association of representations is simply a linking of several related representations in the time of the mind, one after the other is conjured up into the realm of consciousness, and in the same way they displace one another in the frivolous play of the imagination. A higher synthesis is that of several given individual representations being linked into a new whole, so that they appear in the space of the mind as a composite representation. This activity is that of the composing imagination, which connects the first and second stages of representation, since it is a production of representations that are simultaneously mediated by an association, but without this being based on objective grounds. This activity does indeed lead to fixed productions, but the productive lies only in the combination, not in the elements from which the new whole has been formed. The imagination cannot create anything that sensibility has not supplied it, but it can combine the given into independent forms that are not encountered in sensibility. This includes, for example, the mythical representations of Pegasus, the Chimdre, etc. The imagination does not yet create things from within the mind, like thinking, rather, it only transforms the material by processing and combining its material. In particular, the composing imagination is the next step to the productive imagination or fantasy, which contains this even higher synthesis of wrapping the general ideas, which are products of the reproducing imagination, in such images as the composing imagination creates or the fantasy takes from elsewhere.

## Fantasy

### Symbolic Fantasy

The two sides of the sensory image and the representation are not yet related to each other as distinctly different opposites, rather the sensory images produced by the imagination are to be taken in themselves in a more general meaning, which has not yet appeared from the image with complete clarity. Such an image, which still carries the general meaning within itself and enclosed in its physical shell, is a symbol, and the activity that produces such symbols for the representations of the mind is the symbolic fantasy.

In order for something to be a symbol of a representation, it is necessary that the content of the general meaning be contained in the sensory image itself, because the existence of this content is still limited to that sensory shell. The general idea of ​​strength can thus be expressed by the lion or the bull, because these animals are strong in themselves. Here, the circle of natural products is not at all transcended, and the natural creature serves as a symbol of a spiritual content without the productivity of spirit. For not only a replicated bull, but also a real one, the Egyptian Apis, for example, was considered by the Egyptians as a symbol of the creative power of nature. Likewise, the Egyptians used the Nile, which because its floods are the reason for the fertility of their land, as a symbol of the creative activity of God. But especially the products of the imagination are those which the spirit uses as a means for the representations of artistic fantasy. The sphinx is a figure with a human face and is intended to express how, among the Egyptians, the spirit begins to wrestle itself free from its natural existence. Therefore, in order for nature to be an expression of something spiritual, violence must be done to it, its appearance must be distorted. But even if the sensory image contains a general meaning, it still has a great deal of other content within it, so it can also serve as a symbol for another idea. Hence the vagueness and indeterminacy of symbols ensues, which is in keeping with the obscurity of the ideas of those who produce them.

### Allegorical Fantasy

The second stage of fantasy, on the other hand, is where the meaning has clearly emerged and has reached the consciousness of the mind, but the mind feels the need to soar from the prose of general representation to the level of poetic comprehension from which the symbolic fantasy had not yet separated itself. This further activity of the artistic fantasy is the allegorical fantasy, where the visual image, rather than the fully developed meaning, is made the main focus, while the aesthetic form is relegated to a mere ornament. Fertility can, for example, be represented as a goddess with a horn of water that she is pouring out. Fortune stands on a globe to indicate its impermanence, the goddess of victory is adorned with a laurel wreath. The hymns of the fatherland can be depicted as an altar on which young and old lay their gifts, etc. 

Poets also use allegory, but without thereby bringing more warmth to their depictions. In view of the clear awareness of the prosaic nature of the representation, the allegory as a whole is cold and frosty. Homer uses it only very rarely, but the Roman and French poets, to the great detriment of their poetic stance, do so far more frequently. It follows from what has been said that every allegory is a personification. Abstract representations are made into individual figures that act and behave like persons. In allegory however, the general meaning and the personal figure through which it is expressed fall apart as two things that are completely independent of each other. Some attributes are attached to the figure, from which one must infer its meaning. This separation and externality is precisely the basis of the coldness that cannot be removed from allegory. But if we individualize an impersonal object merely by elevating the properties that the object has as non-individual ones to the qualities of a person, then we have simple personification, which is of a highly poetic nature.

### Artistic Fantasy

The imagination must therefore rise again from the counterfactual distortion it suffered in allegory to become the unity of image and representation in such a way that the representation is not, as in a symbol, submerged in the image. The representation should be expressed with complete clarity, as in allegory, and yet the image should no longer be something foreign and external to it. To this end, the image must not appear as a natural form in space, rather, the representation must contain the image it uses for its expression. In other words, the sensuous image is itself taken from the realm of representation, or conversely, the abstract representation is made into a concrete representation. This is precisely what artistic fantasy achieves. Meaning and expression both belong to the realm of the intelligible, except that expression is a transfigured sensibility that has become the property of the spirit.

#### Metaphor

The first, arguably most vivid, form of artistic fantasy is that in which meaning and form are so intimately intertwined that the former does not appear on its own and the latter is immediately substituted for the actual representation. This is what we call metaphor, in which the representation is directly carried over into its sensuous expression. However, metaphor is not limited merely to poetic expression, but has often been abbreviated and thus reduced to prose. This is because poetry is older than prose among all peoples, for one first captured one's ideas in sensory images before one could grasp them in a pure form of universality. The sensory expression, however, remained long after the general meaning had clearly emerged from it and had proven itself to be the only valid one within it. The more that a people grasps the abstract generality of thought, the more that metaphors in their spoken language are reduced to prose.

#### Simile

The artistic fantasy then also separates the sensory representation from the general meaning and places both parallel to each other. This is the simile, which relates to metaphor as allegory relates to symbol, but goes beyond the prosaic allegory by being the true instrument of poetic expression. The beauty of the simile lies in the particularity of its detail, so that specific aspects of the image correspond to the various aspects of the general idea. Such a simile becomes even more vivid when that which connects the two elements of the simile is omitted, and they are brought into such close grammar that the simile looks almost like a series of netaphors. Schiller's beautiful diction belongs to this category: "The youth sails boldly into the Teutonic with a thousand masts, the old man hurries to the harbor on his rescued boat." The hopes and plans of the youth are immediately transported to the many masts of the ships, and the experiences of the old man are expressed through the rescued boat. The simile in tragic pathos also has the value that the hero thereby rises above emotion and demonstrates the freedom of his spirit, avoiding giving the expression of his feelings an unnatural form. However, the poetic quality of simile lies in the fact that we make it an object of disinterested contemplation in itself, and out of concern with the purpose and goal of the discourse, view its individual parts as independent wholes and embellish them with a beauty that is appropriate to them.

#### Fable

The fable is simply an extended comparison that is based on a spiritual, often moral, meaning, that is to be explained through the sensory mode of representation. From this, it immediately follows that the means of representation must have such a meaning, or at least be reinterpreted as such. Since the sensory image must be taken from nature, partly from the inanimate, partly from the animate, it certainly follows that only an analogy of the moral can be discovered in it. But this analogy must be in the context for which the moral meaning is to be immediately clear. It is therefore essential to a good fable that, as with a symbol, there is an inner relationship between the content and the meaning. The fable, however, comes close to metaphor in that it presents its series of analogies as a self-contained poetic whole from which the moral lesson emerges, as it were, without needing to be specifically introduced. A good example of a fable is the oak and the reed, because while the oak is snapped by the storm, the reed is only bent, so the resistance to the inevitable destroys the strong, while the weak, by retreating, saves themselves. Equally praiseworthy is the fable of the farmer who is wounded by the frozen snake that he warmed in his garment, as if he was deceived by a false friend.

#### Parable

Parables also belong here, such as those of the New Testament, where the kingdom of heaven is often illustrated through sensory images. "The kingdom of heaven," as it is said, “A farmer went out to sow his seed. As he was scattering the seed, some fell along the path, and the birds came and ate it up. Some fell on rocky places, where it did not have much soil. It sprang up quickly, because the soil was shallow. But when the sun came up, the plants were scorched, and they withered because they had no root. Other seed fell among thorns, which grew up and choked the plants. Still other seed fell on good soil, where it produced a crop—a hundred, sixty or thirty times what was sown. Whoever has ears, let them hear." (Matthew 13). The difference between the parable and the fable is that the fable is more of a self-contained genre of poetry, where the moral meaning must emerge as if by itself and almost unintentionally, whereas in the parable, the moral instruction is always front and center as the main theme, and so it relates to the fable in much the same way that rhetoric relates to poetry. Because of the clear appearance of the moral purpose of the parable, one could also say that it relates to fable as allegory relates to symbol. Since in fable, the meaning is only meant to emerge from the image, it is limited to the realm of natural phenomena, and humans only appear in it accidentally. By contrast, the parable can just as easily take its figures from the realm of human life, since the meaning itself is perfectly clear.

#### Proverb

Finally, proverbs can also be cited. The proverb forms people's awareness of their moral and social principles. To ensure the most comprehensive access to the general meaning for everyone, such a fundamental principle is reduced to an individual norm that is universally applicable. The proverb, as the identity of the completely clear meaning with the sensory image, is a return to the symbolic. For example, "One hand washes the other" means: "One must help one another." This is not a metaphor that lies more in the expression of analogy, rather one can call such a proverb a shortened parable. It is possible to cite proverbs without this metaphorical imagery, but in general they have at least some semblance of poetic content. With the proverb, fantasy has now reduced the sensory expression to a means for its ideas. The sensible forms no longer count in themselves, they have no independence from the spirit and exist only to manifest its truth. The spirit generates its expressions from within itself and only in this way does it become the property and dependent means of the spirit. Such a representation of the idea is now the sign.

## Language

Symbol and allegory have this in common: representation and expression, spiritual content and sensuous form, stand opposite each other as universality and particularity, and therefore have not yet fully connected with each other. Expression is always a sensuously given image, even if the composing imagination arbitrarily assembles it: what it is meant to express is a universal representation. But if what is expressed and expression are to become closer to each other, the sensuous image must also be given the form of universality. In doing so, it loses its sensuous aspect, gradually losing the similarity of the sensuous content to the spiritual representation. But because the expression itself is elevated to universality, it is instead a product of the spirit and thus becomes homogeneous with it. This is the sign-creating imagination, or the faculty of language. The sign is the non-sensory, sensuous thing in which the arbitrarily active mind frees itself from the given images, disdains their similarity, and establishes a relationship that only arises from it, the meaning first placed in the sign. Language is now the system of signs through which we express the system of our ideas. As for spatial signs or graphemes, first, they have not yet completely lost their symbolic nature in pictorial language. The system of signs in time, i.e. of sounds, is secondly tonal language, to which one can also attribute something symbolic. The fact that these temporal signs are in turn expressed by spatial signs gives, thirdly, written language, a system of signs of signs that preserves the temporal sign in space and ultimately in the mind.

### Pictorial Language

Pure or proper graphemes already contain the signs created by the imagination, which are still symbolic in nature and have retained some part of the thing they are meant to represent. There can be no doubt that visual representation is the origin of language. The first mode of communication among people must have been that of illustration, an imitation of the thing to be represented that was somehow placed by the mind into the space of another, and to whom something was to be communicated. For how would it be possible to understand the sign of an idea if it had been entirely arbitrary from the beginning? The illustration, initially a true representation of the past, then became a symbol, which already designated the figures with arbitrariness. The completely arbitrary production of signs could only occur after the sphere of the intelligible had already developed to a certain extent. Originally, for example, one would draw the image of the object to be signified, wholly or partially, in the sand with a stick, in order to direct the other person's attention to this object. Naturally, only representations of tangible objects could be signified in this way. If one wanted to signify human deeds and the like, the sign for this remains entirely symbolic and becomes indeterminate precisely because it can also mean many other things. Purely intellectual content, such as abstract qualities, virtues, truth, etc., will ultimately be either completely unexpressed by pure imagery, or can only be expressed very imperfectly by resorting to a pictoral representation. Because of these necessary gaps, it also lacks the systematic nature and scope that are precisely the characteristic features of language. Such representations only become writing when they are able to reproduce speech in its entirety. 

The next moment of the logographic language is represented by the hieroglyphic language of the Egyptians, the only visual language that this people knew. The Egyptian hieroglyphs, which Humboldt aptly calls an idea script, in contrast to phonetic script, did not pass through the mediation of sound like the phonetic alphabets of other peoples. Instead, the individual figures are direct signs for the concepts, with the exception of proper names and inscriptions, where the hieroglyphs were later used phonetically and could be deciphered with certainty to that extent that each individual letter of the hieroglyph began with or consisted of that sound. One could surmise that hieroglyphs were remnants of an original pictorial language, just as written letters are remnants of logographs. Indeed, even among hieroglyphs, apart from a few grammatical signs, one finds almost exclusively forms that represent real things. But physical objects were not often indicated by themselves, but were indicated figuratively by other objects. Thus it often seems that there is no resemblance at all between the sign itself and what it signifies. In the case of intellectual objects, figurative indication was completely omitted, as when hatred was represented by the image of a fish, justice by an ostrich feather, obedience by a bee, and so on. Hieroglyphic language is very limited because it is very difficult to find universally understandable signs for intellectual content, as the Egyptians seem to have deliberately suppressed the symbolic in their hieroglyphic script. Yet, a few symbolic connections have survived, e.g. the image of the lion still denotes courage, and that of the heart combined with a censer, denotes jealousy. In most cases, however, the connection between the idea and the sign, bypasses what is readily apparent. The Egyptians realized that even the most improved art of drawing could never lead to writing, so they created a completely new system in hieroglyphic writing by separating the written system from the pictorial one. Nevertheless, this fixed spatial designation of ideas through writing, where one remained in the realm of images and found it more difficult to break free from them, was always very unhelpful, since the subtle intellectual nuances and relationships cannot possibly be represented in this way. 

From what has been said, the value of Leibniz's proposal for a characteristica universalis becomes clear: given the diversity of different spoken languages, to invent a complete hieroglyphic language for communication among all peoples, especially scholars, without affecting the spoken language of each people, would serve to directly express ideas. For a certain circle of ideas, which are exactly the same for all peoples, such signs already exist, such as numbers, planets, mathematical concepts, and so on. But to express the entire complex of our ideas in this way would be extremely difficult. The sheer magnitude of designations required for the language would render the meaning of any logographic script indeterminate. Furthermore, since intellectual content constantly evolves in time, how can further developments and modifications of meaning be expressed by a logographic script? Finally, the same word can have different meanings within the same language. If two concepts were expressed by the same logograph, the logographic script of a specific book would only make sense if it were translated back into the language from which it came. One would have to know among which people the language appeared and know the language of that people in order to understand it, which is precisely what this detour was intended to avoid. Doesn't every language also have turns of phrase, particles, and idioms that would become completely meaningless if one were to replace the logographic script word for word with symbols from another language? Only the most general idea, and very clumsily at that, could be gleaned.

### Tonal Language

The spatial sign that signifies an idea is an imperfect expression of it because it retains a duration in space independently of the idea's existence and thus acquires a meaning for itself. The other form of intuition, time, contains more appropriate expressions for the mind. The sound does not remain inscribed on a physical surface, like the hieroglyph, when the idea has disappeared. Instead, the sound is so closely connected with it that its duration does not extend beyond that of the idea. Just as in the succession of ideas, one gives way to another, so too does a sound disappear at the very moment it appears. Because of the transience and ideality of the sound, it becomes precisely the appropriate expression for the mind, which finds its own ideality reflected in it. For as the trembling of space, sound is the negativity of the sensible within sensibility itself. Sound is also higher than the spatial image because it is a sign does not depend on the sensuous nature of the human being, but independently has its own sensuous being. The throat and the air enclosed within it, which trembles, are the elements of sound. This therefore a sign attached to self-expression and is thus necessarily related to the self.

But if the tone is to be an adequate expression of the mind, it must not be left as nature gives it, with the purely natural modifications of strong or weak, high or low, etc. The essence of the articulated sound lies in its intention and capacity for specific meaning through the representation of something imagined. In its being, however, the articulation of natural sound depends on this power of the mind over the vocal organs, that they are modified by the will in order to give the sound a human character and a spiritual meaning. Animals have all the tools they need to speak, but they still cannot do so, primarily because they do not have the will to use their vocal organs for this purpose.

#### Vowels

The modifications during the vibration of the vocal tract enclosed in the throat produces the vowel sounds, the immediate tones independently produced by the vocal tract without requiring any other sounding component. The vowel is the immediate resonance of self-expression, the voice in its original freedom and genuine simplicity. Depending on whether the vocal tract is constricted or expanded as it exits the mouth, the tone also changes. Since this modification is produced by increasing or decreasing the opening of the mouth, the vowels are already articulated tones because they can only be produced with the help of this part of the mouth.

As far as the simple vowels are concerned, the mouth is opened the most when pronouncing the letter A, allowing the vibrations of the vocal tract in the throat to occur most freely and unhindered. A is the pure, unconditional, internally homogeneous sound, the expression of the undifferentiated, continuous unity of inner and outer, and is the natural harmony of both. When we pronounce U, we open our mouths the least and bring our lips closer together. With I, the mouth is half-open and the throat is contracted, so these vowels are thus on the verge of allowing the vocal organs to resonate, and in this way are related to the consonants. E is the middle ground between A and I, hence the combination of these two in many languages, such in French and in Greek, E is fully pronounced. Lastly, O stands between I and U in the middle. Just as A is the simple vowel, U and I are the vowels of opposition. U represents the negative sound that appears in the dull, the dark and the horrific, while I represents the positive sound that appears in the the vibrant, the intimate and the cheerful. O is the mediation between A and U, appearing as a striving from darkness to clarity, as an expression of admiration or pride. E takes place as the mediation between A and I, expressing a regular, but unpretentious mood of speech, the expression of brightness associated with insight and thought. 

The umlauts are further modifications of the original vowels. Only in actual diphthongs are two vowel sounds audible, but they also have this in common with the pure vowels, in that the tongue mechanically follows the movements of the mouth. The oldest languages, e.g. the Semitic languages, ​​have no mixed sounds at all, and Latin and Greek have far fewer than the more modern languages, and in which the most complicated sounds occur, such as ai, ei, au, eu, oi sounds that appear in German, in the Scandinavian languages, in English and French, etc. The Greeks only have the umlaut å (as) because ou is a very simple sound. Both belong to the class of diphthongs, of which the Greek grammarians say that both vowels have the same value. 

#### Consonants

When the various organs of the mouth resonate with the vibrations in the vocal tract, we have the consonant, the mediated sounds of the voice against which it breaks but also acquires support and firmness. The main division of consonants in Greek grammar corresponds to the nature of this fact in that real differences of vibration correspond to different letters. The coronal letters are thus d, τ, θ, ν, λ, ρ, σ, ζ, the labial letters β, π, φ, ψ, and the throat and palatal letters are y, x, and μ. The simplest of all guttural sounds, however, is the Hebrew aleph (א), as the simple opening of the throat. Because the consonant forms the contrast to the immediacy and simplicity of the vowel, it is also divided within itself and is produced by two mutually exciting linguistic organs. One of these organs is fixed, and the other is mobile. In the labial letters, the lower lip moves towards the upper lip, and in the palatal letters, the larynx moves towards the palate. The relationship and totality of these three classes are constructed in such a way that the consonants of the labial organ denote loudness, as the voice forces its way out of closed lips, while the consonants of the laryngeal organ denote silence, as the voice, which had free passage, is now forced back into itself. The consonants of the lingual organ, finally, are meant to represent the process and transition from one to the other and thus immediately overcome the momentary inhibition. This is because the tongue, lying in the middle of the organs, is the most mobile and lively of all, and is so central to language that most people denote tongue and language by the same word. Regarding intellectual expression, that the lip consonants have the meaning of going outside oneself or of striving upwards, the throat consonants represent the meaning of going inwards, or of returning to oneself. The essence of the coronal letters can be recognized in this inhibition and the associated overcoming of it. However, different people can express different things with the same vowels or consonants because the language describes and evaluates things in very different ways.

The secondary differences between the classes of consonants arise from the fact that partly the consonants are simple or compound, and partly the consonants are articulated softly or hard. In other words, the natural modifications of the tone are added to the articulated tones. In the letter S, aspiration, which generally applies preferentially to the resonant letters, becomes an independent consonant. The aspirated tongue letters approximate the sibilant S in some languages, such as English and Greek. This also explains how the Greek spiritus often changed to S in both Romance languages. The absolute aspiration, neither consonant nor vowel, is the letter H, a peculiarity of the Germanic languages, since it does not appear to have been pronounced in Latin and Greek, and indeed, it does not form a position. Double consonants are actually just intensified aspirates, formed by combining the S with consonants of all organs. Grooving and labial consonants can also combine with each other, which actually consists of K and W, and therefore must always be accompanied by a u in orthography, even if this u, especially in French, is not always pronounced.

#### Syllables

The articulated sound is completed with the combination of the vowel and the consonant. This combination is the syllable, each of which forms only one sound uttered in a specific way. The purer and simpler the vowels sound in it, and the fewer consonants are involved, the more melodious the language.

In the Eastern languages, every consonant, as the basis and stem of the syllable, was accompanied by a vowel. Therefore, among the Eastern languages, vowels were not yet letters at all, but were modifications of the consonants. The Hebrews, following Spinoza, called vowels the souls of the letters, and consonants the bodies. Indeed, even if the consonants exclusively contained the meaning of the words, the living relationship between the consonants is based in the vowels. Since the consonant was made the root and principal element, the Hebrews originally did not distinguish vowels at all. Even in Sanskrit, every consonant immediately followed by another was naturally pronounced with an A sound. In Western languages, the vowel frees itself from its dependence on the consonant, often becoming the root of the word itself, and the consonant even serves the vowel to modify its expression. A vowel or diphthong can form a syllable on its own without a consonant, but it can also act as the unity that organizes a multiplicity consonants. In Greek, at the beginning of words remained the need for the vowel to rely on a more or less faint consonantal breath, which was most developed in the Hebrews, but from then on it gradually lost more and more of its significance until it was finally reduced to graphical distinction. U and I are therefore characteristic vowels in both Western languages of Greek and Latin. Even in Latin, the consonantal meaning becomes more frequent. In the Germanic languages, vowels and consonants are of equal value, and the accumulation of consonants does not influence the expression vowels, until finally, in the Slavic languages, the sound of the expressed vowels are obscured by a disproportionate accumulation of consonants.

We can call a simple syllable one that consists of only a vowel. From this first types of syllable, the open syllable, in which a consonant precedes a vowel, follows as the second type. Each consonant can do this individually, but only a consonant of the unilateral classes can be reinforced by a consonant of totality, i.e. a receptive consonant. More than three consonants, however, do not agree at the beginning of a vowel as a general rule. The exception to this rule is provided by the Slavic languages (where open syllables are predominant), where the vocal element is only implicit and contributes in the background. In the closed syllable, finally, the free, infinitely extended intonation of the vowel is brought to a firm hold, whether it begins with a vowel, like the simple syllable, or with a consonant, like the open syllable.

In addition to the articulated tone, a natural modification of the tone occurs in relation to the syllables as an accompanying feature. For one part, the different syllables are either long or short, and for the other part, the syllables of one and the same word differ from each other in that it can either raise or lower the voice. These two modes of natural intonation accompanying the articulated tone are quantity and accent, which is their qualitative relationship, the degree of which is determined by the voice itself. In ancient languages, the quality and quantity of syllables are independent of each other, whereas in modern languages, ​​the accent always seeks the long syllable and stem of the word. From this it follows that natural intonation can no longer assert itself on its own, but is bound to and serves the intellectual meaning. But even in Ancient Greek, this quantity was not entirely without influence on accent, but Modern Greek eventually conformed to the spirit of modern languages ​​in this respect as well. Among the modern languages, French has achieved the highest level of development in this respect since it has completely suppressed word accent, and the signs of accentuation serve to denote articulated differences between vowels. The accent of meaning, which all languages ​​possess, fully expresses the complete dependence of natural tone on meaning.

### Written Language

While sound is certainly a more appropriate expression of language than imagery, it nevertheless has the deficiency of transience and ephemerality, like the idea itself, whereas the image places the idea into the greater solidity and permanence of inert matter. The hieroglyphic language had thus immortalized its ideas in the stones of the obelisks. However, the ideas remained incomprehensible because there was no connection whatsoever between the images and the ideas they signified, not even symbolically. The mediating element of sound between the image and the idea was precisely what was missing. The Egyptians likely had a tonal language, but it would be of no help in deciphering hieroglyphs because they signified ideas and not sounds. If the hieroglyphs were symbols for sounds, then one could easily read the concepts from them.

#### Alphabet

Alphabetic writing has the tremendous advantage over hieroglyphic writing that a much smaller number of signs suffices to express the totality of sounds of a language through its most varied combinations. In hieroglyphic writing, each sign has a unique meaning and is absorbed into this individual meaning, which is moreover quite arbitrarily linked to the sign (which further contributes to making it difficult to understand). By contrast, each letter of an alphabet has no meaning at all. A letter is a completely general sign that always expresses only one and the same sound, regardless of whatever combination of sounds it is incorporated into. This very small number of elements is all that is necessary to know in order to be able comprehend a combination of sounds from a combination of letters or vice versa. Because the number of these signs is so small and their forms are nearly identical among all peoples, their understanding has been preserved in time and their decipherment is possible without great difficulty. The effect of written language is that the existence of concepts are no longer bound to the momentary production of the mind, but that they can be easily deciphered even without the presence of human speech.

#### Lexical Memory

Writing best preserves ideas through the combination of sound and imagery, yet it is still transient as a physical medium in space, whereas lexical memory preserves ideas and sounds in the realm of the imperishable by inscribing them on the tablet of the mind. Hence, language has been accused of having damaged memory with the invention of writing as being as a poor substitute for it. However, lexical memory does not inscribe words on the tablet of the mind as static signs, but only as active, constantly renewing ones, which is characteristic of the mind itself. The word is not petrified as a dead name, but is the living thing itself, which is now no longer distinct from meaning.

Lexical memory is, firstly, factual and verbal memory. The two are identical because the word is meaning, and meaning is the thing. The word is the expression of the thing adequate to meaning, because the word, as the universal, signifies only the universal thing in the imagination. Lexical memory, however, is a synthesis of word and meaning, namely, recalling one when the other is given. If the word is given, whether it is read or heard, and its meaning is found, then this is to understand a language. But if the meaning is given and the word is recalled from memory, then that is called being able to write and speak a language. The latter is more difficult than the former, especially with a foreign language, because the words appear to be foreign, arbitrary signs in contrast to the sign I already associate with the meaning. In one's mother tongue, the difference is less pronounced because the words have become familiar to the mind from childhood. But it is still more difficult to speak and especially to write in one's native language than to understand it. A child, at the very least, understands what words mean before it can speak. However, the advantage is immense when the parents or other family members speak different languages. 

If word and meaning are intimately synthesized through the memory of things and names, then secondly it is important to synthesize the whole series of things and names together. We call this reproductive memory, which differs from reproductive imagination in that the latter simply connects ideas with one another and thus, as an association of ideas, is limited to the inner subjectivity of the mind. In reproductive memory, however, the mental image is not reproduced as a static sign, but only as active, constantly renewing ones, which performs this function as it is laid before it. However, in mnemonics, which Kant in his Anthropology calls ingenious memorization, reproductive memory makes even deeper connections than associationa of ideas. Through memorization, the meanings of words, names, and sentences return of their own accord when one looks at the things to which they refer. When I see the thing, I recall a certain word, or sentence, or phrase because I had associated it with the thing in my mind. But this is not really ingenious if memorization is ingenious, rather that it is a procedural and streamlined activity. Therefore, Kant rightly prefers the judicious form of memory, where the order of propositions is determined by their logical structure. 

#### Procedural Memory

If it was more important to overcome the externality of the word by making it internal to the mind, then the reverse path is that the mind itself creates the sign as an external thing. This is, thirdly, procedural memory, where the connection between the word and its meaning has become an identity. The mind creates the series of signs that proceed spontaneously from it. The mind has therefore become objective to itself, even unconsciously, through the word. This is rote memorization, and it has been found that it proceeds more easily when one forgets the meaning, and the words themselves are completely connected in the mind. But if the mind has become an object to itself, then conversely, the object has also become the mind itself. But that the object has become the subject and the subject has become the object, then we have thought, which is no less inseparable from the word than it is to the thing itself.
